    The block structure

    Nested blocks, congruent children, and the decimation identity they make exact.

    part 1 of 3 · 6 articles · part-block-structure

    Six articles establish the object everything else rests on: a group whose balls are cosets of nested finite-index subgroups, with the same number of children at every scale. The child decomposition says that every pair of vertices in different children of a block sits at the top level of that block, which is what makes the energy split into the children plus one interaction between their sums. The decimation identity is that statement written as an identity for partition functions, and it is the whole reason the renormalisation group is exact here. The fourth article passes from the blocks to the lattice, where the two-point function and the phase live; the fifth isolates what the argument actually uses and carries it to the p-adic integers; and the sixth describes the phase diagram from monotonicity alone, ending where the recursion has to take over.

    5 tagged assertions

    The hierarchical lattice

    An ultrametric group whose blocks fall into L^d congruent children.

    The hierarchical lattice is the direct sum of countably many copies of (Z/LZ)^d, with an ultrametric whose balls have exactly L^(dn) points. Each ball splits into L^d congruent children, every pair of vertices in different children of a ball sits at the top distance of that ball, and power kernels are summable exactly when their exponent exceeds the dimension. These three facts are what make block decimation close on one-variable laws.

    The isometries of the hierarchical lattice

    Why any two pairs at the same level are the same pair, and why block sums of a block are exchangeable.

    Translations and coordinate-wise bijections are isometries of the hierarchical lattice, and together they act transitively on ordered pairs at each level. Three statements that other articles use as "by symmetry" follow: a radial kernel gives a radial two-point function, the block sums of a block are exchangeable, and conditional expectations given the block sums are constant on the vertices of a child. A bijection that preserves the level of every vertex need not be an isometry, so the criterion has content.

    Hierarchical spin systems and decimation

    The block spin of a hierarchical model is distributed as a functional of independent copies of the block spin one scale down.

    A spin system on the hierarchical lattice with couplings constant on levels has a Hamiltonian that splits into the Hamiltonians of its children plus one interaction between their sums. Consequently the partition function and the law of a block spin are recovered from the law one scale down by an explicit operation, the free energy has an exact recursion, and the whole renormalisation group is a recursion on one-variable laws.

    The infinite-volume limit

    Finite-volume measures, the boundary field of a block, and which limits are Gibbs measures.

    The decimation identity is finite-volume, but the two-point function, the phase and the exponents are infinite-volume objects. This article takes the limit: configurations form a compact space, the boundary field of a block decays at rate L^{-alpha n} while its total energy grows like L^{(d-alpha)n}, the conditional law of a finite region is Lipschitz in that field, and consequently every limit point of the finite-volume measures is a Gibbs measure in the sense of Dobrushin, Lanford and Ruelle. Every such limit point of the free-boundary measures is invariant under translations and coordinate-wise bijections, so its two-point function depends on the level alone.

    Ultrametric block systems and the p-adic models

    What the recursion actually needs, and how little of it is specific to the hierarchical lattice.

    The decimation recursion uses only a nested system of blocks with a constant number of children per block. Any ultrametric group with such a system carries the same model, the same map and the same exponents: the p-adic integers with their balls, the hierarchical lattice with its subgroups, and their variants. The child decomposition and the decimation identity are proved here in that generality, and the one place where the compact and discrete cases differ is isolated.

    The magnetised phase, and what monotonicity gives

    Two extremal states, the order parameter, and why the FKG inequality cannot locate the threshold.

    For ferromagnetic couplings the FKG inequality makes the Gibbs measures of the hierarchical Ising model a stochastically ordered family with a largest and a smallest element: the monotone limits of the plus and minus boundary conditions. The order parameter M of the plus state is positive exactly when the Gibbs measure is not unique, and it equals the block average of that state at every finite volume; a nondegenerate limit of block averages is impossible when M vanishes, so the trichotomy's block spin and the order parameter carry the same information. Correlations under the plus state dominate M squared, so a magnetised phase has infinite susceptibility, and M is at most tanh of beta over the Gaussian critical intensity, which is the only bound on its size that monotonicity gives. The arguments are insensitive to the intensity, so the location of the threshold is not a monotonicity statement: it is the branch of the recursion's trichotomy, which is where Bleher and Sinai's analysis enters.
