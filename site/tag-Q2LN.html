    Block-spin transformations can destroy the Gibbs property

    theorem · Q2LN · in Hierarchical and Euclidean renormalisation

    There are translation-invariant Gibbs measures for finite-range lattice interactions whose image under a block-spin transformation is not a Gibbs measure for any interaction, and there are models for which the image is Gibbsian but with an interaction that is not absolutely summable. Consequently position-space decimation does not define a map on a space of interactions in general [@van-enter-fernandez-sokal-1993].

    The reason is a long-range dependence in the coarse variables that is invisible in the fine ones: conditioning on the outside of a region in the coarse lattice constrains the fine configuration in a way that is not mediated by finitely many coarse variables, so the conditional probabilities of the coarse measure fail the Dobrushin-Lanford-Ruelle form. The hierarchical model is immune because its coarse variables are the block sums and a block interacts with the rest of the lattice through its sum alone, so conditioning on the outside of a block constrains the block only through one number.

    ## Consequences and applications {#section-consequences}

    The reference model for the local analysis. The renormalisation map of a hierarchical model is a nonlinear map on a space of measures with exponential moments, and the analysis of its fixed points, its unstable manifold and the $\varepsilon$-expansion can be carried out completely, as in the Bleher–Sinai article. This is the source of the structural facts that the Euclidean theory reproduces: two relevant directions, one of which is the thermal one; the irrelevance of the quartic coupling below the upper critical exponent; the analytic form of the corrections to scaling; and complex eigenvalues producing oscillatory corrections [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983].

    The approximation scheme. Gawedzki and Kupiainen organise the rigorous renormalisation group for a lattice field theory as a convergent expansion whose terms are indexed by the hierarchical approximation of the fluctuation covariance and by the corrections to it [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1980]. The hierarchical model is then not an analogy but the leading term of an expansion, and the control of the remainder is the technical content of the method.

    A whole orbit, not only its ends. The renormalisation-group control of a Euclidean model is a statement about its trajectory between fixed points, and such a trajectory has been constructed for a three-dimensional model with modified propagator by perturbing the one-dimensional iteration that the hierarchical model supplies [@abdesselam-2007]. That construction is described in Abdesselam's trajectory, and it is the precise sense in which the hierarchical recursion is the leading term of the Euclidean analysis rather than an analogy for it.

    Power counting and the upper critical exponent. The exponent at which the two-point exponent stops being $2-\alpha$, and the corresponding statements for $\varphi^4$, are read off from the hierarchical map and transferred. For $\varphi^4_3$ the trajectory of the renormalisation group is constructed in the same way, with the hierarchical analysis of the quartic coupling as the model computation [@brydges-mitter-scoppola-2003] [@bauerschmidt-brydges-slade-2019].

    Bounds transported the other way. The comparison also runs from the hierarchy to the lattice: sharp upper bounds on the critical two-point function for long-range percolation on $\mathbb Z^d$ are obtained by a hierarchical argument, with the hierarchy serving as the comparison object rather than as a model of interest [@hutchcroft-2022-bounds]. This is the percolation counterpart of the perturbation scheme above, and it is the clearest case in which a statement about the hierarchical lattice yields one about $\mathbb Z^d$ without an expansion.

    What does not transfer. The exact exponent values of the hierarchical model are not the Euclidean ones, except in the classical region where both are given by the Gaussian fixed point. For a short-range model in $d$ dimensions with $2<d<4$ the exponents are not the classical ones and are not the hierarchical ones either: the hierarchical model's $\nu$ depends on $\alpha$, while the Euclidean model's depends on $d$. The two agree in the mean-field region and in the structure of the corrections, not in the numbers.
