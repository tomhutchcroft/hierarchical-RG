    The fixed points, and what is known about each

    An inventory of the fixed points of the hierarchical recursion, what they share, and what distinguishes them.

    theorem · part 2 · 17 sections · 5 tagged assertions · hierarchical-fixed-points

    The renormalisation group of a hierarchical model is one operator on laws on the line, and the theory of the model is the study of its orbits. This article is the inventory of the fixed points that govern the critical behaviour of the models here: what they are, which of them are proved to exist and by which argument, what they have in common, and what distinguishes them. It is not a classification of the fixed points of the map, and the last section says why that is a different and harder question. It adds no analysis of its own beyond the assembling; every statement is proved or attributed in the article it links to, and the tables say which.

    The organising fact is a division of labour that is easy to miss when the exponents are read off one fixed point at a time. The autonomy of the recursion fixes one of the two numbers at every fixed point: the growth exponent is $\theta_0$ and the susceptibility exponent is $\alpha$, whatever the fixed point. The other number, the multiplier of the linearisation, is what the fixed point determines, and it is the only one that changes as the critical theory changes. So in this model the two-point exponent is universal across regimes and the correlation-length exponent is not — the opposite of the short-range intuition, where the borderline case $\alpha=2$ makes the two coincide. The dictionary says why in general terms; this article says it fixed point by fixed point.

    What counts as a fixed point#

    A fixed point is a law $\nu^*\in\mathcal E$ with $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}\nu^*=\nu^*$ for some $\theta$ and $b$, where $\mathcal E$ is the class of the fixed-point article: symmetric, with $0<\int s^2d\nu^*<\infty$ and an exponential moment. Four kinds occur in this model, and nothing else is known to:

    1. the trivial fixed point $\delta_0$, the law of the block spin in the disordered phase, which is a fixed point only in the degenerate sense that its variance vanishes — it is the limit of every orbit with $b<b_c$, and it lies on the boundary of $\mathcal E$ rather than in it;
    2. the Gaussian fixed points $N(0,v_b)$, one for each $b>0$, of which the critical member is $N(0,1)$ at $b=b_1^{(0)}$;
    3. the nontrivial fixed point $\nu^*$ of the critical orbit, in $\mathcal E$ and not Gaussian when $\alpha>d/2$;
    4. the degenerate fixed points of the supercritical phase, $\delta_{+M}$ and $\delta_{-M}$, which are fixed points of the unrescaled map and lie outside $\mathcal E$ because their variance is of the order of the block rather than of the block's square root.

    "Fixed point" below means the second or third kind unless another is named, and the distinction between the four is the point of the inventory: only two of them can govern a critical theory.

    Two remarks on the columns. The Gaussian row is one family rather than one point: for every $b$ the rescaled map has exactly one Gaussian fixed point, of variance $v_b=\frac{\lambda-m}{\lambda b(m-1)}$, and it is the critical one only at $b_1^{(0)}=\frac{\lambda-m}{\lambda(m-1)}$, where the variance is one and the multiplier is $L^{\alpha}$. And the nontrivial row's spectrum is part of its existence statement: the linearisation is compact on the signed measures of zero mass, its spectrum is a simple eigenvalue $\lambda_1>1$ with the rest in a disk of radius $\rho<1$, and complex pairs occur among the rest.

    One model at a time: what varies with the exponent#

    Two things about the inventory are easy to misread, and both concern what the exponent $\alpha$ does to it.

    The Gaussian row is a family, and that is not a multiplicity of critical theories. For every $b>0$ the rescaled map has a Gaussian fixed point, because the recursion closes on the variance and the resulting fractional linear map always has one: $v_b=\frac{\lambda-m}{\lambda b(m-1)}$. So there are infinitely many Gaussian fixed points at every $\alpha$, but they are not alternatives to choose between. The intensity is part of the model, so exactly one $b$ is the critical one, $b_1^{(0)}$, and only the member with $v=1$ is the critical theory; the rest are fixed points of the flow at other intensities, each with its own variance and its own scale, and they describe the same model away from criticality rather than different critical behaviour.

    The exponent indexes the model, not the flow. Changing $\alpha$ changes the kernel, hence the space and the map, so strictly there is one inventory per $\alpha$; what one has across $\alpha$ is a one-parameter family of critical theories rather than many fixed points of one map. Three regimes, in order of $\alpha$:

    • for $\alpha<d/2$ the critical fixed point is the Gaussian one, and the exponents are the classical values [@bleher-sinai-1975];
    • at $\alpha=d/2$ the two fixed points coalesce and the classical values hold with logarithmic corrections, as above;
    • for $\alpha>d/2$ the nontrivial fixed point governs, with $\lambda_1\neq L^{\alpha}$ and $\nu\neq1/\alpha$.

    So small $\alpha$ is the simplest end of the family, not the richest: the critical theory is Gaussian, and what grows as $\alpha\downarrow0$ is not the number of fixed points but the number of scales over which the flow is nearly marginal. The couplings decay at rate $L^{-\alpha k}$, so the smaller $\alpha$ the more levels contribute at comparable strength — the total mass at a vertex is $\frac{J(1-L^{-d})}{L^{\alpha}-1}$, which diverges as $\alpha\downarrow0$ — while the linearisation degenerates: the multiplier is $L^{\alpha}\to1$, so $\nu=1/\alpha\to\infty$, and $\kappa=\alpha\to0$, $\eta=2-\alpha\to2$. In the limit the thermal direction is marginal and the exponent dictionary, whose whole content is $\nu=\log L/\log\lambda_1$, stops being informative — which is consistent with the model ceasing to be a Gibbs system at $\alpha=0$, where the kernel $\lVert x\rVert^{-d}$ is not summable.

    Where the number of fixed points can genuinely proliferate is a different place, and it is not a range of $\alpha$ but a point: at $\alpha=d$ the level sequence $l$ in $J_k=l_kL^{-2dk}$ is free, the map is no longer autonomous along the scales, and the fixed-point equation becomes an equation on sequences. That is the case the article on the two thresholds treats, and the reason its criterion is stated in terms of $\sum_kl_k$ rather than in terms of an exponent.

    What is the same at every fixed point#

    Let $\nu^*\in\mathcal E$ be a fixed point of $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}$ with $\theta>0$ and $b>0$. Then $\theta=\theta_0=\tfrac12(d+\alpha)$, so $\kappa=2\theta_0-d=\alpha$ and $\eta=2-\alpha$ at every fixed point. Consequently the critical orbit has $\mathbb E[S_n^2]\asymp L^{(d+\alpha)n}$ whichever fixed point governs it, and the two-point exponent does not distinguish the Gaussian fixed point from the nontrivial one.

    Proof

    By the autonomous form, $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}$ is independent of the scale $n$ only at $\theta=\theta_0$: at any other exponent the map obtained by iterating $n$ scales carries the factor $L^{(\theta_0-\theta)n}$ in its argument, so it is a different map at each $n$. A fixed point of the rescaled map is by definition unchanged by one step, and the one step is the step of the model only at $\theta_0$; hence a fixed point with $0<\int s^2<\infty$ occurs only at $\theta_0$. The consequences for $\kappa$ and $\eta$ are the definitions of the exponents.

    The statement is the map article's autonomy read at a fixed point rather than along an orbit, and the reason to read it that way is the shape it gives the inventory: the growth direction is the same at every fixed point, so a fixed point can tell one only what the multiplier in that direction is — $L^{\alpha}$ at the Gaussian point and $\lambda_1$ at the nontrivial one. Equivalently, $\kappa$, $\eta$ and the order of the two-point function are mean-field in the sense of the dictionary everywhere, while $\nu$ and everything the dictionary derives from it is not.

    At the critical fixed point the growth statement can be sharpened to a two-sided bound, which is what the amplitude theorem does: $0<\inf_nR_n\leq\sup_nR_n<\infty$ for $R_n=\mathbb E[S_n^2]/L^{(d+\alpha)n}$, so the rate is exactly $d+\alpha$ and the bound holds in both directions.

    What changes from one fixed point to another#

    Everything the dictionary derives from $\nu$, and the corrections. Three things are worth naming separately.

    The correlation-length exponent. $\nu=\log L/\log\lambda_1$. At the Gaussian fixed point $\lambda_1=L^{\alpha}$ and $\nu=1/\alpha$; at the nontrivial one $\lambda_1$ is a different number, known through the expansion in the distance from the marginal exponent and not in closed form [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983]. This is the exponent the fixed point determines, and above $\alpha=d/2$ it is the one that cannot be computed exactly.

    The subleading spectrum, hence the corrections. A perturbation in a direction of eigenvalue $\lambda$ is multiplied by $\lambda$ per scale, so it appears as a relative correction of order $\xi^{-\omega}$ with $\omega=-\log\lvert\lambda\rvert/\log L$; a complex pair turns the power into a power times a cosine of the logarithm of the length. The corrections article derives this from the spectral expansion, and the oscillatory corrections of the hierarchical model are a case of it.

    The relevant directions. The linearisation at the Gaussian fixed point is triangular in the even monomials with eigenvalues $\Lambda_{kk}=L^{d(1-k)+k\alpha}$, so the quadratic direction has $L^{\alpha}$ and the quartic $L^{2\alpha-d}$: the first is relevant for every $\alpha$, the second only above $\alpha=d/2$. The spectrum article proves this and derives the threshold from it. At the nontrivial fixed point the count of relevant directions is the same, which is why the comparison article can say that the structure of the linearisation transfers to the Euclidean theory even when the numbers do not.

    Where two fixed points meet#

    The inventory is not static: two of its rows coalesce at a value of the exponent, and the way they meet is what makes that value special.

    $\alpha=d/2$. As $\alpha\downarrow d/2$ the nontrivial fixed point approaches the Gaussian one and $\lambda_1\to L^{d/2}$; at the threshold the two coincide, the classical values hold with logarithmic corrections, and the corrections are logarithmic because the second eigenvalue tends to one [@bleher-sinai-1975] [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983]. The threshold is therefore a bifurcation point of the inventory rather than a boundary between two unrelated regimes, which is why the $\varepsilon$-expansion is organised in $\alpha-d/2$.

    $\alpha=d$. Here the model stops being a Gibbs system for a level sequence that does not decay, as the article on the two thresholds sets out, so the inventory itself is at stake rather than a row of it: there is no fixed point to have, because there is no model. The two marginal values are different in kind, and the table above is a table of fixed points of a model that exists.

    Above criticality: only degenerate fixed points#

    Let $b>b_c$. Then $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}$ has no fixed point in $\mathcal E$, and the unrescaled orbit of the Ising initial law concentrates: the law of $S_n/m^n$ converges to the symmetric mixture $\tfrac12(\delta_{-M}+\delta_M)$, where $M=M(\beta)>0$ is the spontaneous magnetisation.

    Proof

    The concentration is part (ii) of the trichotomy: the orbit has unbounded variance after rescaling, and in the limit the block spin per site is $\pm M$, the two signs being the two phases of the magnetised-phase article. For a fixed point in $\mathcal E$ to exist at $b>b_c$ it would have to be fixed by $\mathcal R_{\theta_0,b}$ with a finite nonzero variance, and then the orbit of the Ising law would have to converge to it up to the relevant directions; but the orbit's variance per site grows like $m^{2n}$, so the rescaled variance diverges, and no limit in $\mathcal E$ exists. The only invariant laws of the unrescaled map consistent with concentration on $\pm M$ are $\delta_{\pm M}$ and their mixture, each of variance of order $m^{2n}$, which is outside $\mathcal E$.

    So the four kinds of the definition are exhaustive in the following sense: the rescaled map has fixed points in $\mathcal E$ only at $b\leq b_c$, and at $b=b_c$ the one it has is the nontrivial fixed point. That is the technical content of the phrase "the phase transition is the critical orbit": every other orbit runs to a fixed point that is either outside the class or on its boundary.

    Multicritical points#

    A tricritical point is where a critical point becomes a critical line in a larger parameter space, so that two parameters must be tuned rather than one; a tetracritical point is the next order of the same phenomenon. The question is whether the inventory above needs more rows for them, and at the Gaussian level the answer is no: they are the same fixed point, seen with a different coupling marginal. What distinguishes the orders is a count of eigenvalues, and the count is arithmetic from the spectrum.

    At the Gaussian fixed point the direction $e_k$ has eigenvalue $\Lambda_{kk}=L^{d(1-k)+k\alpha}$, so it is relevant exactly when $k(d-\alpha)<d$ and marginal exactly when $k(d-\alpha)=d$. Hence the number of relevant even directions is $\#\{k\geq1: k(d-\alpha)<d\}$, and the $k$-th one becomes marginal at

    $$\alpha_k=d\Bigl(1-\frac1k\Bigr),\qquad k=2,3,4,\ldots$$

    In particular: at $k=2$, $\alpha=d/2$, the quartic is marginal, which is the ordinary critical point at its upper critical dimension; at $k=3$, $\alpha=2d/3$, the sextic is marginal, which is the tricritical point at its own; at $k=4$, $\alpha=3d/4$, the octic is marginal, which is the tetracritical point; and so on. Setting $\alpha=2$, the short-range power law, the relation $\alpha_k=d(1-1/k)$ inverts to $d=2k/(k-1)$, giving $4,3,8/3,5/2,\ldots$ — the classical upper critical dimensions of the $\varphi^4$, $\varphi^6$, $\varphi^8$ and $\varphi^{10}$ theories.

    Proof

    $\Lambda_{kk}>1$ iff $d(1-k)+k\alpha>0$ iff $k\alpha>d(k-1)$ iff $k(d-\alpha)<d$, using $k>0$ and $\alpha<d$; equality is the marginal case. Solving $k(d-\alpha)=d$ for $\alpha$ gives $\alpha=d(1-1/k)$. The count is the number of positive integers below $d/(d-\alpha)$, and since $d-\alpha>0$ it is finite; it increases by one each time $\alpha$ passes one of the values $\alpha_k$.

    Three consequences are worth separating. First, the thresholds accumulate at $\alpha=d$: the count $\#\{k:k<d/(d-\alpha)\}$ diverges as $\alpha\uparrow d$, so near the summability threshold the Gaussian fixed point has arbitrarily many relevant directions and the model is multicritical of arbitrarily high order. It is large $\alpha$, not small, that makes the structure rich — the opposite of the guess that the slowly decaying kernels of small $\alpha$ carry more structure, which the previous section settles. Second, the Gaussian multicritical points are one fixed point: the tricritical Gaussian point at $\alpha=2d/3$ is the law $N(0,1)$ at the critical intensity, not a second law, and what changes as $\alpha$ passes $\alpha_k$ is which coupling is the leading interaction and hence which theory the $\varepsilon$-expansion expands in. Third, reaching a multicritical point needs more tunings: at $\alpha<d/2$ the Gaussian fixed point has a single relevant direction, so tuning the intensity suffices for criticality, and the tricritical point is not on the one-parameter orbit of any single prior; it is reached by tuning the quartic coefficient of the prior as well, which is why a tricritical point is a property of a family of models rather than of one.

    How many fixed points are there?#

    The tables above list the fixed points that govern the critical behaviour of the models of this wiki: the orbit of the Ising prior at the critical intensity, its Gaussian relatives, and the degenerate limits. They are not a classification of the fixed points of the map, and the difference matters.

    The map acts on all of $\mathcal E$. A fixed point is a property of $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}$, not of the model, and $\mathcal E$ is an infinite-dimensional space of laws. Nothing in the argument that produces $\nu^*$ at the critical intensity says that its intensity is the only one at which $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}$ has a non-Gaussian fixed point, and there is no reason to expect the list to close at two kinds. What the theorems of this wiki give is the fixed point on the physical orbit, together with its attractivity, which is what a phase transition needs; a classification of the map's fixed points would be a different and much harder statement, and the wiki does not attempt it.

    The multicritical fixed points are the natural candidates for more rows. For each $k$ the Gaussian fixed point is marginal in the $e_k$ direction at $\alpha=\alpha_k$, and the same bifurcation that at $\alpha=d/2$ replaces the Gaussian fixed point by the nontrivial one should, at each $\alpha_k$, produce a nontrivial $(2k)$-critical fixed point with the quartic and its predecessors tuned to zero. That is the natural conjecture and it is not proved here: the theorem of tag B7WD is one fixed point at one intensity for one class of priors, and it does not come with a family indexed by $k$. A proof would be the analogue of that theorem with the first $k-2$ even couplings set to zero, and it is the clearest gap in the inventory; the section on the missing multicritical points is about it, including how one would compute one.

    Fixed points off every orbit. Beyond the multicritical family there is no reason to think the list is complete, and the wiki has no tool for the question: the methods in the table above are methods for a chosen orbit, not for the whole space of laws. The honest summary is that the inventory is complete for critical behaviour and open for the map.

    The missing multicritical fixed points#

    The counting section fixes where each even direction becomes marginal; this one is about the fixed points one expects above those values, and about how one would actually compute one. The short answer to the first half is that nothing in this wiki proves any of them exists, and the short answer to the second is that the problem is well posed and numerically attackable but not attacked successfully here. Both answers are worth their space, because the gap is the clearest one in the inventory.

    What is known, and what is conjectured#

    What is known is the Gaussian analysis: at $\alpha=\alpha_k$ the $k$-th even eigenvalue of the Gaussian fixed point is exactly one, and for $\alpha>\alpha_k$ it is larger than one. Nothing in this wiki computes what becomes of the $k$-th direction above $\alpha_k$ except at $k=2$, where the answer is the Bleher–Sinai theorem: for $\alpha>d/2$ the Gaussian fixed point is replaced, as the governor of the critical behaviour, by a nontrivial fixed point in which the quartic direction is irrelevant — that is the content of $\lambda_1\neq L^{\alpha}$, and it is why the Ising model, whose quartic coefficient is fixed by its prior, is nonetheless governed by it.

    The conjecture for general $k$ is the same statement one power up: for each $k\geq2$ and each $\alpha>\alpha_k$ there is a fixed point of $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}$ whose linearisation has exactly $k-1$ eigenvalues greater than one, the $k$-th even direction being irrelevant there, and which coalesces with the Gaussian fixed point at $\alpha=\alpha_k$. Its unstable manifold has the dimension one expects of a $2k$-critical point — the thermal direction and the $k-2$ couplings that must be tuned to reach it — and in that sense it is the hierarchy of tricritical, tetracritical and higher points. The wiki proves this for no $k$, including $k=2$ in the form just stated: B7WD is one fixed point at one intensity for one class of priors, and it does not come indexed by $k$. A proof would be that theorem with the first $k-2$ even couplings set to zero, which is a genuine generalisation and not a corollary.

    How one would compute one#

    The fixed-point equation is elementary to write and awkward to solve, and the difference between those two facts is the whole of the computational problem.

    The equation. For $m=2$ and a law $\mu$ with density $f$, one decimation step followed by the rescaling $L^{-\theta_0}$ gives

    $$(\mathcal M f)(s)=\frac{L^{\theta_0}}{Z}\int f(x)\,f(L^{\theta_0}s-x)\,e^{b x(L^{\theta_0}s-x)}\,dx,\qquad \theta_0=\tfrac12(d+\alpha),$$

    and a fixed point is a solution of $\mathcal M f=f$. The integral is not a convolution — the tilt depends on the product of the arguments — so the equation does not close on any finite set of moments, which is why the Gaussian case is the only one solved in closed form.

    The scheme. Truncate the potential rather than the map: write $f(s)=\exp\bigl(-\sum_{j=1}^{K}u_js^{2j}\bigr)/Z(u)$, apply one step to the full density by quadrature, and fit $-\log$ of the result to the same family. That defines a map $\Pi\circ\mathcal M$ on $K$ coefficients, and its fixed points are found by Newton rather than by iteration: $\mathcal M$ has eigenvalues greater than one at a fixed point, so plain iteration is repelled along the relevant directions and converges only if started almost exactly on the point.

    Identifying the branch. Newton finds whatever sits nearest its initial guess, so the scheme needs a criterion for which fixed point it has found: the multicritical branch is the one whose Jacobian has exactly $k-1$ eigenvalues larger than one, and the Gaussian solution must be discarded by that test rather than by inspection. The two other ingredients are continuation — starting at $\alpha$ just above $\alpha_k$, where the branch is close to the Gaussian fixed point and Newton converges easily, then stepping $\alpha$ upwards and using each solution as the next initial guess — and truncation stability, since a solution is only believed when it survives an increase of $K$.

    What this scheme does do. It is implemented for this wiki and checked on the case where the answer is known: with $K=3$ it reproduces the Gaussian fixed point to $1.5\times10^{-4}$ in the coefficients and its first three eigenvalues to about $4\times10^{-4}$, at $\alpha=0.5$ and $\alpha=0.8$ (the check is check_multicritical_scheme in tools/numeric_checks.py). That validation matters: a scheme that cannot find the fixed point one knows about cannot be trusted to find one nobody has seen.

    What it does not do. It does not locate a multicritical point. The obstacle is visible in the validation: with $K=4$ the highest coefficient is not controlled by the region in which the density is resolved, and the fourth eigenvalue comes out as noise, so the truncation that would be needed to see a $2k$-critical point for $k\geq3$ — whose distinguishing feature is precisely the behaviour of the $k$-th direction — is not reliable at this discretisation. Widening the resolved region, fitting in a basis adapted to the tails, and working in the exponential family generated by the moments are the three standard repairs, and none is carried out here.

    The analytic complement. Near the bifurcation the amplitude of the new branch is small and the problem is perturbative: expanding the fixed-point equation about the Gaussian point in powers of the deviation gives, at the order where the $k$-th direction first appears, an algebraic equation for the amplitude whose solution is the bifurcating branch. That is the same expansion that the fixed-point article attributes to Gawedzki and Kupiainen for $k=2$, organised in $\alpha-d/2$; running it at $\alpha_k$ for general $k$ is the natural way to get the branch's location without a global numerical solve, and it is not carried out in this wiki either.

    So the honest status is this: the equation is written, the scheme exists and is validated against the one case with a known answer, the criterion that would identify a multicritical branch is stated, and the numerical problem is left where it is hard — at the truncation order needed to resolve the $k$-th direction. A reader who wanted to close the gap would have a well-posed problem and a working validation to build on.

    What is not a fixed point#

    A law with heavy tails. If the prior's density decays like $\lvert s\rvert^{-p}$ with $p$ large, the tilt is not normalisable for any $b>0$, so the map is not defined on such a law and it can be neither a fixed point nor on an orbit. The class $\mathcal E$ is chosen to exclude it, and the exponential moment is what makes the tilt integrable.

    The product measure at infinite temperature. The prior $\mu_0$ is not a fixed point of the rescaled map: rescaling by $L^{-\theta_0}$ changes its variance. It is $\delta_0$, not $\mu_0$, that attracts the disordered phase.

    The percolation recursion. The percolation class has no one-variable fixed point, and that is structural rather than a gap in the analysis: the state of a block is the partition of its vertices into clusters, not the law of a number, so decimation acts on a much larger object [@hutchcroft-hierarchical-2021]. What survives is the growth of the cluster of the origin, which is fixed at exponent $\alpha$ by the same autonomy, and the exponents of the class are read off that rather than off a fixed law.

    The Euclidean renormalisation group. On $\mathbb Z^d$ the decimation step does not close on any finite-dimensional object, so there is no analogue of the table above: the fixed point exists as a point of an infinite-dimensional space and the statement of its existence is the content of the theory rather than a preliminary to it. The comparison article sets out what transfers and what does not.

    What is not known#

    Five things, in order of how much they would add. Whether the recursion has a nontrivial multicritical fixed point at each $\alpha_k=d(1-1/k)$, and whether it has fixed points off the physical orbit at all, is the question of the previous section and the largest of the gaps: the inventory of this article is complete for critical behaviour and open for the map. The value of $\lambda_1$ at the nontrivial fixed point is not known in closed form above $\alpha=d/2$; it is known near the threshold, which is what the $\varepsilon$-expansion computes. The pointwise two-point function at the critical fixed point is not proved to have the order $\lVert y\rVert^{-(d-\alpha)}$ for Ising spins, although the summed statement is now a theorem and for percolation the pointwise one is Hutchcroft's. The subleading spectrum is not classified: complex pairs occur, and the wiki records that as part of the spectrum statement without listing the structure. And the field direction is treated separately throughout, so the inventory above is of the zero-field map; a version with the field included would have a two-dimensional unstable manifold and would gain a column rather than a row.

    History and attribution#

    The transformation as an operator on laws, the Gaussian fixed points in closed form, and the existence, uniqueness and attractivity of the nontrivial fixed point are the work of Bleher and Sinai [@bleher-sinai-1973] [@bleher-sinai-1975], whose trichotomy of the orbit organises the inventory. The local analysis — the $\varepsilon$-expansion of the leading eigenvalue and the normal form of the unstable manifold — is Gawedzki and Kupiainen's [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983]. The parameter-free construction for the three-dimensional scalar hierarchy, by Borel summation of the large-field region, is Koch and Wittwer's [@koch-wittwer-1986]. The organisation of this article is the wiki's own; the statements are those of the articles cited in each row.
