    Hierarchical RG

    Twelve articles on the block-spin transformation, its exact recursion, its fixed points and their exponents.

    The renormalisation group is exact on a hierarchical lattice: a block has no boundary, every pair between two children carries one coupling, and one decimation step is an operation on the law of a single number. This wiki develops that structure — the lattice, the recursion, the fixed points, the exponents, the literature — with complete proofs where they are short and precise attribution where they are not. Each article has a simulation or a worked computation attached where one is illuminating.

    The articles

    model · Hierarchical models

    The hierarchical lattice

    An ultrametric group whose blocks fall into L^d congruent children.

    The hierarchical lattice is the direct sum of countably many copies of (Z/LZ)^d, with an ultrametric whose balls have exactly L^(dn) points. Each ball splits into L^d congruent children, every pair of vertices in different children of a ball sits at the top distance of that ball, and power kernels are summable exactly when their exponent exceeds the dimension. These three facts are what make block decimation close on one-variable laws.

    theorem · Hierarchical models

    Hierarchical spin systems and decimation

    The block spin of a hierarchical model is distributed as a functional of independent copies of the block spin one scale down.

    A spin system on the hierarchical lattice with couplings constant on levels has a Hamiltonian that splits into the Hamiltonians of its children plus one interaction between their sums. Consequently the partition function and the law of a block spin are recovered from the law one scale down by an explicit operation, the free energy has an exact recursion, and the whole renormalisation group is a recursion on one-variable laws.

    definition · Renormalisation group

    The renormalisation group map

    Decimation as an operator on one-variable laws, and the scaling that makes it autonomous.

    Block decimation sends the law of a child's block spin to the law of the parent's: a nonlinear operator on probability measures on the line, a Gibbs tilt of a product measure followed by summation. Rescaling the spin by L to the power minus (d+alpha)/2 makes the operators at different scales into powers of a single map, whose fixed points and linearisations carry the critical behaviour.

    theorem · Renormalisation group

    The Gaussian renormalisation group

    On Gaussian laws the decimation closes on the variance, and the critical fixed point has multiplier L to the alpha.

    For a Gaussian prior, the law of a block spin stays Gaussian and its variance obeys a fractional linear recursion that is solved in closed form. The critical intensity is the reciprocal of the total kernel mass, the susceptibility is exactly beta_c over beta_c minus beta, and the recursion, written in the dimensionless coupling, has a nonzero fixed point with multiplier L to the alpha; hence the correlation-length exponent is one over alpha and the two-point exponent is two minus alpha.

    theorem · Renormalisation group

    Critical exponents from the renormalisation group

    Two numbers from the fixed point, and the algebra that turns them into the exponents.

    The exponents of a hierarchical model are read off from its fixed point: one from the multiplier of the linearised map, giving the correlation-length exponent, and one from the growth of the block spin, giving the susceptibility exponent and hence the two-point exponent. The scaling relations are then algebra, and hyperscaling is the one hypothesis that is not automatic.

    Corrections to scaling

    The second eigenvalue, and why a power law is only ever the first term.

    Near a fixed point the orbit approaches along the stable directions, so every power law carries corrections from the second eigenvalue. In the hierarchical model the corrections are geometric in the scale and therefore pure powers of the correlation length, one for each subleading eigenvalue, with exponent omega equal to minus the logarithm of the subleading eigenvalue over the logarithm of the scale; a complex pair makes them oscillate in the logarithm of the length, and an eigenvalue tending to one makes them logarithmic.

    theorem · Renormalisation group

    The Bleher–Sinai fixed point

    Above the threshold a non-Gaussian law is self-similar, and its multiplier is not one over alpha.

    For kernel exponent above half the dimension the critical orbit of the hierarchical recursion converges to a self-similar law that is not Gaussian: the fixed point of Bleher and Sinai and of Gawedzki and Kupiainen. Its linearisation has a simple leading eigenvalue, which gives a correlation-length exponent different from the classical one over alpha, and a spectrum whose next eigenvalue controls corrections to scaling, sometimes oscillating.

    theorem · Hierarchical models

    Hierarchical models with continuous symmetry

    With a vector spin the block direction does not settle at criticality, and the marginal exponent is decided by a slowly varying factor.

    For the r-component hierarchical model the decimation map acts on laws on R^r by the same tilt, and it commutes with rotations. Bleher and Major proved Dyson's conjecture for this model: at the marginal kernel exponent a transition exists exactly when the level couplings are not summable, the spontaneous magnetisation vanishes at the critical temperature, and the normalised average spin still converges to the uniform law on the sphere. The Gaussian fixed point is r-independent; the continuous symmetry enters through a marginal angular direction.

    model · Hierarchical models

    The phi-fourth hierarchical model and its truncations

    The recursion is an integral operator on potentials; the Gaussian truncation is exact and every other truncation leaks.

    Written on potentials rather than on laws, the decimation step is an explicit integral operator: the effective potential of a block is minus the logarithm of a constrained convolution of the child potential. Quadratic potentials are mapped to quadratic potentials and the flow on them is the fractional linear recursion for the variance; quartic and higher truncations are not invariant, because the constrained average of a quartic generates powers of every even order. The two rigorous treatments of the resulting infinite-dimensional flow are Koch and Wittwer's fixed point and, in the Euclidean setting, the trajectory of Abdesselam.

    theorem · Renormalisation group

    Koch and Wittwer's non-Gaussian fixed point

    Convergent expansions for a fixed point with unbounded spins, large fields included.

    Koch and Wittwer proved the existence of a non-Gaussian fixed point of the hierarchical renormalisation map, in the hierarchical approximation of the three-dimensional phi-fourth fixed point, by a method that treats the large-field region: Borel summation brings the region where the perturbative expansion fails back into it, so that the theory is described by convergent expansions. The companion papers study the transformation itself, with its beta function and the relation between Dyson's and Gallavotti's hierarchies, and treat the Dyson-Baker hierarchy.

    theorem · Renormalisation group

    Abdesselam's trajectory between two fixed points

    A whole orbit of the renormalisation group, from the Gaussian fixed point to the nontrivial one.

    A fixed point describes one scale-invariant theory; a trajectory between two fixed points describes the whole crossover from the ultraviolet to the infrared. Abdesselam constructed such a trajectory rigorously and nonperturbatively for a three-dimensional phi-fourth model with modified propagator, joining the Gaussian fixed point to an analogue of the Wilson-Fisher fixed point, by a fixed point argument in a Banach space of sequences which perturbs a nonlinear one-dimensional iteration.

    theorem · Hierarchical models

    Renormalisation for hierarchical percolation

    Cluster amplification is the relevant direction, and the triangle threshold is three alpha against d.

    For long-range percolation on the hierarchical lattice the renormalisation group acts on clusters rather than on a one-variable law, and its relevant direction is the amplification of large clusters across a block. The critical two-point function decays like the distance to the power minus d minus alpha for every exponent in the long-range range, and the triangle diagram converges exactly when three alpha is below d, which is where the model stops being mean-field.

    proposition · Renormalisation group

    Hierarchical and Euclidean renormalisation

    Why the recursion closes here, what fails on the lattice, and how the model is used anyway.

    The hierarchical recursion is exact because a block has no boundary: all pairs between two children carry one coupling, so the decimated state is a function of the child states alone. On the Euclidean lattice a block has a boundary of codimension one, the effective interaction after decimation depends on the geometry of the boundary and not on block sums, and the decimated measure need not be Gibbsian. The hierarchical model is nevertheless the reference for power counting, for the epsilon expansion and for the local analysis of the renormalisation map.

    model · Hierarchical models

    Ultrametric block systems and the p-adic models

    What the recursion actually needs, and how little of it is specific to the hierarchical lattice.

    The decimation recursion uses only a nested system of blocks with a constant number of children per block. Any ultrametric group with such a system carries the same model, the same map and the same exponents: the p-adic integers with their balls, the hierarchical lattice with its subgroups, and their variants. The child decomposition and the decimation identity are proved here in that generality, and the one place where the compact and discrete cases differ is isolated.
