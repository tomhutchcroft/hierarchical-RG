    The hierarchical lattice

    An ultrametric group whose blocks fall into L^d congruent children.

    model · separate page

    The hierarchical lattice $\mathbb H^d_L$ is the abelian group of finite sequences with entries in $(\mathbb Z/L\mathbb Z)^d$, carrying the ultrametric in which two vertices are at distance $L^k$ when the last coordinate at which they differ is the $k$-th. Its balls are nested subgroups, of exactly $L^{dn}$ vertices at radius $L^n$, and each ball splits into $L^d$ congruent children. Every pair of vertices in different children of a ball lies at the top distance of that ball, so a coupling that charges each pair according to its distance charges all pairs between two children at one rate, which depends only on the two block sums. This is the structure that makes block decimation an exact operation on a single variable, and it is why the model appears throughout the rigorous theory of the renormalisation group [@bauerschmidt-brydges-slade-2019].

    The lattice was introduced by Dyson (1969) as a one-dimensional model with long-range couplings in which a phase transition can be proved [@dyson-1969], and the group-theoretic description used here, with balls as cosets of nested subgroups, is the form in which the renormalisation group is usually set up [@bleher-sinai-1973]. A power kernel $\lVert x\rVert^{-d-\alpha}$ is summable exactly when $\alpha>0$, so the model with such a kernel is a long-range spin system with a finite interaction range in the only sense that matters: the total intensity at each vertex is finite.

    Two conventions are worth fixing at the outset. The dimension enters the lattice only through the block volume $m=L^d$ and the decay ratio $\lambda=L^{d+\alpha}=mL^{\alpha}$; any integer $m\geq2$ and any ratio $\lambda>m$ arise from some pair $(d,\alpha)$ once a scale $L$ is chosen. And the lattice is a group, so distances are translation invariant and every vertex looks like the origin.

    Definitions and setting

    Fix integers $L\geq2$ and $d\geq1$ and write $m=L^d$ and $A=(\mathbb Z/L\mathbb Z)^d$, so that $\lvert A\rvert=m$. The vertex set is the direct sum

    $$\mathbb H^d_L=\bigoplus_{i\geq1}A=\bigl\{x=(x_1,x_2,\ldots):x_i\in A,\ x_i=0\text{ for all but finitely many }i\bigr\},$$

    an abelian group under coordinatewise addition. Write $0$ for its identity.

    For $x\neq0$ put $\operatorname{lev}(x)=\max\{i\geq1:x_i\neq0\}$ and $\operatorname{lev}(0)=0$, and define the hierarchical distance

    $$\lVert x\rVert=L^{\operatorname{lev}(x)}\quad(x\neq0),\qquad \lVert0\rVert=0,$$

    and $\lVert x-y\rVert$ for a pair of vertices. For $n\geq0$ the level-$n$ block at the origin is $\Lambda_n=\{x:\operatorname{lev}(x)\leq n\}$, the set of sequences vanishing above coordinate $n$, and the level-$n$ block at $x$ is the coset $x+\Lambda_n$. A vertex of level $n$ is one at distance $L^n$ from the origin; the shell at level $n$ is $\Lambda_n\setminus\Lambda_{n-1}$.

    Here $\Lambda_0=\{0\}$, and the first nontrivial displacement available on $\mathbb H^d_L$ has length $L$. The notation $\langle x\rangle=\max\{1,\lVert x\rVert\}$ regularises the distance at the origin and is used whenever a formula has to be read at $x=0$.

    Blocks, shells and the triangle inequality

    The level is subadditive under addition, $\operatorname{lev}(u+v)\leq\max\{\operatorname{lev}(u),\operatorname{lev}(v)\}$, and invariant under negation. Consequently $\lVert x-z\rVert\leq\max\{\lVert x-y\rVert,\lVert y-z\rVert\}$ for all $x,y,z$, the distance is symmetric and translation invariant, and $\lVert x\rVert=0$ only at $x=0$. Each $\Lambda_n$ is a subgroup, the blocks of a fixed level are its cosets, and two blocks are disjoint or one contains the other. Finally

    $$\lvert\Lambda_n\rvert=L^{dn}=m^n,\qquad \bigl\lvert\{x:\operatorname{lev}(x)=n\}\bigr\rvert=L^{dn}-L^{d(n-1)}\quad(n\geq1).$$
    Proof of ultrametricity and the counts

    Let $u,v\in\mathbb H^d_L$ and $k=\max\{\operatorname{lev}(u),\operatorname{lev}(v)\}$. Both vanish above coordinate $k$, hence so does $u+v$, and $\operatorname{lev}(u+v)\leq k$; the coordinates of $-u$ are the negatives of those of $u$, so $\operatorname{lev}(-u)=\operatorname{lev}(u)$. Taking $u=x-y$ and $v=y-z$ gives the strong triangle inequality. Symmetry is $\operatorname{lev}(y-x)=\operatorname{lev}(x-y)$, translation invariance is $\operatorname{lev}((x+w)-(y+w))=\operatorname{lev}(x-y)$, and positive definiteness is the definition together with $\operatorname{lev}(x)\geq1$ for $x\neq0$.

    The condition $\operatorname{lev}(z)\leq n$ is exactly $z_i=0$ for $i>n$, and the set of such $z$ is closed under addition and negation, so $\Lambda_n$ is a subgroup; it is contained in $\Lambda_{n+1}$. A translate of a subgroup is a coset, and two cosets of the same subgroup coincide or are disjoint; nested subgroups give the same statement for blocks of different levels.

    Restriction to the first $n$ coordinates maps $\Lambda_n$ bijectively onto $A^n$, with inverse given by appending zeros, so $\lvert\Lambda_n\rvert=\lvert A\rvert^n=m^n=L^{dn}$. The shell at level $n$ is $\Lambda_n\setminus\Lambda_{n-1}$ and the two blocks are nested, so its cardinality is the difference.

    Every vertex of $\Lambda_n$ is within distance $L^n$ of every other: a pair of points of $\Lambda_n$ differs only in coordinates at most $n$, so its level is at most $n$. This is the sense in which a block has no boundary: the diameter of $\Lambda_n$ is exactly the radius at which it first appears.

    The child decomposition

    The single structural fact behind the exactness of decimation is that a block is the disjoint union of congruent children, and that the interaction between two children is a function of their total spins alone. For $a\in A$ and $n\geq1$ let $e_n(a)$ be the vertex whose $n$-th coordinate is $a$ and whose other coordinates vanish.

    For $n\geq1$,

    $$\Lambda_n=\bigsqcup_{a\in A}\bigl(e_n(a)+\Lambda_{n-1}\bigr),$$

    the pieces are the blocks of radius $L^{n-1}$ contained in $\Lambda_n$, each is a translate of $\Lambda_{n-1}$, and $e_n(a)$ has level $n$. If $x$ and $y$ lie in different pieces then $\lVert x-y\rVert=L^n$, whatever the distance between the two pieces as sets.

    Proof of the child decomposition

    Let $z\in\Lambda_n$ and $a=z_n$. Then $z-e_n(a)$ has vanishing $n$-th coordinate and vanishes above $n$ because $z$ does, so $z-e_n(a)\in\Lambda_{n-1}$ and $z\in e_n(a)+\Lambda_{n-1}$. If $z\in e_n(a)+\Lambda_{n-1}$ then $z_n=a$, so the pieces for distinct $a$ are disjoint, which gives the union. Since $e_n(a)\in\Lambda_n$ and $\Lambda_n$ is a subgroup, $e_n(a)+\Lambda_{n-1}$ is a coset of $\Lambda_{n-1}$ inside $\Lambda_n$; the blocks of radius $L^{n-1}$ are exactly the cosets of $\Lambda_{n-1}$, and by the previous section each is contained in or disjoint from $\Lambda_n$. Finally $\operatorname{lev}(e_n(a))=n$ because the $n$-th coordinate is the last nonzero one.

    For the last claim let $x=e_n(a)+u$ and $y=e_n(b)+v$ with $u,v\in\Lambda_{n-1}$ and $a\neq b$. Then $x-y=e_n(a-b)+(u-v)$, and $u-v\in\Lambda_{n-1}$ vanishes at coordinate $n$, so the $n$-th coordinate of $x-y$ is $a-b\neq0$ and no coordinate above $n$ is nonzero. Hence $\operatorname{lev}(x-y)=n$.

    The count is consistent: $\lvert A\rvert\cdot\lvert\Lambda_{n-1}\rvert=m\cdot m^{n-1}=m^n=\lvert\Lambda_n\rvert$.

    The theorem is what a decimation step needs, since a pair chosen from two different children of $\Lambda_n$ has the same level $n$ whatever the vertices, so a kernel that is constant on levels assigns the same intensity to all $m(m-1)/2$ pairs of distinct children, and the energy of the block depends on the children only through their sums.

    Power kernels

    A kernel is a nonnegative symmetric function $J:\mathbb H^d_L\to[0,\infty)$ with $J(0)$ irrelevant for the graph. The power kernel at exponent $\alpha$ is $J(x)=\lVert x\rVert^{-d-\alpha}$ off the origin, and its level-$k$ value is $J_k=L^{-(d+\alpha)k}$, so it charges each pair of the level-$k$ shell by one constant.

    For real $\alpha$ and $J\geq0$,

    $$\sum_{x\neq0}\lVert x\rVert^{-d-\alpha}<\infty\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\alpha>0,$$

    and for $\alpha>0$ the row sum of the kernel $J\lVert x\rVert^{-d-\alpha}$ is

    $$\sum_{x\neq0}J\lVert x\rVert^{-d-\alpha}=(1-L^{-d})\,J\,\frac{L^{-\alpha}}{1-L^{-\alpha}}.$$
    Proof of the summability criterion

    The shell at level $k$ has $(1-L^{-d})L^{dk}=L^{dk}-L^{d(k-1)}$ vertices, all at distance $L^k$, so the sum is $(1-L^{-d})\sum_{k\geq1}L^{dk}L^{-(d+\alpha)k}=(1-L^{-d})\sum_{k\geq1}L^{-\alpha k}$. All terms are nonnegative, so the sum may be regrouped. The geometric series $\sum_{k\geq1}L^{-\alpha k}$ converges exactly when $L^{-\alpha}<1$, that is when $\alpha>0$, and then equals $L^{-\alpha}/(1-L^{-\alpha})$.

    Since $\alpha>0$, the kernel is integrable and the intensity of the interaction at a vertex is finite; this is the sense in which the model is nearest-neighbour-like despite its long range. At $\alpha=0$ each shell contributes the same mass $1-L^{-d}$ and the sum diverges; for $\alpha<0$ the mass of the shells grows.

    Examples and non-examples

    The level-2 block. Take $d=1$, $L=2$, so that $m=2$ and $\Lambda_2$ has four vertices. With $e_2(1)$ the sequence $(0,1,0,\ldots)$ and $e_1(1)=(1,0,\ldots)$, the children of $\Lambda_2$ are $\{0,e_1(1)\}$ and $\{e_2(1),e_1(1)+e_2(1)\}$, each a translate of $\Lambda_1$; the four pairs between the children have distance $4$, and the two pairs inside them have distance $2$. A coupling that charges pairs by distance therefore charges the within-child pairs at $J_1$ and the between-child pairs at $J_2$, so the block splits into two interacting copies of itself.

    The geometric kernel. The kernel $J(x)=Cr^{\operatorname{lev}(x)}$ on $\mathbb H^d_L$, with $C\geq0$ and $0\leq r<L^{-d}$, is summable, with row sum $C(1-L^{-d})r/(1-L^dr)$. This family is closed under rescalings of the level, and it contains the power kernel at $r=L^{-(d+\alpha)}$; a Gaussian model with such a kernel is exactly solvable, which is the subject of the Gaussian article.

    A pair-dependent kernel. If $J(x,y)$ depends on the pair and not on the level, as for instance a kernel that charges only pairs inside a fixed family of blocks, then the children of a block are still congruent but the energy of the block is no longer a function of the child sums. The decimation identity of the next article is specific to kernels that are constant on levels.

    The borderline exponent. At $\alpha=0$ the power kernel is not summable and the corresponding percolation model is $\beta_c=0$ for every $\beta>0$: every vertex has infinitely many neighbours of infinite total intensity. The condition $\alpha>0$ is therefore not a technical convenience in the hypotheses below.

    The full product. The compact group $\prod_{i\geq1}A$, with the same definition of level for pairs differing in finitely many coordinates, is not $\mathbb H^d_L$: it is uncountable, and the distance between two of its points can be zero even when the points differ, at coordinates tending to infinity. All statements here concern the direct sum.

    Variants and generalisations

    Dimension as bookkeeping. Only $m=L^d$ and the ratios $J_k/J_{k+1}=L^{d+\alpha}$ enter the definitions, so a model is parametrised by the integer $m\geq2$, the ratio $\lambda>m$ and the amplitude $J$, and the pair $(d,\alpha)$ records a choice of scale: $d=\log m/\log L$ and $\alpha=\log(\lambda/m)/\log L$. Fixing integer $d$ costs nothing.

    Let $d\geq1$ and $L\geq2$, and let $\psi:(\mathbb Z/L\mathbb Z)^d\to\mathbb Z/L^d\mathbb Z$ be a bijection taking $0$ to $0$. Applying $\psi$ in every coordinate gives a group isomorphism $\Psi:\mathbb H^d_L\to\mathbb H^1_{L^d}$ with

    $$\lVert\Psi(x)-\Psi(y)\rVert_{\mathbb H^1_{L^d}}=\lVert x-y\rVert_{\mathbb H^d_L}^{\,d}.$$
    Proof of the distance identity

    The map $\psi(a)=\sum_{j=1}^da_jL^{j-1}$ is a bijection of the underlying sets fixing $0$, and applying it coordinatewise is a bijection of the direct sums. A vertex of $\mathbb H^d_L$ has level $k$ exactly when its last nonzero entry is the $k$-th, which the coordinatewise application preserves because level is read off the index and not the entry; a vertex of $\mathbb H^1_{L^d}$ at level $k$ has distance $(L^d)^k=L^{dk}$ from the origin and distance $L^k$ in the other lattice. Group structure is not needed for the distance identity, and it holds for $\Psi$ applied to a difference since the map is applied entrywise.

    Under this identification the power kernel $\lVert x\rVert^{-d-\alpha}$ on $\mathbb H^d_L$ becomes $\lVert y\rVert^{-1-\alpha/d}$ on $\mathbb H^1_{L^d}$, so a statement about power kernels at every exponent on the one-dimensional lattice covers every dimension. The dimensional parameter is thus the ratio $\alpha/d$, which is why exponents in this model are functions of $\alpha/d$ and of the ratio of the kernel to the scale, and not of $d$ alone.

    Other coefficient groups. Nothing in the definitions uses finiteness of $A$ except the counts. With $A=\mathbb Z^d$ the level-one block is infinite, the shells are infinite, the summability criterion changes, and the model is a long-range system on a tree-like group; the child decomposition survives, the counting arguments do not.

    History and attribution

    Dyson (1969) introduced the one-dimensional model with couplings decaying as a power and proved that a phase transition occurs for decay exponents between one and two [@dyson-1969]; the hierarchical approximation used there is the lattice above with $d=1$ and a coupling that is constant on the shells of the ultrametric. The group-theoretic description, in which blocks are cosets of nested subgroups with $L^d$ children each, is the form in which Bleher and Sinai set up the renormalisation transformation [@bleher-sinai-1973], and it is the presentation used in the modern development of the renormalisation group for lattice field theory [@bauerschmidt-brydges-slade-2019].

    The counts of this article are elementary and appear in each of those sources; the statement that all pairs between two children of a block lie at the top level of that block is the property that distinguishes this lattice from a Euclidean block partition, where the interaction between two neighbouring blocks depends on the shape of their common boundary. The comparison is the subject of Hierarchical and Euclidean renormalisation.

    The isometries of the hierarchical lattice

    Why any two pairs at the same level are the same pair, and why block sums of a block are exchangeable.

    theorem · separate page

    Five articles in this wiki reason "by symmetry", and this article supplies the symmetry. The statements are that the two-point function of a radial kernel depends only on the distance; that the block sums of the children of a block are exchangeable; and that a conditional expectation given those block sums is constant over the vertices of a child. Each is true, and each follows from the same two facts: translations are isometries, and so are bijections that act on each coordinate separately. Together they move any ordered pair of vertices at a given level onto any other such pair.

    The last of the three is the one that does real work. In the Gaussian two-point computation the conditional expectation of a single spin given the block sums is replaced by the block sum divided by the size of the block; in the percolation article the level isometries convert an averaged bound into a pointwise one; and in the vector model it is exchangeability of the child sums that reduces the recursion to the law of one block sum, with all components moving together. Without a transitivity statement each of those steps is an assertion about a picture.

    One caution organises the proofs. Preserving the level of every vertex is strictly weaker than being an isometry: on a block of eight vertices a transposition of two vertices of level three preserves all vertex levels and changes the level of a pair. The example is given below, and it is the reason the theorem is about pairs.

    Definitions and setting

    A bijection $\phi:\mathbb H^d_L\to\mathbb H^d_L$ is an isometry if $\lVert\phi(x)-\phi(y)\rVert=\lVert x-y\rVert$ for all $x,y$, equivalently if $\operatorname{lev}(\phi(x)-\phi(y))=\operatorname{lev}(x-y)$. Write $\operatorname{Isom}$ for the set of isometries; it is a group under composition, and it contains the translations $\tau_v(x)=x+v$ for $v\in\mathbb H^d_L$.

    For $a\in A$ let $\psi_a$ be the map that acts on each coordinate separately by a bijection of $A$: if $\sigma=(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\ldots)$ is a sequence of bijections of $A$, then $\psi_\sigma(x)^{(i)}=\sigma_i(x^{(i)})$ for every $i$. These are the coordinate-wise bijections.

    Two families of isometries

    Translations are isometries, and so is every coordinate-wise bijection. Consequently, for each $n\geq0$ and every pair of ordered pairs $(x,y)$, $(x',y')$ with $\operatorname{lev}(y-x)=\operatorname{lev}(y'-x')=n$, there is an isometry $\phi$ with $\phi(x)=x'$ and $\phi(y)=y'$.

    Proof

    A translation satisfies $\operatorname{lev}((x+v)-(y+v))=\operatorname{lev}(x-y)$ because levels of differences are translation invariant, which was proved in the lattice article.

    For a coordinate-wise bijection, the coordinates at which $\psi_\sigma(x)$ and $\psi_\sigma(y)$ differ are exactly the coordinates at which $x$ and $y$ differ: if $x^{(i)}\neq y^{(i)}$ then $\sigma_i(x^{(i)})\neq\sigma_i(y^{(i)})$ because $\sigma_i$ is injective, and if $x^{(i)}=y^{(i)}$ then the images are equal. The set of indices at which two vertices differ is therefore preserved, so its maximum is preserved, which is the level.

    For transitivity, let $x,y$ have $y-x=z$ of level $n$, and let $x',y'$ have $y'-x'=z'$ of level $n$. Translate by $-x$: the pair becomes $(0,z)$. By the previous paragraph, choose bijections $\sigma_i$ of $A$ with $\sigma_i(z^{(i)})=z'^{(i)}$ for $i\leq n$ and arbitrary for $i>n$ (possible because $A$ is a set and $\sigma_i$ is only required to take one prescribed value); the corresponding coordinate-wise bijection fixes $0$ and sends $z$ to $z'$. Composing with the translation by $x'$ gives an isometry taking $(x,y)$ to $(x',y')$.

    The proof uses no structure of $A$ beyond its cardinality, so the statement holds for the coefficient group $\mathbb Z^d$ as well, where the lattice is the direct sum of copies of $\mathbb Z^d$: the levels are unaffected.

    Consequences that were being assumed

    Let the kernel depend only on the level, and let the model be the hierarchical spin system on a block with free boundary.

    (i) Radiality. For the model on $\mathbb H^d_L$, the two-point function satisfies $\tau(x,y)=\tau(0,y-x)$ and depends only on $\operatorname{lev}(y-x)$.

    (ii) Exchangeability of child sums. In the model on $\Lambda_N$, the $m$-tuple of block sums $(S_a)$ of the children of $\Lambda_N$ is exchangeable, and so is the corresponding tuple for the children of any block.

    (iii) Constancy on a child. For $x,y$ in the same child of a block of level $n$, and for any function $F$ of the child sums, $\mathbb E[\sigma_xF]=\mathbb E[\sigma_yF]$ and $\mathbb E[\sigma_x\mid(S_a)]$ takes the same value at $x$ and $y$.

    Proof

    An isometry preserving the level of every difference preserves the Hamiltonian, because the Hamiltonian is a sum of couplings that depend only on the level; and it preserves the prior, which is a product. Hence it preserves the Gibbs measure, and expectations of functions of the configuration composed with it are unchanged. This is the mechanism of all three statements.

    (i) Given $x,y$ with $z=y-x$ of level $n$, the theorem gives an isometry fixing $0$ and sending $z$ to any prescribed vertex of level $n$; the pair $(x,y)$ is sent to $(0,w)$ with $w$ of level $n$, and $\tau$ is preserved. So $\tau(x,y)$ depends only on $n$ and equals $\tau(0,w)$ for every $w$ at that level.

    (ii) Translation by any $v\in\Lambda_N$ preserves $\Lambda_N$, preserves the Hamiltonian (levels are translation invariant) and permutes the children, since the children are the cosets of $\Lambda_{N-1}$ and $v+\cdot$ is a bijection of the set of cosets. For any two children $B,B'$ choose $v\in B-B'$; then the translation by $v$ maps $B'$ to $B$ and permutes the others, and it preserves the law. Iterating gives exchangeability of the tuple.

    (iii) Translation by any $v\in\Lambda_{n-1}$ maps each child of a level-$n$ block to itself — the children are cosets of $\Lambda_{n-1}$ — so it fixes every child sum, and it is an isometry, so it preserves the law. Any two vertices of a child differ by an element of $\Lambda_{n-1}$, so the transitivity of these translations inside the child gives the first identity; the second follows by taking $F$ to run over the generating functions of the conditional law, or directly from the definition of conditional expectation.

    Statement (iii) is exactly what licenses the step "$\mathbb E[\sigma_x\mid(S_a)]=S_a/\lvert\Lambda_{n-1}\rvert$" in the Gaussian two-point computation, and its analogues elsewhere. Note that it is a statement about conditional expectations given the sums, not about the conditional law being uniform: the conditional law of the spins in a child given their sum is not uniform, but its first moment is.

    Examples and non-examples

    Translations alone are not enough. A translation preserves the displacement $y-x$, so it moves a pair only within its own class and cannot relate two different pairs at the same level. The coordinate-wise bijections supply the rest, and they are generally not translations: in $\Lambda_2$ with $L=3$, $d=1$, the map sending $(x_1,x_2)$ to $(2x_1,x_2)$ is a coordinate-wise bijection fixing $0$, and it is not a translation, since it exchanges $(1,0)$ and $(2,0)$ while a translation fixing $0$ is the identity.

    A level-preserving bijection that is not an isometry. Take $d=1$, $L=2$ and the block $\Lambda_3$ of eight vertices, written as triples. Transpose $(0,1,1)$ and $(1,0,1)$ and fix everything else. Both moved vertices have level three, and every other vertex is fixed, so the level of every vertex is preserved. But the pair $\{(0,0,1),(0,1,1)\}$ has level two, being a pair differing in coordinate two, while its image $\{(0,0,1),(1,0,1)\}$ has level one. The map therefore changes a level of a pair and is not an isometry.

    The same symmetry on another block system. The theorem is stated for the lattice; on a general ultrametric block system of the general article the translation part survives verbatim, since levels are translation invariant there too, and transitivity on pairs at a level holds whenever the coefficient group is large enough to carry the coordinate-wise bijections.

    The p-adic case. On $\mathbb Z_p$ the maps $x\mapsto a+bx$ with $a\in\mathbb Z_p$ and $\lvert b\rvert_p=1$ are isometries, since $\lvert b(x-y)\rvert_p=\lvert x-y\rvert_p$, and they are already transitive on pairs at a level: if $\lvert x-y\rvert_p=\lvert x'-y'\rvert_p$ then $b=(x'-y')/(x-y)$ has $\lvert b\rvert_p=1$ and $a=x'-bx$ lies in $\mathbb Z_p$. So the conclusion of the theorem holds there with a smaller group.

    The two families are a subgroup, not the whole group. The isometry group is larger. Permuting the $L^d$ children of a single block and leaving every other vertex alone is an isometry, since it moves each child of that block onto a child of the same block and preserves the internal structure of each; and such a map need not be a composition of a translation with coordinate-wise bijections. What cannot be done is to permute the coordinates: the level records which coordinate is the outermost, so in $\Lambda_2$ with $L=2$, $d=2$ the vertices $0$ and $((0,0),(1,0))$ are at level two, while their images under the interchange of the two coordinates are at level one. The two families are used below because they are the ones that act transitively on pairs, not because they exhaust $\operatorname{Isom}$.

    Variants and generalisations

    Isometries of a general block system. For a block system $(G,G_n)$ the same two families exist whenever $G_n/G_{n+1}$ carries enough bijections; the translation part always does, and the conclusion (i) of the corollary then holds for radial kernels with no further hypothesis.

    Vector spins and percolation. The isometries act on configurations by permuting vertices, so they preserve vector-valued models and percolation configurations as well. This is why the two-point function of a radial kernel is radial in those models, and why the sibling clusters in the percolation recursion are equidistributed.

    Symmetry breaking. Below the critical temperature the Gibbs measure need not be invariant under the full isometry group; the symmetric state is the average over the broken ones. The statements above are about the symmetric Gibbs measure, which is the one the free-boundary finite-volume models converge to along symmetric subsequences.

    History and attribution

    The level isometries are used by Hutchcroft to convert an averaged two-point bound into a pointwise one, on the grounds that any two pairs at a distance are exchanged by an isometry preserving the law [@hutchcroft-hierarchical-2021]; the proof given here is the elementary form of that observation. The symmetry of the block sums is used from the beginning of the subject, in the reduction of the recursion to a single variable [@bleher-sinai-1973], and again in the p-adic setting [@abdesselam-2018]. The counterexample in the examples section is included because the distinction between preserving vertex levels and preserving pair levels is easy to miss.

    Hierarchical spin systems and decimation

    The block spin of a hierarchical model is distributed as a functional of independent copies of the block spin one scale down.

    theorem · separate page

    A hierarchical spin system is a spin model on $\mathbb H^d_L$ whose coupling between two vertices is a function of their level alone, so that the coupling at level $k$ has one value $J_k=JL^{-(d+\alpha)k}$. Its energy on a block splits exactly into the energies of the children of that block plus a single interaction between the child sums: pairs inside a child are at levels below the top, pairs in different children are all at the top level, and no pair inside the block is at a level above it. The partition function therefore factorises, and the law of a block spin is the image of the law one scale down under an explicit operation — block decimation — which acts on the single variable $\sum_{x\in\text{child}}\sigma_x$.

    This article states that identity, its iterates, and the free energy recursion that follows; the operation itself is studied in the next article and solved exactly in the Gaussian case. The identity is what is special about the lattice: on $\mathbb Z^d$ a decimation step produces an interaction that depends on the shape of the boundary between blocks, whereas here it produces one number per block.

    The structure is Dyson's (1969), who introduced the hierarchical approximation to prove a phase transition for power-law couplings [@dyson-1969], and it is the setting of the renormalisation analysis of Bleher and Sinai [@bleher-sinai-1973]. Only free boundary conditions are used, so every object below is finite-dimensional and every identity is exact at finite volume.

    Definitions and setting

    Fix integers $L\geq2$, $d\geq1$ and a real $\alpha>0$, write $m=L^d$, and let $J>0$. Let $\varrho$ be a probability measure on $\mathbb R$ with $\int\sigma^2\varrho(d\sigma)<\infty$; the two cases used below are the Ising prior $\varrho=\tfrac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)$ and the Gaussian prior $\varrho=N(0,1)$. Spins are real numbers $\sigma_x$.

    For finite $S\subset\mathbb H^d_L$ and $\beta\geq0$, the hierarchical Hamiltonian is

    $$H_S(\sigma)=-\sum_{\{x,y\}\subset S}J(\lVert x-y\rVert)\sigma_x\sigma_y,\qquad J(L^k)=J_k=JL^{-(d+\alpha)k},$$

    the partition function and Gibbs measure are

    $$Z_S(\beta)=\int_{\mathbb R^S}\exp\bigl(-\beta H_S(\sigma)\bigr)\prod_{x\in S}\varrho(d\sigma_x),\qquad \mathbb P_S(d\sigma)=\frac{e^{-\beta H_S(\sigma)}}{Z_S(\beta)}\prod_{x\in S}\varrho(d\sigma_x),$$

    and the block spin of $S$ is $S_S=\sum_{x\in S}\sigma_x$. For $n\geq0$ put $Z_n=Z_{\Lambda_n}$, $\mathbb P_n=\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}$ and $\mu_n$ for the law of $S_{\Lambda_n}$ under $\mathbb P_n$, so that $\mu_0=\varrho$. The two-point function is $\tau_\beta(x,y)=\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}}[\sigma_x\sigma_y]$ for any $n$ with $x,y\in\Lambda_n$, a definition independent of $n$ because the Hamiltonian is a sum over pairs.

    Pairs at level $k$ are those in different children of a common level-$k$ block, by the child decomposition, so the Hamiltonian may be regrouped by level. That regrouping is the first statement.

    The level decomposition

    For $n\geq1$ let $\Lambda^{(a)}=e_n(a)+\Lambda_{n-1}$, $a\in A$, be the children of $\Lambda_n$ and let $Q_k(\sigma)$ be the sum of $\sigma_x\sigma_y$ over the pairs at level $k$. Then

    $$-\beta H_{\Lambda_n}(\sigma)=\sum_{a\in A}\bigl(-\beta H_{\Lambda^{(a)}}(\sigma)\bigr)+\beta J_n\sum_{a

    and $-\beta H_{\Lambda_n}=\beta\sum_{k=1}^nJ_kQ_k$.

    Proof of the level decomposition

    Every pair $\{x,y\}\subset\Lambda_n$ lies in a unique pair of children, since the children partition $\Lambda_n$. If $x,y$ lie in the same child, the child is a coset $e_n(a)+\Lambda_{n-1}$ and levels are translation invariant, so the pair's level is at most $n-1$; the term it contributes is one of the pairs summed by $H_{\Lambda^{(a)}}$, and conversely every pair of a child is a pair of $\Lambda_n$ with the same level. If $x,y$ lie in different children, the pair has level exactly $n$ by the child decomposition, so it contributes $J_n\sigma_x\sigma_y$, and summing over the pairs of the two children gives $J_nS_aS_{a'}$. No pair of $\Lambda_n$ lies at a level above $n$, because every vertex of $\Lambda_n$ vanishes above coordinate $n$ and a difference of two of them therefore does. Collecting the terms of level $k$ gives the second display.

    The regrouping shows where the exactness comes from: the interaction between two children is a function of their two sums, and the interaction between a child and all the others is a function of its sum as well, so a child's influence on the rest of the block is carried by one number.

    $$\mathcal T_b\mu(A')=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb R^m}\mathbf 1_{A'}\Bigl(\sum_{a}s_a\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(b\sum_{a

    for $b\geq0$, the block-spin laws satisfy $\mu_n=\mathcal T_{\beta J_n}\mu_{n-1}$.

    Proof of the decimation identity

    Write $\sigma=(\sigma^{(a)})_{a\in A}$ with $\sigma^{(a)}$ the restriction to the child $\Lambda^{(a)}$, and $S_a=\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{(a)}}\sigma^{(a)}_x$. The prior is a product over vertices, hence over children, and the level decomposition gives

    $$\exp(-\beta H_{\Lambda_n}(\sigma))=\prod_{a\in A}\exp\bigl(-\beta H_{\Lambda^{(a)}}(\sigma^{(a)})\bigr)\exp\Bigl(\beta J_n\sum_{a

    Because a child is a translate of $\Lambda_{n-1}$ and the Hamiltonian only depends on differences, the integral of the $a$-th factor against the prior over that child is $Z_{n-1}$ on the set where $S_a=s_a$, as the definition of the Hamiltonian gives the same function of the translated configuration. Integrating out all children with $S_a$ held fixed therefore produces $\prod_aZ_{n-1}\mu_{n-1}(ds_a)$, and integrating the remaining factor over $(s_a)$ gives the displayed identity for $Z_n$. Dividing by $Z_n$ gives the law of $(S_a)$, and the law of $\sum_as_a$ is its image.

    In this form the identity is the renormalisation transformation of Bleher and Sinai, who made the law of the block spin the object of study [@bleher-sinai-1973].

    The display is the whole renormalisation group, at finite volume: the state of the model after $n$ scales is the law of one number, and one more scale acts on that law by a fixed rule depending on the coupling $b=\beta J_n$. Three features are immediate. First, $\mathcal T_b$ is a nonlinear but explicit operation, given by a Gibbs tilt of a product measure by the variance-type quantity $\sum_{a<a'}s_as_{a'}=\tfrac12\bigl((\sum_as_a)^2-\sum_as_a^2\bigr)$; second, the coupling argument decreases geometrically, $J_n=L^{-(d+\alpha)n}J$; third, the identity holds for any prior $\varrho$, so it applies to Ising, Gaussian and $\varphi^4$ spins at once, differing only in the initial law $\mu_0$.

    Iteration and the free energy

    For $1\leq k\leq n$, $\mu_n=\mathcal T_{\beta J_n}\cdots\mathcal T_{\beta J_{k+1}}\mu_k$. With $F_n=m^{-n}\log Z_n$,

    $$F_n=F_0+\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{1}{m^k}\log\mathbb E_{\mu_{k-1}^{\otimes m}}\Bigl[\exp\Bigl(\beta J_k\sum_{a

    If the prior satisfies $\lvert\sigma\rvert\leq1$ and $J_k>0$ for every $k$, then $F_n$ is nondecreasing in $n$ and

    $$F_0\leq F_n\leq F_0+\tfrac{\beta}{2}\sum_{x\neq0}J\lVert x\rVert^{-d-\alpha}$$

    for every $n$, so $F=\lim_nF_n$ exists and is finite; $F$ is convex in $\beta$.

    Proof of the free energy recursion

    The iterate is the decimation identity applied $n-k$ times. Taking logarithms in the identity for $Z_n$ and dividing by $m^n=m\cdot m^{n-1}$ gives $F_n=F_{n-1}+m^{-n}\Lambda_n$ with $\Lambda_n$ the logarithm in the display, and iterating this relation gives the stated formula.

    For the monotonicity, apply the second Griffiths inequality [@griffiths-1967] to the ferromagnetic measure $\mathbb P_{k-1}^{\otimes m}$, whose couplings between distinct children are all zero: it gives $\mathbb E[s_as_{a'}]\geq0$ for $a\neq a'$. Hence $\mathbb E[\sum_{a<a'}s_as_{a'}]\geq0$, and Jensen's inequality $\log\mathbb E[e^X]\geq\mathbb E[X]$ gives $\Lambda_k\geq0$, so $F_n-F_{n-1}\geq0$.

    For the upper bound, $\lvert\sigma_x\sigma_y\rvert\leq1$ gives $\exp(-\beta H_{\Lambda_n})\leq\exp(\beta\sum_{\{x,y\}\subset\Lambda_n}J(\lVert x-y\rVert))$, so $Z_n\leq(\int\varrho)^{m^n}\exp(\beta\sum_{\{x,y\}}J(\lVert x-y\rVert))$ and $F_n\leq F_0+\beta m^{-n}\sum_{\{x,y\}\subset\Lambda_n}J(\lVert x-y\rVert)$. Each vertex of $\Lambda_n$ has at most $\sum_{x\neq0}J(\lVert x\rVert)$ total intensity to its partners, so the double sum is at most $m^n\sum_{x\neq0}J(\lVert x\rVert)/2$, which is finite by the summability criterion. Convexity follows because $\beta\mapsto\log Z_n(\beta)$ has second derivative the variance of $H_{\Lambda_n}$.

    For the Ising prior, $F_0=\log2$, and the bounds read $\log2\leq F_n\leq\log2+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{x\neq0}J(\lVert x\rVert)$. The recursion is the useful statement: the increment at scale $k$ is a function of the level-$(k-1)$ block-spin law alone, so the free energy is a sum of per-scale contributions, each computed from the renormalised state at that scale. This is the form in which the free energy is used in the article on exponents.

    Correlations of block sums

    The derivative of the free energy in a level coupling identifies a block correlation, and this is the observable the renormalisation group controls.

    For $k\geq1$ let $T_k=\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_k}[S_1S_2]$, where $S_1$ and $S_2$ are the sums over two distinct children of $\Lambda_k$; by the symmetry of the model on $\Lambda_k$ this does not depend on which two children are chosen. Then

    $$\frac{\partial}{\partial J_k}\log Z_k(\beta)=\beta\,\frac{m(m-1)}{2}\,T_k,\qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial J_k}\log Z_n(\beta)=\beta\,\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_n}[Q_k]\quad(n\geq k).$$
    Proof of the derivative identity

    Differentiating $Z_n=\int\exp(\beta\sum_{j\leq n}J_jQ_j)\prod_{x\in\Lambda_n}\varrho(d\sigma_x)$ in $J_k$ brings down the factor $\beta Q_k$, so the derivative is $\beta\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_n}[Q_k]$, which is the second identity.

    For $n=k$ the only level-$k$ block in $\Lambda_k$ is $\Lambda_k$ itself, so $Q_k$ is the sum of $S_aS_{a'}$ over the unordered pairs of distinct children, $m(m-1)/2$ terms. An isometry of $\mathbb H^d_L$ that fixes $0$ and preserves $\Lambda_k$ permutes its children, and it preserves the Hamiltonian because the Hamiltonian depends on distances, hence preserves $\mathbb P_k$; so every term has the same expectation $T_k$, and the first identity follows.

    Two cautions attach to this identity. The derivative is taken in a coupling that is present in the model, so it computes the block-sum correlation at the same intensity and not at a shifted one; and the second identity is a statement about $\mathbb P_n$, in which the spins of a level-$k$ block also interact with the rest of $\Lambda_n$, so it does not reduce to $T_k$ times the number of blocks. What the identity controls is the correlation of child sums, not of individual spins. For the Gaussian prior the two are related by a linear factor, which is how the exact solution obtains its two-point function; for other priors individual-spin correlations require separate arguments.

    Examples and non-examples

    Ising spins. With $\varrho=\tfrac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)$, each $s_a$ is an integer between $-m^{n-1}$ and $m^{n-1}$, and $\mu_0$ is uniform on $\{\pm1\}$. The decimation identity reproduces the standard block-spin recursion of the Dyson model: the level-$n$ measure is $m$ independent copies of the level-$(n-1)$ measure, reweighted by a ferromagnetic coupling between their sums.

    Gaussian spins. With $\varrho=N(0,1)$ the same identity holds, and both the tilt and the pushforward preserve Gaussianity, so the recursion closes on the variance. This is the Gaussian renormalisation group, and it is the only case in which the recursion is solved in closed form.

    A kernel that is not constant on levels. If $J$ depends on the pair rather than on the level, the first display fails: the interaction between two children is then a sum of terms with different intensities, and the block's energy depends on the internal arrangement of spins, not only on the sums. The Euclidean obstruction is of this kind but worse, since there the geometry of the blocks also changes with scale.

    Periodic boundary conditions. With the torus $\mathbb Z^d$ replacing $\mathbb H^d_L$ there is no ultrametric and no analogue of the level decomposition. Within the hierarchical lattice one can impose a "periodic" condition by identifying $\Lambda_n$ with a quotient of $\mathbb H^d_L$, but the resulting model is a finite graph whose blocks at the top have no children above them, and the identity then describes one decimation step only.

    Variants and generalisations

    External field. Adding $-h\sum_x\sigma_x$ to the Hamiltonian multiplies the exponent of the decimation identity by $e^{h\sum_as_a}$, so the recursion becomes one for the law of the block spin with a field argument: $\mu_n=\mathcal T_{\beta J_n,h}\mu_{n-1}$. The field is relevant at the level of single spins and must be rescaled together with the spin.

    Other priors. A $\varphi^4$ prior $e^{-V(\sigma)}d\sigma$ with $V$ a quartic polynomial gives the same identity with $\mu_0$ not Gaussian, and the recursion no longer closes: it generates an infinite-dimensional family of laws. Controlling that family is the content of the Bleher–Sinai analysis.

    Infinite volume. The measures $\mathbb P_n$ are not consistent under restriction, because a restricted Gibbs measure is not Gibbsian for the restricted Hamiltonian; the infinite-volume limit requires a boundary condition and a compactness argument. Every identity above is finite-volume and exact, which is what the renormalisation group needs; passing to the limit is a separate step, taken in the article on the infinite-volume limit, which uses the boundary field of a block and produces a Gibbs measure with a radial two-point function.

    History and attribution

    Dyson (1969) introduced the block structure and the coupling $J_k$ decaying as a power to exhibit a phase transition in one dimension [@dyson-1969]. Bleher and Sinai (1973) made the block-spin law the object of study and wrote the recursion of this article as a nonlinear transformation of probability measures [@bleher-sinai-1973]; the presentation in terms of a level decomposition of the Hamiltonian is the one used in the modern renormalisation-group treatment of lattice field theory [@bauerschmidt-brydges-slade-2019]. The positivity of the block-sum correlations used for the free energy bound is a case of Griffiths's correlation inequalities for ferromagnetic systems [@griffiths-1967].

    The infinite-volume limit

    Finite-volume measures, the boundary field of a block, and which limits are Gibbs measures.

    theorem · separate page

    Every identity in the decimation article is finite-volume and exact, and that is deliberate: the renormalisation group acts on the law of a block spin of a block, and a block never needs the lattice. But the objects the rest of the wiki measures are not finite-volume objects. The two-point function $\tau_\beta(x,y)$ of the exponent article, the phase that the intensity selects, and the statement that $\tau_\beta$ depends on the level alone are all statements about a measure on the whole lattice, and the lattice is infinite.

    This article supplies the passage. Three things are proved and nothing more is assumed: that the finite-volume measures have limit points, that every limit point satisfies the defining property of a Gibbs measure — the conditional law of a finite region given the rest of the configuration, which is the one condition that survives the limit — and that a limit point of the free-boundary measures inherits the lattice symmetry, so its two-point function is radial. What the limit is, whether it is unique, and whether the model is magnetised are questions of a different order: the last section of this article says which of them this argument does not touch, and the next one answers them for ferromagnetic couplings, in the form of the sign of a single number.

    The passage needs one arithmetic input, and it is the reason the article belongs to the first part. The coupling from a block to its complement, summed over the complement, is the same number for every vertex of the block — the lattice gives each vertex of $\Lambda_n$ exactly the same view of the outside — and that number decays like $L^{-\alpha n}$ while its sum over the block grows or decays according to $\alpha-d$. The first fact is what makes the specification continuous; the second says when the boundary is thermodynamically large.

    The specification

    Spins are attached to the vertices of $\mathbb H^d_L$, so a configuration is a point $\sigma=(\sigma_x)_{x\in\mathbb H^d_L}$ of $\Omega=\{\pm1\}^{\mathbb H^d_L}$, and $\sigma_x$ is the coordinate map. For a finite set $A$ let $\mathcal F_A$ be the $\sigma$-algebra generated by $\{\sigma_x:x\in A\}$; a function is local if it is bounded and $\mathcal F_A$-measurable for some finite $A$. The Hamiltonian $H_\Lambda$ and the prior $\varrho$ are those of the decimation article.

    A boundary field on a finite $\Lambda$ is a function $h:\Lambda\to\mathbb R$, and the finite-volume measure with boundary field $h$ is

    $$\mathbb P_\Lambda^h(d\sigma)=\frac{1}{Z_\Lambda(h)}\exp\Bigl(-\beta H_\Lambda(\sigma)+\beta\sum_{x\in\Lambda}h_x\sigma_x\Bigr)\prod_{x\in\Lambda}\varrho(d\sigma_x).$$

    Two cases are used. The free boundary condition is $h\equiv0$, which is the measure $\mathbb P_\Lambda$ of the decimation article. A fixed boundary condition is a configuration $\omega$, and the field it induces is $h_x=\sum_{y\notin\Lambda}J(x-y)\omega_y$; the resulting measure is the model on $\Lambda$ with the outside held at $\omega$, since the omitted energy is $-\sum_{x\in\Lambda,y\notin\Lambda}J(x-y)\sigma_x\omega_y$ and is linear in $\sigma$ on $\Lambda$. For finite $A\subset\Lambda$ and a field $h$ on $A$ write $\gamma_A^h$ for the law of $\sigma_A$ with density $\exp(-\beta H_A(\sigma_A)+\beta\sum_{x\in A}h_x\sigma_x)$ against the prior: the Gibbs conditional of $A$ in the field $h$. With $h_A(\omega)_x=\sum_{y\notin A}J(x-y)\omega_y$, a probability measure $\mu$ on $\Omega$ is a Gibbs measure if

    $$\mathbb E_\mu\bigl[f\mid\mathcal F_{A^c}\bigr](\omega)=\int f\,d\gamma_A^{h_A(\omega)}\qquad\mu\text{-a.e.}$$

    for every finite $A$ and every bounded local $f$ on $A$.

    The sums defining $h_A(\omega)$ converge absolutely because $\omega$ is bounded and $\sum_{y}\lvert J(x-y)\rvert<\infty$, which is the summability criterion of the lattice article; without it the conditional law in the definition would not exist, and the last section of examples is built on that. The condition is the one of Dobrushin and of Lanford and Ruelle, and it says that the measure is compatible with all of its own one-region conditional laws at once [@dobrushin-1968] [@lanford-ruelle-1969]. Nothing in it refers to a volume or to a boundary, which is why it is the right notion for a limit.

    The boundary field of a block

    The hierarchical lattice makes one computation exact, because a block is a subgroup: every vertex of $\Lambda_n$ sees the complement in the same way.

    Let $n\geq0$ and $x\in\Lambda_n$. Then

    $$B_n:=\sum_{y\notin\Lambda_n}\lvert J(x-y)\rvert=J(1-L^{-d})\frac{L^{-\alpha(n+1)}}{1-L^{-\alpha}},$$

    independently of $x\in\Lambda_n$, and in particular $B_n\to0$. The total coupling between $\Lambda_n$ and its complement is $\lvert\Lambda_n\rvert B_n$, and

    $$\lvert\Lambda_n\rvert B_n=\frac{J(1-L^{-d})L^{-\alpha}}{1-L^{-\alpha}}\,L^{(d-\alpha)n}\ \longrightarrow\ \begin{cases}0,&\alpha>d,\\ \infty,&\alpha
    Proof

    Let $x\in\Lambda_n$ and $y\notin\Lambda_n$, so that $x_i=0$ for $i>n$ and $y$ has some coordinate above $n$ different from $0$. Then $x-y$ vanishes above the largest index $m$ with $y_m\neq0$, and does not vanish there, so $\operatorname{lev}(x-y)=\operatorname{lev}(y)$: the level of the pair is the level of $y$ as a vertex, exactly when $x$ is in the block below $y$. Hence $J(x-y)=J_{\operatorname{lev}(y)}$, and summing over the shell at level $j$ gives

    $$B_n=\sum_{j\geq n+1}\bigl(L^{dj}-L^{d(j-1)}\bigr)JL^{-(d+\alpha)j}=J(1-L^{-d})\sum_{j\geq n+1}L^{-\alpha j}=\frac{J(1-L^{-d})L^{-\alpha(n+1)}}{1-L^{-\alpha}},$$

    using the shell counts of the lattice article and the geometric series, which converges because $\alpha>0$. The total coupling is $\lvert\Lambda_n\rvert B_n$ with $\lvert\Lambda_n\rvert=L^{dn}$, and $L^{dn}L^{-\alpha(n+1)}=L^{-\alpha}L^{(d-\alpha)n}$ gives the dichotomy.

    Both statements are about the same number, and they pull in opposite directions. Per vertex the boundary is negligible for every $\alpha>0$: that is the hypothesis the Gibbs property needs, and it holds in the whole range of the model. The block totals it, and the cross energy $-\sum_{x\in\Lambda_n,y\notin\Lambda_n}J(x-y)\sigma_x\sigma_y$ has modulus at most $\lvert\Lambda_n\rvert B_n$ with each pair counted once from the block's side, so $\beta\lvert\Lambda_n\rvert B_n$ — the largest exponent the boundary can contribute — diverges exactly when $\alpha<d$. So in the long-range range the boundary of a block carries unbounded energy while no single vertex feels more than $B_n$, and that is the precise sense in which the model is long-range: not that a spin is strongly coupled to the outside, but that there are too many outside spins. For $\alpha>d$ the cross energy stays bounded, and the boundary is a finite-volume effect.

    Neither case obstructs the limit. The next two sections take the limit with only $B_n\to0$, and the dichotomy is recorded because it is what distinguishes the two ranges of the model, and because the marginal value $\alpha=d$ — the value at which the cross energy neither vanishes nor diverges — is where the model's own criteria become delicate, as the vector article records.

    The conditional law is continuous in the boundary

    Everything below is a consequence of one Lipschitz estimate, which says that changing the boundary field changes the conditional law of a finite region by at most the size of the change.

    Let $A$ be finite, let $h,h'$ be fields on $A$, and let $c=\beta\sum_{x\in A}\lvert h_x-h'_x\rvert$. Then the laws $\gamma_A^h$ and $\gamma_A^{h'}$ satisfy $\lVert\gamma_A^h-\gamma_A^{h'}\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\tanh(c/2)\leq c/2$, and consequently for every bounded local $f$ on $A$

    $$\Bigl\lvert\int f\,d\gamma_A^h-\int f\,d\gamma_A^{h'}\Bigr\rvert\leq 2\lVert f\rVert_\infty\tanh(c/2)\leq\beta\lVert f\rVert_\infty\sum_{x\in A}\lvert h_x-h'_x\rvert.$$

    Two consequences are used. First, $\omega\mapsto\gamma_A^{h_A(\omega)}$ is continuous in the product topology: each $h_A(\omega)_x=\sum_{y\notin A}J(x-y)\omega_y$ is a uniform limit of functions of finitely many coordinates, hence continuous, because $\sum_y\lvert J(x-y)\rvert<\infty$ and $\lvert\omega\rvert\leq1$. Second, for $A\subset\Lambda_n$ the conditional of the free-boundary measure differs from the infinite-volume conditional by at most

    $$\Bigl\lVert\gamma_A^{h_{A|\Lambda_n}(\sigma)}-\gamma_A^{h_A(\sigma)}\Bigr\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\tanh\Bigl(\tfrac12\beta\lvert A\rvert B_n\Bigr),$$

    where $h_{A|\Lambda_n}(x)=\sum_{y\in\Lambda_n\setminus A}J(x-y)\sigma_y$ uses only the spins inside the block, as the free boundary condition prescribes.

    Proof

    Write $\Lambda=A$. The two densities against the prior differ only by the factor $\exp\bigl(\beta\sum_{x\in A}\sigma_x(h_x-h'_x)\bigr)$, which lies in $[e^{-c},e^{c}]$ because $\lvert\sigma_x\rvert\leq1$. If two probability densities have ratio everywhere in $[a,b]$, their total variation distance is at most $(b-a)/(b+a)$: the positive part of the difference is at most $\tfrac12\int p\,(b-a)/(b+a)$ in the normalised form, which is the bound. With $b/a=e^{c}$ this is $\tanh(c/2)$, and $\tanh u\leq u$ since $(\tanh u)'=\operatorname{sech}^2u\leq1$ and $\tanh0=0$. Expectations differ by at most $2\lVert f\rVert_\infty$ times the total variation distance.

    For the second consequence, the two fields on $A$ agree on the contribution of $y\in\Lambda_n\setminus A$ and differ only in the contribution of $y\notin\Lambda_n$, which is at most $B_n$ in modulus at each $x\in A$ by the boundary-field proposition. So $c\leq\beta\lvert A\rvert B_n$ and the estimate applies.

    For the continuity, fix $x$ and $N$; the partial sum $\sum_{\lVert y\rVert\leq L^N}J(x-y)\omega_y$ depends on finitely many coordinates, and the remainder is at most $\sum_{\lVert y\rVert>L^N}\lvert J(x-y)\rvert\to0$ uniformly in $\omega$. A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, and a finite tuple of continuous fields gives a conditional law that depends continuously on them by the estimate above, so $\omega\mapsto\gamma_A^{h_A(\omega)}$ is continuous.

    The estimate is quantitative where it matters: the error in the conditional is of order $\beta\lvert A\rvert B_n$, so it is small uniformly in the configuration and does not degrade as the volume grows. That uniformity is the whole content of the theorem below, and it is what fails when the interaction is not summable.

    Existence, and the Gibbs property

    Let $\alpha>0$, let $\lvert\sigma_x\rvert\leq1$, and let $(\Lambda_n)_{n\geq0}$ be the exhausting sequence of blocks. Then:

    (i) the set of limit points in the weak topology of the free-boundary measures $(\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n})_{n\geq0}$ is nonempty, and every limit point is a Gibbs measure;

    (ii) for any fixed configuration $\omega$, every limit point of the measures $(\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{\omega})_{n\geq0}$ with boundary condition $\omega$ is a Gibbs measure.

    Proof

    Compactness. $\Omega=\{\pm1\}^{\mathbb H^d_L}$ is a product of compact spaces, hence compact, and metrisable because the index set is countable; its Borel probability measures are weakly compact. So every sequence has a limit point, and the set of limit points of a sequence is nonempty. This is where the boundedness of the spins enters: for $\varrho=N(0,1)$ the space is not compact and tightness has to be supplied separately.

    Conditioning. Let $f$ be bounded and local on a finite $A$, and $g$ bounded and local on $A^c$; both are continuous. For $n$ with $A\subset\Lambda_n$, the tower property in the finite volume gives

    $$\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{\omega}}[fg]=\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{\omega}}\bigl[\gamma_{A|{\Lambda_n}}(f)\,g\bigr],$$

    where $\gamma_{A|{\Lambda_n}}(f)(\sigma)=\int f\,d\gamma_A^{h}$ with the field $h_x=\sum_{y\in\Lambda_n\setminus A}J(x-y)\sigma_y$ on the spins of the block outside $A$.

    Case (ii). With the fixed boundary condition $\omega$, the spins outside $\Lambda_n$ are $\omega$, so $h_x=\sum_{y\in\Lambda_n\setminus A}J(x-y)\sigma_y+\sum_{y\notin\Lambda_n}J(x-y)\omega_y$ and therefore $\gamma_{A|{\Lambda_n}}(f)=\gamma_A^{h_A(\cdot)}(f)$ exactly, as functions on $\Omega$: the two fields are the same field. This function is continuous and bounded by $\lVert f\rVert_\infty$, so along any weakly convergent subsequence $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_{n_k}}^{\omega}\to\mu$ both sides converge, giving $\mathbb E_\mu[fg]=\mathbb E_\mu[g\,\gamma_A^{h_A}(f)]$ for all such $f,g$, which is the Gibbs property.

    Case (i). Here the block outside $A$ is free, so the field of the conditional is $h_{A|\Lambda_n}$ and the second consequence of the continuity proposition applies: $\gamma_{A|{\Lambda_n}}(f)\to\gamma_A^{h_A}(f)$ uniformly, with error at most $2\lVert f\rVert_\infty\tanh(\beta\lvert A\rvert B_n/2)\to0$. Each $\gamma_{A|{\Lambda_n}}(f)$ is a function of finitely many spins, hence continuous, and bounded by $\lVert f\rVert_\infty$. So for a weakly convergent subsequence $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_{n_k}}\to\mu$, the left side tends to $\mathbb E_\mu[fg]$ and the right side to $\mathbb E_\mu[g\,\gamma_A^{h_A}(f)]$, and the two are equal. Since $f$ and $A$ were arbitrary and the local functions on $A^c$ generate $\mathcal F_{A^c}$, this is the Gibbs property.

    Note where the argument cannot be shortcut: $\gamma_A^{h_A}(f)$ depends on the whole outside configuration and is not continuous, so it cannot be tested directly against a weak limit. The proof passes through the finite-volume conditionals, which are continuous because they see finitely many spins, and uses the uniformity of their convergence to replace them.

    Two remarks on the statement. The measures in (ii) with different $\omega$ have different limits in general, and the theorem asserts only that each is Gibbs; the Gibbs property constrains the conditional laws of a measure and does not compare two measures. And the proof of (i) uses nothing about $\Lambda_n$ beyond the fact that the blocks exhaust the lattice and that the coupling of $\Lambda_n$ to its complement decays at each vertex, so it applies to any exhaustion of any block system in which each vertex has finite total intensity — which is the form in which the general article states the lattice.

    The limit is symmetric

    The finite-volume measures are not invariant under anything, since a block has a boundary, but a limit point is, because every symmetry of the lattice eventually preserves every block.

    Let $\mu$ be a limit point of $(\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n})_{n\geq0}$. Then $\mu$ is invariant under every translation of $\mathbb H^d_L$ and under every coordinate-wise bijection, and consequently

    $$\tau_\mu(x,y):=\mathbb E_\mu[\sigma_x\sigma_y]=\tau_\mu(0,y-x),$$

    depends on $\operatorname{lev}(y-x)$ alone. Moreover the block sums of the children of any block are exchangeable under $\mu$.

    Proof

    Let $\psi$ be a translation by $v\in\mathbb H^d_L$ or a coordinate-wise bijection. For $n\geq\operatorname{lev}(v)$ in the first case and every $n$ in the second, $\psi$ maps $\Lambda_n$ onto itself, and it preserves the level of every pair, so it preserves the Hamiltonian $H_{\Lambda_n}$ and the prior; hence $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}$ is invariant under $\psi$. For a bounded local $f$ and a weakly convergent subsequence $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_{n_k}}\to\mu$,

    $$\mathbb E_\mu[f]=\lim_k\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_{n_k}}}[f]=\lim_k\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_{n_k}}}[f\circ\psi]=\mathbb E_\mu[f\circ\psi],$$

    because $n_k\to\infty$ and the invariance holds from some index on. So $\mu$ is invariant under both families. Invariance under the translations gives $\tau_\mu(x,y)=\tau_\mu(0,y-x)$, and invariance under the coordinate-wise bijections, together with the transitivity of the isometry theorem on pairs at a fixed level, gives that $\tau_\mu(0,z)$ depends only on $\operatorname{lev}(z)$. Exchangeability of the child sums is statement (ii) of the same article, applied to $\mu$.

    This is the statement the rest of the wiki needs. The exponent article passes from the susceptibility, an average over a shell, to the pointwise two-point function, and it does so for a measure that is known to be radial; the corollary is what makes the free-boundary limit such a measure. It is not a statement about every Gibbs measure: a Gibbs measure selected by a magnetised boundary condition is not translation invariant in the same way, and the corollary is about the symmetric limit, which is the one the recursion produces.

    Examples and non-examples

    No interaction. For $J=0$ the finite-volume measures are the product of the prior, and $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}=\varrho^{\otimes\Lambda_n}$ has the product measure $\varrho^{\otimes\mathbb H^d_L}$ as its limit; the Gibbs property holds with $\gamma_A$ the product law, and the limit is invariant. This is the only case in which the limit is read off without an argument.

    A kernel that is not summable at a vertex. Take $\alpha=0$, that is $J_k=L^{-dk}$, so that each vertex is coupled to every level at the same total rate. Then the sum over the $j$-th shell is $(1-L^{-d})J$ for every $j$, the boundary field $B_n$ diverges with $n$, and the series defining $h_A(\omega)$ diverges. The energy of a configuration is not defined, no conditional law exists, and the model is not a Gibbs system at all. The hypothesis $\alpha>0$ is therefore not technical: it is exactly summability at a vertex, and it fails at $\alpha=0$ and at no other value of the exponent of this family.

    The long-range range is not a non-example. For $0<\alphathe boundary-field proposition. The two facts are compatible because they are about different quantities: the theorem needs the per-vertex field to vanish, and the divergence is a statement about how many vertices there are. This is the resolution of the gap that the decimation article left open.

    Gaussian spins. For $\varrho=N(0,1)$ the spins are unbounded and neither half of the theorem applies as stated. Compactness becomes tightness: if $\sup_n\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}}[\sigma_0^2]<\infty$ then the sequence is tight and limit points exist; the conditional law is still Lipschitz in the field on the set where the outside configuration is bounded, which is where the uniform convergence of the fields holds, so the Gibbs property follows on that set and hence everywhere under the tightness assumption. Both statements are assumptions here and are not proved in this article: what is proved is the bounded-spin case, and what is needed for the unbounded one is stated.

    Not a Gibbs system: percolation. Bond percolation on the same lattice is not a spin system and has no boundary field of this kind: the state of a bond is not the sum of local energies, and the object that plays the role of the specification is the connectivity event. The percolation article takes the infinite-volume limit of the connection probabilities directly, which is a different construction, and the two should not be conflated.

    Variants and generalisations

    The set of Gibbs measures. It is convex, because the Gibbs property is an identity between integrals that is preserved by convex combinations, and it is closed in the weak topology: if $\mu_k\to\mu$ and each $\mu_k$ is Gibbs, then for bounded local $f$ on $A$ and $g$ on $A^c$ the identity $\mathbb E_{\mu_k}[fg]=\mathbb E_{\mu_k}[g\,\gamma_A^{h_A}(f)]$ passes to the limit, since $g\,\gamma_A^{h_A}(f)$ is continuous and bounded. In the compact case it is therefore a nonempty compact convex set, and a Gibbs measure that is not its only point is a phase: convex combinations of two distinct Gibbs measures are again Gibbs, so the set of phases is the set of extreme points.

    An external field. Adding $-h\sum_x\sigma_x$ to the Hamiltonian adds $\beta h$ to each field $h_x$ in the specification, and the boundary-field computation is unchanged since the field is constant. The triviality of this modification is the reason the recursion treats the field as a separate argument in the map article.

    Other block systems. Nothing in the argument uses more than that the blocks exhaust the vertex set and that $\sum_y\lvert J(x-y)\rvert<\infty$; the exact formula for $B_n$ uses that every vertex of a block has the same view of the complement, which is a property of any block system whose blocks are cosets of nested subgroups. On $\mathbb Z^d$ with a finite-range interaction the same theorem holds and is classical; on $\mathbb Z^d$ with a power-law interaction the per-vertex sum converges exactly when the exponent exceeds the dimension, which is the condition under which the model is a Gibbs system at all.

    What the limit does to the recursion. The decimation identity is a statement about $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}$ and holds at every $n$; a limit point $\mu$ does not inherit it as an identity, because a block of an infinite lattice has a complement to be coupled to. What $\mu$ does inherit is the symmetry of the corollary, and the laws of the block sums $S_{\Lambda_n}$ under $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}$ — the finite-volume objects of the recursion — converge whenever the measures do, along the same subsequence. The recursion is thus the right object at finite volume, and the limit is the right object for the exponents, and the two are connected by the fact that the exponents are read off the growth of the $S_{\Lambda_n}$ precisely along this limit.

    History and attribution

    The formulation of a Gibbs measure by its conditional laws is due to Dobrushin [@dobrushin-1968] and to Lanford and Ruelle [@lanford-ruelle-1969], and the general theory, including the statement that limit points of finite-volume measures are Gibbs measures when the specification is continuous, is the content of the first chapters of Georgii's book [@georgii-2011]. The hierarchical model was introduced by Dyson and analysed by Bleher and Sinai, who work with the finite-volume measures and the recursion and identify the critical point as the boundary between two domains of attraction [@bleher-sinai-1973]; the passage to the infinite volume in that literature is made with the compactness argument of this article and is not usually separated from the analysis that follows it.

    The computation of the boundary field is elementary and is the same count as the summability criterion for the kernel; the reason to record it separately is that it separates two conditions that are easy to confuse. Summability at a vertex, which holds for every $\alpha>0$, is what the definition of a Gibbs measure needs; the growth rate $\lvert\Lambda_n\rvert B_n\asymp L^{(d-\alpha)n}$ is what decides whether the boundary is thermodynamically relevant, and it is the arithmetic behind the distinction between the mean-field and non-mean-field ranges in the rest of the wiki. The corollary on invariance is the infinite-volume form of the symmetry statements of the isometry article.

    Ultrametric block systems and the p-adic models

    What the recursion actually needs, and how little of it is specific to the hierarchical lattice.

    model · separate page

    Every proof in this wiki about decimation uses exactly two facts: that the blocks of a fixed level are the cosets of a subgroup, and that each block has the same number $m$ of children. Nothing else about the hierarchical lattice is used: not the direct-sum construction, not countability, not the arithmetic of $(\mathbb Z/L\mathbb Z)^d$. This article states the construction for an arbitrary abelian group with a nested block system and proves the two structural results in that generality; the decimation identity, the map and the exponent dictionary then apply verbatim.

    The standard compact example is the ring of p-adic integers $\mathbb Z_p$ with the balls of the p-adic metric, which are the cosets of $p^n\mathbb Z_p$: each ball has exactly $p$ children, at every scale, forever. The models on $\mathbb Z_p$ are used in the renormalisation-group approach to conformal field theory, where the exactness of the block structure is worth the loss of translation invariance in the Euclidean sense [@abdesselam-2018].

    The one point at which the two settings differ is the meaning of the volume of a block: a coset of $\Lambda_n$ in the hierarchical lattice is a finite set and its volume is its cardinality, while a ball in $\mathbb Z_p$ is uncountable and its volume is its Haar measure. The recursion sees only the ratio of the volumes of a block and its child, which is $m$ in both cases, so the two models have the same renormalisation group.

    Definitions and setting

    Let $G$ be an abelian group and $G=G_0\supset G_1\supset G_2\supset\cdots$ a decreasing sequence of subgroups, each of finite index in $G$, with

    $$[G_n:G_{n+1}]=m\qquad\text{for every }n\geq0 .$$

    The level of a pair $x\neq y$ is the least $n\geq1$ with $x-y\in G_n$, and $\operatorname{lev}(x)=0$ only for $x=0$. The block at $x$ of level $n$ is the coset $x+G_n$; the children of a block $x+G_n$ are its cosets of $G_{n+1}$. Vertices at level $n$ are those in $G_n\setminus G_{n-1}$, and the shell at level $n$ is $G_n\setminus G_{n-1}$.

    Two examples carry the subject. For the hierarchical lattice $\mathbb H^d_L$ one takes $G_n=\Lambda_n$, the sequences supported on the first $n$ coordinates, and $m=L^d$; the blocks are finite and the volumes are cardinalities $m^n$. For the p-adic integers one takes $G=\mathbb Z_p$ and $G_n=p^n\mathbb Z_p$; then $[G_n:G_{n+1}]=p$, the blocks are the p-adic balls of radius $p^{-n}$, and the normalised Haar measure gives them volume $p^{-n}$. In both cases $G_n/G_{n+1}$ has $m$ elements and a block of level $n$ has $m^n$ children of level $0$.

    A model on such a system is a spin system with coupling $J(x-y)=J_k$ for $\operatorname{lev}(x-y)=k$, exactly as in the decimation article; the prior is a fixed probability measure on the single-spin space, and the Gibbs measure on a block is normalised with counting measure or Haar measure according to which case one is in. When $G$ is uncountable, $\mathbb P_\beta$ is a measure on $S^{G}$ for a countable set $S$ of blocks; the statements below are about a block, which is a finite-index subgroup, and are unaffected.

    The child decomposition in general

    The first structural fact was proved for the lattice in the lattice article. Its proof uses only the group axioms.

    Let $n\geq1$. The children of $G_n$ are the $m$ cosets of $G_{n+1}$ in $G_n$, they partition $G_n$, each is a translate of $G_{n+1}$, and if $x,y$ lie in different children of a block $B$ then $\operatorname{lev}(x-y)=\operatorname{lev}(B)$. The same holds with $G_n$ replaced by any coset of it.

    Proof

    Since $G_{n+1}\subseteq G_n$ are subgroups, the cosets of $G_{n+1}$ inside $G_n$ are disjoint and their union is $G_n$; there are $[G_n:G_{n+1}]=m$ of them. A coset $g+G_{n+1}$ with $g\in G_n$ is a translate of $G_{n+1}$. If $x\in g+G_{n+1}$ and $y\in g'+G_{n+1}$ with $g\neq g'$ modulo $G_{n+1}$, then $x-y\in G_n$ because both terms lie in $G_n$, while $x-y\notin G_{n+1}$ because its class in $G_n/G_{n+1}$ is $g-g'\neq0$. Hence the least $k$ with $x-y\in G_k$ is exactly $n$. For a coset $z+G_n$ the same argument applies after translating both points by $-z$, and levels are translation invariant because $G_k$ is a subgroup.

    The proposition is what "the block has no boundary" means algebraically: any two vertices in different children of a block are at the top level of that block, whatever their distance, so a coupling that depends only on the level charges all of them the same rate. In $\mathbb Z_p$ this says that the two balls of radius $p^{-(n+1)}$ inside a ball of radius $p^{-n}$ are joined by edges of one intensity, even though the p-adic distance between a point near the middle of one and a point near the middle of the other is much smaller than $p^{-n}$ — the ultrametric makes every such pair equal.

    The decimation identity in general

    Let $B$ be a block of level $n$ with children $B_1,\ldots,B_m$, let $\mu_{n-1}$ be the law of the total spin of a block of level $n-1$ in the model with couplings $J_1,\ldots,J_{n-1}$ on that block, and let $Z_{n-1}$ be its partition function. Then

    $$Z_B=Z_{n-1}^{\,m}\int\exp\Bigl(\beta J_n\sum_{a<a'}s_as_{a'}\Bigr)\prod_a\mu_{n-1}(ds_a),$$

    and the law of the $m$-tuple of child spins is the normalised version. Consequently the block-spin law satisfies $\mu_n=\mathcal T_{\beta J_n}\mu_{n-1}$, with $\mathcal T_b$ as in the map article, and the rescaled orbit is the orbit of $\mathcal R_{\theta_0,\beta J_1}$ with $\theta_0=\tfrac12(d+\alpha)$.

    Proof

    By the proposition, the pairs of vertices inside $B$ split into the pairs inside the children, at levels at most $n-1$, and the pairs joining two different children, all at level $n$. The energy is therefore the sum of the children's energies plus $\beta J_n\sum_{a<a'}S_aS_{a'}$, where $S_a$ is the total spin of the $a$-th child. A child is a translate of a level-$(n-1)$ block, and the Hamiltonian depends only on differences, so integrating the child at fixed $S_a$ gives $Z_{n-1}\mu_{n-1}(dS_a)$ — the additive constant in the couplings makes no difference. Multiplying over the children and integrating over the normalised measures gives the display. The rescaling step is the computation of the autonomous form, which uses only $2\theta_0=d+\alpha$ and the ratio $J_n/J_{n-1}$.

    Two remarks isolate what the generality does and does not buy. The identity needs the amount of branching to be constant: with $[G_n:G_{n+1}]=m_n$ varying, the tilt has $m_n$ children and the coupling ratio is unchanged, so the map is the same but the composition is no longer a power of one operator, and the exponents of the dictionary no longer follow from a single fixed point. And it needs the coupling to depend only on the level, which in the group setting is the statement that the interaction is invariant under the isometries that preserve the block structure.

    The volume, and where the two cases differ

    For the exponents, the volume of a block enters through the growth exponent $\kappa=2\theta_0-d$ and through the summability criterion. In the discrete case the volume of a level-$n$ block is $m^n$; in the compact case it is $\mu(G_n)=m^{-n}$ for the normalised Haar measure if the measure is chosen so that $\mu(G_0)=1$. The two are reciprocal, and the recipe is the same in both: the number of children is the ratio of the volumes of a block and of its child, and the shell at level $n$ has volume the difference.

    Because of this, a percolation model on $\mathbb Z_p$ with a summable kernel behaves as follows: the number of open edges at a vertex is a sum of independent Bernoulli variables with summable probabilities, so it is finite almost surely, and every cluster is countable. The percolation thresholds and the two-point exponent are the same statements as on the hierarchical lattice, since they are statements about the block system.

    Examples and non-examples

    The p-adic integers. Here $m=p$ for every $p$, so the branching is the prime and cannot be varied while keeping the ring: changing $m$ means changing the prime. The dimension is $d=1$ in the normalisation of this wiki, and the kernel exponent $\alpha$ is a free parameter; the models are the ones in Abdesselam's programme for conformal probability [@abdesselam-2018].

    The hierarchical lattice. Here $m=L^d$ and both the scale and the branching are free, which is why the model is used to draw phase diagrams in $(d,\alpha)$ rather than at one prime.

    Subgroups with varying index. Taking $G_n=p^{n^2}\mathbb Z_p$ gives $[G_n:G_{n+1}]=p^{2n+1}$, so the branching grows; the decimation identity still holds at each scale but the map at scale $n$ depends on $n$, and the model behaves as though the kernel exponent changed with scale. This is a useful non-example: it separates the part of the theory that needs constant branching from the part that does not.

    Non-example: the regular tree. A rooted tree with constant branching is not a group, and its "blocks" have no canonical translates: the children of a vertex are distinguishable by their position, and there is no isometry moving one to another while fixing the structure. The decimation identity holds for the spin model on the tree in the form of a cavity recursion, but the state is a distribution of cavity fields rather than a block spin, and the exponents are not those of a fixed point of a single map.

    Non-example: $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb Z$ with the usual metric. There is no nested system of finite-index subgroups with constant branching, and the analogue of a block has a boundary of codimension one; this is the obstruction of the comparison article in its group-theoretic form.

    Variants and generalisations

    p-adic fields and finite extensions. Replacing $\mathbb Z_p$ by a finite extension of $\mathbb Q_p$ changes the residue field and hence the branching, and multiplies the volume by the ramification index; the recursion is unchanged with the new $m$.

    Products and sums. A product of block systems is a block system with the product branching, which is how the hierarchical lattice is built from copies of $\mathbb Z/L\mathbb Z$; a direct sum of copies is the discrete case of the same construction.

    Vector and percolation models. Nothing in the two proofs refers to the single-spin space, so the vector models and percolation transfer as well, with the branching $m$ replacing $L^d$ throughout.

    History and attribution

    The hierarchical model of Bleher and Sinai was formulated on the lattice of the first article, but their transformation uses only the block structure, as the proof above makes explicit [@bleher-sinai-1973]. The p-adic and ultrametric versions, and their use as a route to a three-dimensional conformal field theory, are developed by Abdesselam [@abdesselam-2018]. The percolation model on the hierarchical lattice is that of Koval, Meester and Trapman [@koval-meester-trapman-2012]; the transfer to an arbitrary block system is the content of this article and is not claimed in those sources. The general framework in which the block structure is one of the data of a renormalisation-group problem is the one of Bauerschmidt, Brydges and Slade [@bauerschmidt-brydges-slade-2019].

    The magnetised phase, and what monotonicity gives

    Two extremal states, the order parameter, and why the FKG inequality cannot locate the threshold.

    theorem · separate page

    The previous article showed that the free-boundary measures have limit points and that these are Gibbs measures, but it said nothing about how many Gibbs measures there are. For a ferromagnetic model the answer has a shape, and the shape costs nothing: the Gibbs measures are stochastically ordered, there is a smallest and a largest, and the model is in one phase exactly when the two coincide. All of that follows from the FKG inequality, which for Ising spins says that increasing functions of the configuration are positively correlated [@fkg-1971], and from the monotone coupling that it implies.

    What it does not follow from is where the transition is. Every inequality below is preserved if the couplings are replaced by any other ferromagnetic system with the same total mass, so no argument of this kind can distinguish the intensities at which the model is ordered — and this is not a gap in the exposition but a feature of monotonicity, which is why the wiki records the split. The threshold is located by the recursion, and the statement that above it the model is magnetised is that of Bleher and Sinai [@bleher-sinai-1973] [@bleher-sinai-1975], recorded here as the one external theorem the section on the threshold relies on.

    The order parameter is the number this article is about. It is defined from the plus state, it is positive exactly when there are two phases, it bounds the correlation from below, and monotonicity gives one weak upper bound on it and no lower one.

    Setting

    Let $J>0$, so that $J_k>0$ for every $k$, and let the prior be the Ising one, so that $\sigma_x\in\{\pm1\}$ and the pointwise order of configurations is the order of the spins. For a finite $\Lambda$ and a configuration $\omega$ let $\mathbb P_\Lambda^{\omega}$ be the finite-volume Gibbs measure with boundary condition $\omega$, as in the specification of the previous article; write $\mathbb P_\Lambda^{+}$ and $\mathbb P_\Lambda^{-}$ for the constant boundary conditions $\omega\equiv+1$ and $\omega\equiv-1$, and $\mu_+,\mu_-$ for the limits of $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}$ and $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{-}$ along the exhausting sequence of blocks, whose existence is proved below.

    A configuration $\omega'$ dominates $\omega$ if $\omega'_x\geq\omega_x$ for every $x$, and a function $f$ is increasing if it is increasing in each spin. For probability measures on $\Omega$, $\nu\preceq\nu'$ means $\int f\,d\nu\leq\int f\,d\nu'$ for every bounded continuous increasing $f$: stochastic domination. Define

    $$M(\beta)=\mathbb E_{\mu_+}[\sigma_0],\qquad C=\sum_{x\neq0}J(\lVert x\rVert)=\frac{J(1-L^{-d})}{L^{\alpha}-1},$$

    so that $C$ is the total coupling mass at a vertex, finite for every $\alpha>0$, and the order parameter is $M(\beta)$, the magnetisation of the plus state at the origin.

    The two facts used throughout are the FKG inequality for the ferromagnetic Ising measures, $\mathbb E[fg]\geq\mathbb E[f]\mathbb E[g]$ for bounded increasing $f$ and $g$ [@fkg-1971] [@griffiths-1967], and its consequence that the measures are monotone in their boundary condition. The second is proved here, because the proof is a coupling and the coupling is what the later arguments run on.

    Monotonicity in the boundary condition

    Let $\Lambda$ be finite and let $\omega\preceq\omega'$. Then $\mathbb P_\Lambda^{\omega}\preceq\mathbb P_\Lambda^{\omega'}$. The same holds with an external field: if $h\leq h'$ then the measure with field $h$ is dominated by the measure with field $h'$.

    Proof by coupling the heat baths

    The heat-bath chain on $\Lambda$ updates one site at a time. Given the configuration $\sigma$ and a uniform $U$, it sets $\sigma_x=+1$ if $U\leq p_x(\sigma)$ and $-1$ otherwise, where

    $$p_x(\sigma)=\frac{1+\tanh\Bigl(\beta\sum_{y\in\Lambda\setminus x}J(x-y)\sigma_y+\beta\sum_{y\notin\Lambda}J(x-y)\omega_y+\beta h_x\Bigr)}{2}$$

    is the conditional probability that $\sigma_x=+1$ given the other spins. Each $p_x$ is increasing in $\sigma$ and in $\omega$ and in $h$, because $J>0$ and $\tanh$ is increasing. The chain is irreducible and aperiodic on the finite state space, so it has a unique invariant law, which is the Gibbs measure $\mathbb P_\Lambda^{\omega}$, and it converges to that law from every starting point.

    Run two chains with the same uniform variables and the same choice of updated site, one for $\omega$ and one for $\omega'$, from starting configurations $\sigma\preceq\sigma'$. The domination is preserved at every update, by the monotonicity of $p_x$: if the two configurations agree at the updated site then so do the new values, and otherwise the one with the larger background has a larger flip probability, so a uniform that flips the lower chain also flips the higher one. Hence the entire path satisfies $\sigma(t)\preceq\sigma'(t)$ for all $t$. Passing to the limit in $t$ gives a coupling of the two invariant laws supported on $\{\sigma\preceq\sigma'\}$, which is domination: for increasing $f$, $\int f\,d\mathbb P_\Lambda^{\omega}=\mathbb E[f(\sigma)]\leq\mathbb E[f(\sigma')]=\int f\,d\mathbb P_\Lambda^{\omega'}$.

    The field case is identical, the field entering $p_x$ the same way as the boundary spin.

    Two consequences are used below. Taking $\omega\preceq\omega'$ to be the extreme configurations gives $\mathbb P_\Lambda^{-}\preceq\mathbb P_\Lambda^{+}$: the plus and minus boundary conditions are the largest and smallest available. Taking instead the boundary conditions produced by the spins outside a smaller block gives the monotonicity in the volume, which is where the limits come from.

    The two extremal states

    The sequence $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}$ is decreasing in $n$ in the stochastic order and $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{-}$ is increasing, so both converge weakly; write $\mu_+$ and $\mu_-$ for the limits. They satisfy $\mu_-\preceq\mu_+$, and every Gibbs measure $\nu$ satisfies $\mu_-\preceq\nu\preceq\mu_+$. Consequently $\mu_+=\mu_-$ if and only if the model has exactly one Gibbs measure, and then it is that measure.

    Proof

    The sequences are monotone. Let $f$ be bounded, continuous and increasing, and local on $\Lambda_n$. Under $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_{n+1}}^{+}$, the conditional law of the spins in $\Lambda_n$ given the rest is $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{\omega}$, where $\omega$ is the configuration $\sigma$ itself, which is $+1$ outside $\Lambda_{n+1}$ and takes the values $\pm1$ in $\Lambda_{n+1}\setminus\Lambda_n$. Every configuration satisfies $\omega\preceq+$, so by the domination proposition $\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{\omega}}[f]\leq\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}}[f]$, and the tower property gives

    $$\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_{n+1}}^{+}}[f]=\mathbb E\Bigl[\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{\omega}}[f]\Bigr]\leq\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}}[f].$$

    So $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_{n+1}}^{+}\preceq\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}$: the plus measure decreases as the block grows, which is what one expects, since a smaller block has the positive boundary closer. The same computation with $\omega\succeq-$ gives that $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{-}$ increases. A monotone sequence of probability measures on a compact space converges, so both limits exist. Since $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{-}\preceq\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}$ for every $n$, the limits satisfy $\mu_-\preceq\mu_+$.

    The sandwich. Let $\nu$ be a Gibbs measure, let $f$ be bounded, continuous and increasing on $\Lambda_n$, and let $\Lambda_m\supset\Lambda_n$. By the Gibbs property, $\nu$ is the average of the conditional laws $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_m}^{\omega}$ against the law of $\omega$ under $\nu$; every $\omega$ satisfies $\omega\preceq+$, so $\mathbb E_\nu[f]\leq\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_m}^{+}}[f]$, and letting $m\to\infty$ gives $\mathbb E_\nu[f]\leq\mathbb E_{\mu_+}[f]$. The other side is the same with $-$.

    Consequences. If $\mu_+=\mu_-$ then every Gibbs measure equals the common value, so it is unique. If the Gibbs measure is unique then $\mu_+$ and $\mu_-$ are both Gibbs measures and hence equal. Both states are Gibbs measures by the existence theorem, applied to the fixed boundary conditions.

    The proposition also settles the question left open by the previous article, in the ferromagnetic case: the set of Gibbs measures is nonempty, and it is an interval in the stochastic order with endpoints that are explicit. That is the whole architecture of the phase diagram, and it was obtained without computing anything.

    The order parameter

    For every $\beta\geq0$, $M(\beta)\geq0$, and $M(\beta)>0$ if and only if $\mu_+\neq\mu_-$, that is, if and only if the hierarchical Ising model has more than one Gibbs measure.

    Proof

    The global flip $\sigma\mapsto-\sigma$ maps $\mathbb P_\Lambda^{+}$ to $\mathbb P_\Lambda^{-}$ and preserves the Hamiltonian, so it maps $\mu_+$ to $\mu_-$. Hence $\mathbb E_{\mu_-}[\sigma_0]=-M(\beta)$; since $\mu_-\preceq\mu_+$ this gives $M\geq-M$, that is $M\geq0$, and $M>0$ gives $\mu_-\neq\mu_+$ at once.

    For the converse, suppose $M=0$. Both $\mu_+$ and $\mu_-$ are translation invariant: $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}$ is invariant under translation by any $v\in\Lambda_n$, because the Hamiltonian, the prior and the constant boundary condition all are, and a limit of invariant measures is invariant. So $\mathbb E_{\mu_+}[\sigma_x]=M=0$ for every $x$, and the same on the minus side.

    Couple the two states monotonically. For each $n$, the proof of the domination proposition gives a coupling of $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{-}$ and $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}$ supported on $\{\sigma\preceq\sigma'\}$. The set of probability measures on the compact space $\Omega\times\Omega$ is compact, so a subsequence of these couplings converges to a coupling $m$ of $\mu_-$ and $\mu_+$ supported on $\{\sigma\preceq\sigma'\}$. Under $m$, the differences $\sigma'_x-\sigma_x$ are nonnegative with $\mathbb E_m[\sigma'_x-\sigma_x]=0-0=0$, so $\sigma'_x=\sigma_x$ $m$-a.s.; intersecting over the countably many vertices gives $\sigma=\sigma'$ $m$-a.s., and therefore $\mu_-=\mu_+$.

    The theorem is the reason the order parameter deserves the name: non-uniqueness, which is a statement about a set of measures, is detected by one number. It says nothing yet about whether that number is positive, and the next two results are everything monotonicity has to say on that question.

    Assume $M(\beta)>0$. Then the correlations of the plus state dominate $M^2$: $\mathbb E_{\mu_+}[\sigma_x\sigma_y]\geq M^2$ for all $x,y$, so the susceptibility $\chi_+(\beta)=\sum_{y}\mathbb E_{\mu_+}[\sigma_0\sigma_y]$ is infinite. For every $\beta<\infty$,

    $$M(\beta)\leq\tanh\Bigl(\beta C\Bigr)=\tanh\bigl(\beta/\beta_c^{\mathrm{G}}\bigr),$$

    where $\beta_c^{\mathrm G}=1/C$ is the critical intensity of the Gaussian model.

    Proof

    Correlations. By the symmetry article the plus state is invariant under translations and the two-point function is radial, and by the Griffiths inequality, the second of the pair in [@griffiths-1967], $\mathbb E_{\mu_+}[\sigma_x\sigma_y]\geq\mathbb E_{\mu_+}[\sigma_x]\,\mathbb E_{\mu_+}[\sigma_y]=M^2$ for every pair. Summing over the shell at level $k$, which has $(1-L^{-d})L^{dk}$ vertices, gives a contribution at least $M^2(1-L^{-d})L^{dk}$, and the sum over $k$ diverges.

    The bound. By the Gibbs property at the single site $0$, $\sigma_0$ is a function of the other spins whose conditional expectation is $\tanh\bigl(\beta H\bigr)$ with $H=\sum_{y\neq0}J(\lVert y\rVert)\sigma_y$, so $M=\mathbb E_{\mu_+}[\tanh(\beta H)]$. Since $\tanh$ is odd and increasing, $\tanh(\beta H)\leq\tanh(\beta\lvert H\rvert)$; since $\lvert H\rvert\leq C$ and $\tanh$ is concave on $[0,\infty)$ with $\tanh0=0$, Jensen's inequality gives $\mathbb E[\tanh(\beta\lvert H\rvert)]\leq\tanh\bigl(\beta\mathbb E\lvert H\rvert\bigr)\leq\tanh(\beta C)$. The last value is $\tanh(\beta/\beta_c^{\mathrm G})$ because $C$ is the reciprocal of the Gaussian critical intensity.

    The same computation at finite volume with the plus boundary condition gives $\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_\Lambda^{+}}[\sigma_x]\leq\tanh\bigl(\beta(C_\Lambda+b_x)\bigr)$, where $C_\Lambda=\sum_{y\in\Lambda\setminus x}J(x-y)$ is the mass inside the block and $b_x=\sum_{y\notin\Lambda}J(x-y)$ the mass at the boundary; both forms follow from $\lvert H\rvert\leq$ the total mass seen by $x$.

    The two statements are of very different strength, and the difference is the point of the article. The first is a genuine consequence: a magnetised phase has infinite susceptibility, so the divergence of $\chi$ is not an extra assumption but a corollary of order, and this is the inequality behind the exponent relation $\gamma\geq0$ in the dictionary. The second is nearly empty: it says that $M$ is below one at every finite intensity, which was clear from $\lvert\sigma_0\rvert=1$, and that it tends to one as $\beta\to\infty$. It does not say that $M$ is zero anywhere, and no argument of this kind will.

    The block average

    The order parameter is defined at a vertex and the recursion is a statement about a block sum, so the transfer between them deserves to be a theorem rather than a remark. On the hierarchical lattice it is one, and for the reason used throughout part one: a block is a subgroup, so a translation by any of its own vertices permutes it.

    Let $\nu$ be a Gibbs measure and $n\geq1$. Then $\lvert\mathbb E_\nu[S_{\Lambda_n}]\rvert\leq m^nM(\beta)$. For the plus state every term of the sum is the same number, with $M_n(\beta)$ in place of its limit:

    $$\frac{1}{m^n}\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}}\bigl[S_{\Lambda_n}\bigr]=\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}}[\sigma_x]=:M_n(\beta)$$

    for every $x\in\Lambda_n$, and $M_n(\beta)$ decreases to $M(\beta)$.

    Proof

    Translation by $v\in\Lambda_n$ maps $\Lambda_n$ onto itself, because $\Lambda_n$ is a subgroup, and it preserves the Hamiltonian and the prior, so it preserves $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}$ — this is the invariance used in the proof of the extremal-state proposition. Hence $\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}}[\sigma_x]$ does not depend on $x\in\Lambda_n$, and averaging $S_{\Lambda_n}$ over the $m^n$ vertices gives the identity.

    The limit: $\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}}[\sigma_0]\to\mathbb E_{\mu_+}[\sigma_0]=M(\beta)$ because $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}\to\mu_+$ weakly and $\sigma_0$ is continuous and bounded, and the sequence decreases by the same proposition. For a general Gibbs measure, each $\mathbb E_\nu[\sigma_x]$ lies in $[\mathbb E_{\mu_-}[\sigma_x],\mathbb E_{\mu_+}[\sigma_x]]=[-M(\beta),M(\beta)]$ by the sandwich and the flip symmetry, so the sum is bounded in modulus by $m^nM(\beta)$.

    Two things follow that the earlier sections could not state. First, the block average of the plus state and the order parameter are not merely related by a limit: at every finite volume they are the same number, so any statement about one is a statement about the other. Second, the block average of an arbitrary Gibbs measure is bounded by the order parameter, so a limit of block averages with a nonzero mean forces $M(\beta)>0$.

    For the symmetric measures the mean is no help, since $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{\mathrm{free}}$ is invariant under the global flip and its block average therefore has mean zero at every $n$. There the statement must be about the law, and it is the following.

    Suppose that under some Gibbs measure the block averages $S_{\Lambda_n}/m^n$ converge in distribution to a random variable $Y$. If $M(\beta)=0$ then $Y=0$ almost surely. Equivalently, a nondegenerate limit of the block averages forces $M(\beta)>0$, and hence more than one Gibbs measure.

    Proof

    Assume $M(\beta)=0$. By the order-parameter theorem there is exactly one Gibbs measure $\mu$; since the extreme points of the set of Gibbs measures are those with trivial tail $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal T=\bigcap_n\mathcal F_{\Lambda_n^c}$, a unique one is trivial on $\mathcal T$ [@georgii-2011]. It is enough to show that $Y$ is $\mathcal T$-measurable and constant, and both come from the group structure.

    The limit is translation invariant. For $v\in\Lambda_k$ and $n\geq k$, translation by $v$ permutes $\Lambda_n$, so $Y_n\circ\tau_v=Y_n$ identically. Fix $v$, and pass to a subsequence along which $Y_n\to Y$ almost surely, which exists because $\lvert Y_n\rvert\leq1$ and $Y_n$ converges in distribution. For $n\geq\operatorname{lev}(v)$ the shifted variables equal the unshifted ones, so $Y\circ\tau_v=Y$ almost surely. As $v$ was arbitrary, $Y$ is invariant.

    An invariant event is a tail event. Let $A$ be invariant and $\Lambda$ finite. Choose $v$ of level above both $\Lambda$ and $n$; then $\Lambda_n+v$ is disjoint from $\Lambda$, so $\sigma_x\mapsto\sigma_{x+u+v}$ for $u\in\Lambda_n$ reads only spins outside $\Lambda$. Since $A$ is invariant, every term of the average $\frac{1}{\lvert\Lambda_n\rvert}\sum_{u\in\Lambda_n}\mathbf 1_A\circ\tau_{u+v}$ equals $\mathbf 1_A$, so $\mathbf 1_A$ is measurable with respect to the spins outside $\Lambda$. As $\Lambda$ was arbitrary, $A\in\mathcal T$.

    So $Y$ is measurable with respect to $\mathcal T$, which is trivial, and $Y$ is almost surely constant. The constant is the mean: $\lvert Y_n\rvert\leq1$, so $\mathbb E[Y]=\lim_n\mathbb E[Y_n]$, and the mean of $Y_n$ is $M_n(\beta)\to M(\beta)=0$ for the plus measures, zero at every $n$ for the free measures by the flip symmetry, and in $[-M,M]=0$ for any Gibbs measure by the block-average proposition. Hence $Y=0$ almost surely.

    The theorem is the converse that the transfer from the recursion needs, and it is where the difference between a mean and a law shows up. Above the critical intensity the free block average converges in distribution to the symmetric law on $\pm M$, whose mean is zero; the previous proposition sees nothing, since its bound $\lvert\mathbb E[S_{\Lambda_n}]\rvert\leq m^nM$ is consistent with any value of $M$ and any mean in $[-M,M]$. What rules that case out when $M=0$ is not an inequality but the structure of the tail: a unique Gibbs measure has none, so a limit of block averages cannot be a fair coin.

    The boundary field gives the same relation a quantitative form. For $t\in[-1,1]$ let $\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{(t)}$ be the measure whose boundary spins are all $t$; the energy of the boundary is then $-tB_nS_{\Lambda_n}$, because the rim mass is the same at every vertex. The family is a one-parameter exponential family in $S_{\Lambda_n}$, so

    $$\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E_{\mathbb P^{(t)}}[S_{\Lambda_n}]=\beta B_n\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb P^{(t)}}(S_{\Lambda_n}),\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E_{\mathbb P^{(t)}}[\sigma_x]=\beta B_n\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb P^{(t)}}(\sigma_x,S_{\Lambda_n})\geq0,$$

    the covariance being nonnegative by the FKG inequality, and integrating with the flip symmetry $\mathbb P^{(-t)}=\mathrm{flip}\,\mathbb P^{(t)}$ gives

    $$\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}}\bigl[S_{\Lambda_n}\bigr]=\tfrac12\beta B_n\int_{-1}^{1}\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb P^{(t)}}(S_{\Lambda_n})\,dt.$$

    So the block magnetisation of the plus state is exactly the accumulated variance of the block spin as the boundary is turned on: $M_n(\beta)$ is the rate at which the block spin's variance is generated by the boundary, and the order parameter is its limit. This is the identity behind the statement that a nonzero block spin and a nonzero order parameter are the same phenomenon seen at two scales.

    Let $\beta>0$ and $n\geq1$. Then $M_n(\beta)>0$: the block of level $n$ with the plus boundary condition is magnetised at every vertex, however high the temperature.

    Proof

    The plus measure is the free measure on $\Lambda_n$ reweighted by the strictly increasing function $w(\sigma)=\exp\bigl(\beta\sum_{x\in\Lambda_n}b_x\sigma_x\bigr)$, where $b_x=B_n=CL^{-\alpha n}>0$ is the boundary field. Its mean at a vertex is

    $$\mathbb E_{\mathbb P^{+}}[\sigma_x]=\frac{\mathbb E_{\mathbb P^{\mathrm{free}}}[\sigma_xw]}{\mathbb E_{\mathbb P^{\mathrm{free}}}[w]}=\mathbb E_{\mathbb P^{\mathrm{free}}}[\sigma_x]+\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb P^{\mathrm{free}}}(\sigma_x,w)}{\mathbb E_{\mathbb P^{\mathrm{free}}}[w]},$$

    and $\mathbb E_{\mathbb P^{\mathrm{free}}}[\sigma_x]=0$ by the flip symmetry. The covariance of the two increasing functions $\sigma_x$ and $w$ is nonnegative by the FKG inequality, and it is positive as soon as $\beta>0$, because then $w$ is not constant and the free measure is non-degenerate. Dividing by a positive normaliser gives $M_n(\beta)>0$.

    The proposition looks like it contradicts the existence of a transition, and it does not, because it is a statement about a block. On a finite block the boundary is at a finite distance from every vertex, so the strictly positive boundary field magnetises it at any intensity; the transition is the statement that the limit of $M_n$ can vanish, which is compatible with every term being positive and is why the sign of $M$ is a question about the limit and not about any volume. The same distinction is what makes the block-average proposition say that the block average decreases to $M$: it decreases, and the question is whether its limit is zero.

    Where the threshold comes from

    It is worth being explicit about why monotonicity cannot locate the transition, because the reason is structural rather than technical. Every assertion in the previous sections is preserved under replacing the kernel by another ferromagnetic one with the same total mass $C$: the domination, the sandwich, the equivalence of the theorem, and both bounds depend on the couplings only through their signs and their sum. In particular the one inequality that mentions the intensity, $M\leq\tanh(\beta C)$, holds at every $\beta$ and is therefore compatible both with $M>0$ at every intensity and with $M=0$ at every intensity. An argument whose conclusions are invariant under a change of kernel that preserves $C$ cannot produce a threshold, and the invariance here is exact, not a matter of the estimates being crude: no sharper use of the same inequalities will do better.

    The threshold is located by the recursion. In the language of the fixed-point article, the orbit of the Ising initial law under the rescaled decimation map converges to the point mass at $0$ below a critical coupling, to a nontrivial fixed point at it, and to a law of unbounded variance above it; that trichotomy is the theorem of Bleher and Sinai [@bleher-sinai-1973] [@bleher-sinai-1975]. The third branch is the magnetised phase: above the critical coupling the block spin per site has a nonzero limit $\pm M$, the two signs being the two states of this article. The identification with the order parameter defined here is exactly the statement of the block-average proposition and its converse: the block average of the plus state is the single-site magnetisation at every finite volume, it decreases to $M(\beta)$, and a nondegenerate limit of block averages is impossible when $M(\beta)=0$. So a nonzero limit of the block spin is not merely an indication of a magnetised phase — it is the same statement.

    So the division of labour is this. Monotonicity gives the shape of the phase diagram — two extremal states, an order parameter, uniqueness exactly when the order parameter vanishes — and answers the previous article's open question about how many Gibbs measures there are, in the sense that it reduces the question to the sign of one number. The recursion decides the sign. That the sign requires the recursion, and is not available from the inequalities of this article, is what the paragraph above argues, and it is the reason the wiki treats the two arguments as separate.

    Examples and non-examples

    No interaction. For $J=0$ the Gibbs measure is the product of the prior and is unique, so $M(\beta)=0$ for every $\beta$; the plus and minus boundary conditions have no effect beyond the boundary itself, and the two limits coincide with the product measure.

    An external field. A field $h\sum_x\sigma_x$ adds the constant $h$ to every field in the specification, and the domination proposition applies in $h$ as it does in the boundary condition: the finite-volume measures are nondecreasing in $h$, hence so is $M(\beta,h)$, and the limit $h\downarrow0$ exists and is the order parameter of this article. The field also breaks the flip symmetry, so the minus state is no longer the mirror image of the plus one, and the two magnetisations, one positive and one negative at $h=0$, move towards each other as $h$ increases. That monotonicity is what makes the phase diagram of a model with a field a pair of curves rather than a pair of symmetric ones; where they meet is not a question monotonicity answers.

    Non-example: the antiferromagnet. For $J<0$ the FKG inequality in the form used here fails, since the lattice condition has the wrong sign, and with it every step of this article: the heat bath is no longer monotone, the plus and minus boundary conditions no longer bound the Gibbs measures, and the order parameter defined above has no reason to detect anything. Recovering a monotone structure for an antiferromagnet needs a gauge transformation, that is a choice of signs $\chi$ with $\chi(x)\chi(y)=-1$ across each coupled pair, which presupposes a bipartition of the coupled pairs; on the hierarchical lattice every pair is coupled, at some level, and no such $\chi$ exists. The wiki treats ferromagnetic models throughout, and this is the hypothesis the article rests on.

    Percolation. The same architecture holds for bond percolation on the lattice: the Harris–FKG inequality gives that the connection probabilities increase in the intensity, the percolation probability $\theta(\beta)=\mathbb P_\beta(\lvert K_0\rvert=\infty)$ plays the role of the order parameter and is positive exactly in the percolating phase. The threshold there is $\beta_c$ of the percolation article, and its finiteness is decided by $\alpha<d$ through a comparison with the recursion, again not by monotonicity.

    Variants and generalisations

    Other boundary conditions. Every configuration $\omega$ satisfies $-\preceq\omega\preceq+$, so the sandwich holds with $\mathbb P_\Lambda^{\omega}$ in place of $\mathbb P_\Lambda^{\pm}$: the plus and minus states bound every finite-volume measure, not only the Gibbs measures. Free boundary conditions are not of that form, since there is no outside configuration to compare with, but the same domination holds: the plus measure is the free measure reweighted by $\exp\bigl(\beta\sum_{x\in\Lambda}b_x\sigma_x\bigr)$ with $b_x=\sum_{y\notin\Lambda}J(x-y)\geq0$, a bounded increasing function of the configuration, so the FKG inequality gives $\mathbb P_\Lambda^{\mathrm{free}}\preceq\mathbb P_\Lambda^{+}$ and the free limit of the previous article lies between $\mu_-$ and $\mu_+$ as well.

    Monotonicity in the intensity. That $M(\beta)$ is nondecreasing in $\beta$ is the first Griffiths inequality, the companion of the second used above, and is the same statement as the monotonicity of the correlations in the couplings [@griffiths-1967]. It gives a critical intensity $\beta_c=\inf\{\beta:M(\beta)>0\}$ as a well-defined number, and it is what makes the phase diagram a diagram rather than a set of values; it still does not evaluate the infimum.

    The general block system. Nothing in the argument uses the lattice beyond the fact that the blocks exhaust the vertex set and that the model is ferromagnetic with a finite total mass at a vertex. For any attractive spin system on a countable set the same two propositions hold, with the same proofs: the boundary-conditioned measures increase in the boundary configuration, the plus and minus limits exist, and every Gibbs measure lies between them. That the Gibbs measures of an attractive system form an interval in the stochastic order is the general form of the sandwich.

    History and attribution

    The inequality that increasing functions are positively correlated under a ferromagnetic measure is due to Fortuin, Kasteleyn and Ginibre [@fkg-1971]; for Ising spins the correlation inequalities, including the pair used here, are Griffiths's [@griffiths-1967]. The extremal states of an attractive system, obtained as the monotone limits of the plus and minus boundary conditions, and the statement that every Gibbs measure lies between them, are part of the general theory in Georgii's book [@georgii-2011], where the argument is given for a general specification; the monotone coupling used above is the standard proof in the finite volume.

    The phase transition of the hierarchical Ising model, with a nonzero spontaneous magnetisation above the critical coupling, is the third branch of the trichotomy of Bleher and Sinai [@bleher-sinai-1973] [@bleher-sinai-1975]; the fixed-point article states their theorem and describes the mechanism of its proof. The separation recorded in the previous section — that the architecture is a matter of monotonicity and the threshold a matter of the recursion — is not a claim about the literature, in which the two are usually proved together for a particular model, but about which argument carries which statement.

    The renormalisation group map

    Decimation as an operator on one-variable laws, and the scaling that makes it autonomous.

    definition · separate page

    Block decimation turns the exact recursion of a hierarchical spin system into an operator $\mathcal T_b$ on probability measures on the line: take $m$ independent copies of a law, tilt them by $\exp(b\sum_{a<a'}s_as_{a'})$, and record the law of their sum. The model applies $\mathcal T_{\beta J_n}$ with $J_n$ decaying geometrically, so the sequence of operators is not autonomous; rescaling the spin by $L^{-\theta}$ with $\theta=\tfrac12(d+\alpha)$ multiplies the coupling by $L^{2\theta}$ at each step and makes the composition a power of one map $\mathcal R_\theta$.

    That map is the object whose fixed points, and whose linearisation at them, carry the critical behaviour: a fixed point is a self-similar block-spin law, and the eigenvalues of the derivative there are the exponents, in the way made precise in the article on exponents. Two of its properties are used constantly and are proved here: it commutes with dilations, in the sense that $\mathcal T_b$ applied to a dilated law is the dilation of $\mathcal T_{b\lambda^2}$ applied to the law, and it is continuous on laws supported in a fixed compact set.

    The construction is that of Bleher and Sinai (1973), who studied the transformation directly for Dyson's model [@bleher-sinai-1973], and it is the map developed from scratch in the renormalisation-group treatment of lattice field theory [@bauerschmidt-brydges-slade-2019]. Nothing in this article depends on the model being Gaussian or Ising; the two cases are worked out in the Gaussian article and, in its own form, in the percolation article.

    Definitions and setting

    Write $A$ for the index set of children, of cardinality $m=L^d$, and let $\mu$ be a Borel probability measure on $\mathbb R$. For $b\geq0$ put

    $$Z_b(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb R^m}\exp\Bigl(b\sum_{a

    with the convention that the integral is $+\infty$ when it diverges. The decimation operator is defined when $0<Z_b(\mu)<\infty$ by

    $$\mathcal T_b\mu(A')=\frac{1}{Z_b(\mu)}\int_{\mathbb R^m}\mathbf 1_{A'}\Bigl(\sum_{a\in A}s_a\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(b\sum_{a

    for Borel $A'\subseteq\mathbb R$, and the scaled decimation operator at exponent $\theta>0$ is

    $$\mathcal R_{\theta,b}\mu=\bigl(s\mapsto L^{-\theta}s\bigr)_*\bigl(\mathcal T_b\mu\bigr),$$

    the image of $\mathcal T_b\mu$ under multiplication by $L^{-\theta}$. The quadratic form of the tilt is $\sum_{a<a'}s_as_{a'}=\tfrac12\bigl(\bigl(\sum_as_a\bigr)^2-\sum_as_a^2\bigr)$, so the tilt rewards configurations whose sum is large in absolute value.

    If $\mu$ is supported in $[-K,K]$ then $0<Z_b(\mu)<\infty$ for every $b\geq0$, because the exponent is bounded on $[-K,K]^m$; and $\mathcal T_b\mu$ is then supported in $[-mK,mK]$. For a law with tails heavier than Gaussian the normaliser can diverge, and then the operator is undefined at that $b$; the Gaussian case in the next article locates the threshold exactly.

    Dilation covariance and continuity

    Two properties make the operator usable: it scales in a fixed way when the input is dilated, and it is continuous in the input law.

    For $\lambda>0$, with $\lambda_*\mu$ the law of $\lambda s$ under $\mu$,

    $$\mathcal T_b(\lambda_*\mu)=\lambda_*\bigl(\mathcal T_{b\lambda^2}\mu\bigr),\qquad \mathcal R_{\theta,b}(\lambda_*\mu)=\lambda_*\bigl(\mathcal R_{\theta,b\lambda^2}\mu\bigr).$$
    Proof of dilation covariance

    Sampling $s_a$ from $\lambda_*\mu$ is the same as writing $s_a=\lambda t_a$ with $t_a$ sampled independently from $\mu$. The exponent becomes $b\lambda^2\sum_{a<a'}t_at_{a'}$, the normaliser becomes $Z_{b\lambda^2}(\mu)$, and the sum becomes $\lambda\sum_at_a$. Hence the law of $\sum_as_a$ is the image under multiplication by $\lambda$ of the law of $\sum_at_a$ computed with coupling $b\lambda^2$, which is the first identity. Multiplying by $L^{-\theta}$ afterwards gives the second, since dilations commute.

    The identity is the reason no separate analysis is needed at each scale: a factor $\lambda$ in the spin is a factor $\lambda^2$ in the coupling, so the pair $(\text{scale},\text{coupling})$ flows on a cone.

    Fix $K<\infty$ and $B<\infty$. The map $(\mu,b)\mapsto\mathcal T_b\mu$ is continuous on the set of laws supported in $[-K,K]$ and couplings $b\in[0,B]$, for the topology of weak convergence, and $\mathcal T_b$ maps that set into the laws supported in $[-mK,mK]$.

    Proof of continuity

    On $[-K,K]^m$ the tilt $\exp(b\sum_{a<a'}s_as_{a'})$ is continuous in $(s,b)$ and bounded above by $e^{Bm(m-1)K^2/2}$ and below by $e^{-Bm(m-1)K^2/2}$, and the sum $\sum_as_a$ is continuous and bounded by $mK$. If $\mu_j\Rightarrow\mu$ weakly with all $\mu_j$ supported in $[-K,K]$ and $b_j\to b$, then $\mu_j^{\otimes m}\Rightarrow\mu^{\otimes m}$ because the product of a weakly convergent sequence of laws on a compact set converges weakly to the product, and the integrands above are continuous and bounded, so $Z_{b_j}(\mu_j)\to Z_b(\mu)$. The normalisers are bounded below by $e^{-Bm(m-1)K^2/2}$, which is positive, so the normalised measures converge weakly by the same bounded convergence applied to $\mathbf 1_{A'}(\sum_as_a)$ tested against bounded continuous functions of $\sum_as_a$. The support statement is immediate from $\lvert s_a\rvert\leq K$.

    The hypothesis of compact support is met by the Ising prior at finite volume, where the block spin of $\Lambda_n$ lies in $[-m^n,m^n]$, and it is met after rescaling only in the sense that the support bound $L^{(d-\theta)n}$ grows with $n$ when $\theta<d$. The rescaled orbit is therefore not confined to a fixed compact set, and the continuity above does not by itself give compactness: what compactness requires is a uniform bound on the second moment of the rescaled laws, and supplying that bound is where the analysis of a particular model enters. Below the critical intensity the block spin has typical size $L^{dn/2}$, above it $L^{dn}$, and at the critical intensity $L^{\theta_0n}$ with $\theta_0=\tfrac12(d+\alpha)$; the rescaled orbit therefore shrinks, grows, or stays of order one in the three cases.

    The autonomous form

    The model applies a different operator at each scale. Rescaling the spin makes the sequence a power of one map, and this is the precise sense in which the renormalisation group is a dynamical system here.

    Let $\beta\geq0$, $\theta_0=\tfrac12(d+\alpha)$ and $\nu_n$ be the law of $L^{-\theta_0n}S_{\Lambda_n}$ under $\mathbb P_n$ of the hierarchical model. Then, with $b=\beta J_1=\beta JL^{-(d+\alpha)}$,

    $$\nu_n=\mathcal R_{\theta_0,b}^{\,n}\nu_0\qquad(n\geq0).$$
    Proof of the autonomous form

    The decimation identity gives $\mu_n=\mathcal T_{\beta J_n}\mu_{n-1}$ with $J_n=J_1L^{-(d+\alpha)(n-1)}$. Let $s_a$ have law $\mu_{n-1}$ and write $s_a=L^{\theta_0(n-1)}t_a$, so that $t_a$ has law $\nu_{n-1}$. Then $\beta J_n\sum_{a<a'}s_as_{a'}=\beta J_nL^{2\theta_0(n-1)}\sum_{a<a'}t_at_{a'}$, and $2\theta_0=d+\alpha$ gives $\beta J_nL^{2\theta_0(n-1)}=\beta J_1$. Hence $\mathcal T_{\beta J_n}\mu_{n-1}$ is the image under multiplication by $L^{\theta_0(n-1)}$ of $\mathcal T_{\beta J_1}\nu_{n-1}$, and $S_{\Lambda_n}=\sum_as_a=L^{\theta_0(n-1)}\sum_at_a$. Multiplying by $L^{-\theta_0n}$ gives $\nu_n=\mathcal R_{\theta_0,\beta J_1}\nu_{n-1}$. Induction from $\nu_0$ gives the statement.

    The exponent $\theta_0$ is not a physical prediction but the unique choice for which the coupling does not depend on $n$: with any other $\theta$ the argument of $\mathcal T$ would be $\beta J_1L^{2(\theta-\theta_0)(n-1)}$, growing or decaying geometrically, and the composition would not be a power of one map. Two consequences follow, and both are used later. The trajectory is the orbit of the initial law $\nu_0$ under a single map, so its asymptotics are those of a dynamical system; and if the orbit converges to a limit $\nu^*$ with $0<\int s^2d\nu^*<\infty$, then $\mathbb E[S_{\Lambda_n}^2]=L^{2\theta_0n}\int s^2d\nu^*\,(1+o(1))$, which fixes the growth of the block spin and hence one exponent, as the exponent article shows.

    Fixed points and the linearisation

    A law $\nu^*$ is a fixed point of $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}$ if $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}\nu^*=\nu^*$ and $0<\int s^2d\nu^*<\infty$. At a fixed point, a bounded linear operator $D\mathcal R$ on the space of signed measures of zero total mass is the linearisation if

    $$\frac{\mathcal R_{\theta,b}\bigl((1-\epsilon)\nu^*+\epsilon\rho\bigr)-\nu^*}{\epsilon}\longrightarrow D\mathcal R\,\rho\quad(\epsilon\downarrow0)$$

    for every signed measure $\rho$ of zero mass with $\int\lvert s\rvert^2d\lvert\rho\rvert(s)<\infty$. A direction is an eigenmeasure $D\mathcal R\rho=\lambda\rho$; it is relevant if $\lvert\lambda\rvert>1$, marginal if $\lvert\lambda\rvert=1$ and irrelevant if $\lvert\lambda\rvert<1$.

    The classification is the standard one for a map on a space of laws and it is used here in the following way. The orbit approaches the fixed point along its stable directions and leaves it along its unstable ones; the critical model is the one whose orbit stays, and the two relevant directions of the hierarchical model are the thermal one, which measures the distance from the critical intensity, and an irrelevant or marginal partner that controls corrections. The exponents are functions of the relevant eigenvalues; the dictionary is the content of the exponent article, and the linearisation is computed in closed form only in the Gaussian case.

    Examples and non-examples

    The Gaussian orbit. For $\nu_0=N(0,1)$ and the Gaussian prior, both the tilt and the summation preserve Gaussianity, so the orbit stays in the one-parameter family of centred Gaussians and the map reduces to a recursion on the variance: $\mathcal T_bN(0,v)=N(0,mv/(1-bv(m-1)))$ whenever the denominator is positive. This is worked out in the Gaussian article, where the fixed point and the eigenvalue $L^\alpha$ are computed.

    A degenerate fixed point. The point mass $\delta_0$ is a fixed point of $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}$ for every $\theta$ and $b$: the tilt of a product of point masses is a point mass at $0$, and the sum is $0$. It carries zero variance and is excluded by the definition above. In the model it is the limit of the orbit below the critical intensity, where the rescaled block spin vanishes.

    Non-example: a law with divergent normaliser. If $\mu$ has a density decaying like $e^{-c\lvert s\rvert^p}$ with $0<p<2$, so that its tails are heavier than Gaussian, then $Z_b(\mu)=\infty$ for every $b>0$ and $\mathcal T_b\mu$ is undefined. The Gaussian threshold at $b=1/((m-1)v)$ is the borderline case, and it is the reason the fixed-point analysis of the Gaussian model stops at the critical coupling.

    Non-example: decimation of a Euclidean model. On $\mathbb Z^d$ the analogous operation produces a law of the block magnetisation that is not a function of the block law alone, because the boundary between two blocks carries an interaction that depends on the arrangement of spins inside them. This is the subject of the comparison article.

    Variants and generalisations

    External field. Adding a field gives a family $\mathcal T_{b,h}$ acting on laws of the block spin with the argument $h$ carried along, and the magnetic eigenvalue is the derivative of the flow at the fixed point in $h$. The single-field versions are what appears in the model with $-\beta h\sum_x\sigma_x$ in the Hamiltonian.

    Anisotropic scaling. Replacing $\theta_0$ by another exponent corresponds to weighting the sum differently at each scale and gives a map whose coupling is scale dependent. A fixed point of such a map is a self-similar law with anomalous scaling; the model's own recursion sits at $\theta_0$ and it is the physical exponents that decide whether the orbit of $\nu_0$ converges to a fixed point of $\mathcal R_{\theta_0,b}$ at all.

    Other numbers of children. Nothing in the definitions uses $m=L^d$ beyond the cardinality of $A$: the same operator makes sense with any integer $m\geq2$, and the lattice realisation with $m$ children and ratio $\lambda$ is the one described in the lattice article.

    History and attribution

    The transformation $\mathcal T_b$ is the renormalisation transformation of Bleher and Sinai (1973), who introduced it as a nonlinear integral operator on probability measures, proved that its fixed points govern the critical behaviour of Dyson's model, and computed the indices from its linearisation [@bleher-sinai-1973] [@bleher-sinai-1975]. The autonomous form of the recursion, and the reading of the model's block-spin sequence as an orbit of one map, are the derivations of that work; the systematic treatment in the language of measures with exponential tails, including the existence of the nontrivial fixed point, is in the next article but one, following Gawedzki and Kupiainen [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983].

    The Gaussian renormalisation group

    On Gaussian laws the decimation closes on the variance, and the critical fixed point has multiplier L to the alpha.

    theorem · separate page

    With a Gaussian single-spin prior the decimation operator preserves Gaussianity: the tilt is quadratic and the sum is linear, so the block spin of a Gaussian model is Gaussian and the whole orbit is described by one number, its variance. The recursion for that variance is fractional linear, so it is solved in closed form: the block-spin variance at scale $n$ is $L^{dn}$ divided by $1-\beta\sum_{x\ne0}J(\lVert x\rVert)$ truncated to the first $n$ shells. Three consequences follow without further work. The critical intensity is the reciprocal of the total kernel mass, so the interaction is summable exactly in the regime in which the intensity is finite; the susceptibility is exactly $(\beta_c-\beta)^{-1}\beta_c$; and the recursion written in the dimensionless variable $u_n=\beta J_nv_n$ has a nonzero fixed point whose multiplier is $L^\alpha$.

    The multiplier is the point of the exercise. In the dictionary of the exponent article, a multiplier $\lambda_1=L^\alpha$ at the critical fixed point gives $\nu=\log L/\log\lambda_1=1/\alpha$, which is the classical long-range value; together with the block-spin growth exponent $\theta=\tfrac12(d+\alpha)$, which the normalisation of the recursion at the autonomous scale forces, it gives $\kappa=\alpha$ and hence $\eta=2-\alpha$. These are the values tabulated for long-range interactions by Fisher, Ma and Nickel [@fisher-ma-nickel-1972], and they are exact for this model.

    The Gaussian model is the one case of the hierarchical recursion that is solved in closed form, and it is the reference point for the others: the non-Gaussian fixed point of Bleher and Sinai merges with it at $\alpha=d/2$ and governs the critical behaviour above that value, and the percolation exponents of the percolation article coincide with the Gaussian ones in the sense that its two-point exponent is also $2-\alpha$.

    Definitions and setting

    Take the prior $\varrho=N(0,1)$ in the hierarchical model, with $J_k=JL^{-(d+\alpha)k}$ and $\beta\geq0$, and let $\mu_n$ be the law of the block spin $S_{\Lambda_n}$. The measure $\mathbb P_n$ is then a centred Gaussian measure on $\mathbb R^{\Lambda_n}$ and $\mu_n=N(0,v_n)$ with $v_n=\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_n}[S_{\Lambda_n}^2]$. Write $A$ for the operator on $\ell^2(\mathbb H^d_L)$ of convolution by the kernel $x\mapsto J(\lVert x\rVert)$, and $A_n$ for its compression to $\Lambda_n$, that is the symmetric matrix $A_n(x,y)=J(\lVert x-y\rVert)$ for distinct $x,y\in\Lambda_n$ and zero on the diagonal.

    Two quantities organise the computation. The coupling at level $n$ is $b_n=\beta J_n=\beta JL^{-(d+\alpha)n}$, and the dimensionless coupling is $u_n=b_nv_n$. The ratio of successive couplings is $\lambda=L^{d+\alpha}$.

    Closure and the variance recursion

    Let $b\geq0$ and let $\mu=N(0,v)$ with $v>0$. If $bv(m-1)<1$ then $Z_b(\mu)<\infty$ and

    $$\mathcal T_b\mu=N(0,v'),\qquad v'=\frac{mv}{1-bv(m-1)};$$

    if $bv(m-1)\geq1$ then $Z_b(\mu)=\infty$ and $\mathcal T_b\mu$ is undefined.

    Proof of the closure and the variance recursion

    Write $s=(s_a)_{a\in A}$ and let $\Sigma^{-1}=(v^{-1}+b)I-b\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T$, where $\mathbf 1$ is the vector with all entries $1$. Completing the square,

    $$-\frac{1}{2v}\sum_as_a^2+\frac b2\Bigl(\bigl(\sum_as_a\bigr)^2-\sum_as_a^2\Bigr)=-\tfrac12s^T\Sigma^{-1}s,$$

    so the tilted measure is $N(0,\Sigma)$ when $\Sigma^{-1}$ is positive definite, and in that case the law of $\sum_as_a$ is $N(0,\mathbf 1^T\Sigma\mathbf 1)$. Positive definiteness of $\Sigma^{-1}$ means $v^{-1}+b>0$ and the determinant is positive; the eigenvalues of $\Sigma^{-1}$ are $v^{-1}+b$ with multiplicity $m-1$ and $v^{-1}+b-bm$ with multiplicity one, so the condition is $v^{-1}>b(m-1)$, which is $bv(m-1)<1$.

    For the variance, apply the Woodbury identity $(A-uu^T)^{-1}=A^{-1}+\frac{A^{-1}uu^TA^{-1}}{1-u^TA^{-1}u}$ with $A=(v^{-1}+b)I$ and $u=\sqrt b\,\mathbf 1$. Then $\mathbf 1^TA^{-1}\mathbf 1=\frac{mv}{1+bv}$, so

    $$\mathbf 1^T\Sigma\mathbf 1=\frac{mv}{1+bv}+\frac{b\,(mv/(1+bv))^2}{1-bmv/(1+bv)}=\frac{mv}{1+bv-bmv}=\frac{mv}{1-bv(m-1)}.$$

    If $bv(m-1)\geq1$, the quadratic form $s^T\Sigma^{-1}s$ takes nonpositive values on the line $\mathbb R\mathbf 1$, so the tilted density is not integrable and the normaliser diverges.

    The recursion is fractional linear in $v$, increasing and convex on its domain, and it blows up at the boundary $v=1/(b(m-1))$. It is the exact statement that the block spin of a Gaussian model is Gaussian: the tilt by a function of the sum and the pushforward by the sum preserve the class of centred Gaussians.

    The closed form and the critical intensity

    For every $n\geq0$,

    $$v_n=\frac{m^n}{1-\beta J\,(1-L^{-d})\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}L^{-\alpha k}} ,$$

    the model on $\Lambda_n$ exists for every $n$ exactly when $\beta\leq\beta_c$, where

    $$\beta_c=\frac{1}{\sum_{x\neq0}J(\lVert x\rVert)}=\frac{1-L^{-\alpha}}{J(1-L^{-d})L^{-\alpha}},$$

    and for $\beta<\beta_c$ one has $\chi(\beta):=\lim_nv_nL^{-dn}=\dfrac{\beta_c}{\beta_c-\beta}$. At $\beta=\beta_c$ one has $v_n=L^{(d+\alpha)n}$ for every $n$, and for $\beta>\beta_c$ there is a first $n$ with $bv_n(m-1)\geq1$.

    Proof of the closed form and the critical intensity

    Put $\rho=1/m=L^{-d}$, so that $\frac{1}{v_n}=\frac{1}{mv_{n-1}}-\frac{m-1}{m}b_n$ by the recursion, and iterate from $v_0=1$:

    $$\frac{1}{v_n}=\frac{1}{m^n}\Bigl(1-(m-1)\sum_{k=1}^nm^{k-1}b_k\Bigr).$$

    Since $b_k=\beta Jm^{-(1+\alpha/d)k}$ and $m^{k-1}b_k=\beta Jm^{-1}m^{-\alpha k/d}=\beta JL^{-d}L^{-\alpha k}$, this is $\frac{1}{v_n}=L^{-dn}\bigl(1-\beta J(1-L^{-d})\sum_{k\leq n}L^{-\alpha k}\bigr)$, which is the first display after inversion. The bracket is decreasing in $n$ and its limit is $1-\beta J(1-L^{-d})\frac{L^{-\alpha}}{1-L^{-\alpha}}=1-\beta/\beta_c$ with $\beta_c$ as displayed; the row sum was computed in the lattice article. Hence the bracket stays positive for all $n$ exactly when $\beta<\beta_c$, and then $v_nL^{-dn}\to\frac{1}{1-\beta/\beta_c}=\frac{\beta_c}{\beta_c-\beta}$. At $\beta=\beta_c$ the bracket is $1-(1-L^{-\alpha n})=L^{-\alpha n}$, giving $v_n=L^{dn}L^{\alpha n}$. For $\beta>\beta_c$ the bracket is eventually negative, so the recursion leaves its domain at some finite $n$.

    The threshold has a second description, which does not use the recursion and which identifies it as the norm of the interaction rather than as an artefact of the block structure.

    Let $A$ be convolution by $J(\lVert\cdot\rVert)$ on $\ell^2(\mathbb H^d_L)$ and $A_n$ its compression to $\Lambda_n$. Then $\lVert A\rVert=\sum_{x\neq0}J(\lVert x\rVert)$, and $\lambda_{\max}(A_n)\to\lVert A\rVert$ as $n\to\infty$.

    Proof that the norm is the row sum

    For $\phi\in\ell^2$ with finite support, $\langle\phi,A\phi\rangle=\sum_{x,y}\phi_xJ(x-y)\phi_y\leq\lVert\phi\rVert_2^2\sup_x\sum_yJ(x-y)=\lVert\phi\rVert_2^2\sum_{z\neq0}J(z)$ by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality applied to the pairs $(x,y)$ with weight $J$. For the reverse inequality take $\phi=\mathbf 1_{\Lambda_n}/\sqrt{\lvert\Lambda_n\rvert}$, which has norm one: then $\langle\phi,A\phi\rangle=\sum_zJ(z)\lvert\Lambda_n\cap(\Lambda_n-z)\rvert/\lvert\Lambda_n\rvert$. For fixed $z$ the ratio tends to $1$ as $n\to\infty$, and it is at most $1$, so dominated convergence gives convergence to $\sum_zJ(z)$. Hence $\lVert A_n\rVert\to\lVert A\rVert$, and $\lVert A\rVert$ is at least the limit of quotients and at most $\sum_zJ(z)$.

    For the finite-volume model, the Gaussian measure with density $\exp(-\frac\beta2\sigma^TA_n\sigma)$ exists exactly when $I-\beta A_n$ is positive definite, that is $\beta<1/\lambda_{\max}(A_n)$, and the critical intensity of the infinite lattice is the limit. This agrees with the recursion, and it shows that the two-sided role of $\alpha>0$ is unavoidable: at $\alpha\leq0$ the row sum diverges and there is no finite intensity at which the field exists.

    The autonomous flow and the multiplier

    Written in the dimensionless coupling, the recursion has a nonzero fixed point, and its multiplier is the exponent.

    [Interactive figure: The Gaussian orbit: choose the intensity and watch the block-spin variance against the scale. Above βc it grows, below it dies, and on the dashed critical orbit it grows like L^{(d+α)n}. — on the web page]

    Let $u_n=b_nv_n$ with $b_n=\beta J_n$. Then $u_n=g(u_{n-1})$, where

    $$g(u)=\frac{mu}{\lambda-(m-1)u},\qquad \lambda=L^{d+\alpha},$$

    on the domain $u<\lambda/(m-1)$. The map $g$ has the fixed point $u^*=\dfrac{m(L^{\alpha}-1)}{m-1}$, and $g'(u^*)=\lambda/m=L^{\alpha}$. The critical orbit is $u_n\equiv u^*$; for $\beta<\beta_c$ one has $u_n\to0$ with ratio $L^{-\alpha}$, and for $\beta>\beta_c$ the orbit leaves the domain.

    Proof of the fixed point and the multiplier

    Multiply the variance recursion by $b_n$: $u_n=\frac{mb_nv_{n-1}}{1-b_nv_{n-1}(m-1)}$. Since $b_nv_{n-1}=\lambda^{-1}b_{n-1}v_{n-1}=\lambda^{-1}u_{n-1}$, this is $u_n=\frac{m\lambda^{-1}u_{n-1}}{1-(m-1)\lambda^{-1}u_{n-1}}=g(u_{n-1})$.

    The fixed point equation $u=g(u)$ with $u\neq0$ is $1-(m-1)\lambda^{-1}u=m\lambda^{-1}$, giving $u^*=\lambda\frac{1-m/\lambda}{m-1}=\frac{\lambda-m}{m-1}=\frac{m(L^\alpha-1)}{m-1}$. Differentiating, $g'(u)=\frac{m\lambda}{(\lambda-(m-1)u)^2}$, and at $u^*$ the denominator is $\lambda-(m-1)u^*=\lambda-m(L^\alpha-1)=L^d(L^\alpha-L^\alpha+1)=L^d=m$, so $g'(u^*)=\frac{m\lambda}{m^2}=\lambda/m=L^\alpha$.

    For the critical orbit, the closed form at $\beta=\beta_c$ gives $v_n=L^{(d+\alpha)n}$ and $b_n=\beta_cJL^{-(d+\alpha)n}$, so $u_n=\beta_cJ$ for every $n$, and $\beta_cJ=\frac{L^\alpha-1}{1-L^{-d}}=\frac{m(L^\alpha-1)}{m-1}=u^*$. For $\beta<\beta_c$ the orbit converges to the fixed point $0$ of $g$, where $g'(0)=m/\lambda=L^{-\alpha}$. For $\beta>\beta_c$ the variance recursion leaves its domain, so the orbit of $g$ leaves the interval $[0,\lambda/(m-1))$.

    The multiplier $L^\alpha$ is the whole content of the model's critical behaviour. A perturbation of the critical orbit is multiplied by $L^\alpha$ per scale, so the distance from criticality acts like a length growing as $L^\alpha$ per scale; inverting, a system at distance $\delta$ from criticality has correlation length of order $\delta^{-1/\alpha}$, which is the statement $\nu=1/\alpha$. The vanishing of $u_n$ below criticality at rate $L^{-\alpha}$ says the same thing for the approach to the disordered fixed point.

    The exponents

    The Gaussian fixed point has

    $$\nu=\frac{1}{\alpha},\qquad \eta=2-\alpha,\qquad \gamma=1,\qquad \beta_{\mathrm{o}}=\frac{d-\alpha}{2\alpha},\qquad \delta=\frac{d+\alpha}{d-\alpha},\qquad \alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}=2-\frac{d}{\alpha}.$$
    Proof of the exponent values

    The multiplier at the fixed point is $\lambda_1=L^\alpha$, so $\nu=\log L/\log\lambda_1=1/\alpha$ by the definition of $\nu$ in the dictionary. The normalisation of the recursion is at $\theta_0=\tfrac12(d+\alpha)$, and the critical orbit has $v_n=L^{2\theta_0n}$ by the previous section, so the susceptibility of a block grows like $L^{\alpha n}$ and $\kappa=\alpha$, that is $\eta=2-\alpha$. The remaining values follow from the dictionary with $\kappa=\alpha$ and $\nu=1/\alpha$: $\gamma=\kappa\nu=1$, $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}=\tfrac{\nu}{2}(d-\kappa)=\frac{d-\alpha}{2\alpha}$, $\delta=\frac{d+\kappa}{d-\kappa}=\frac{d+\alpha}{d-\alpha}$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}=2-d\nu=2-d/\alpha$, and the susceptibility exponent is checked directly by the closed form $\chi=\beta_c(\beta_c-\beta)^{-1}$.

    The values are those of the classical long-range theory, with $\nu=1/\alpha$ independent of the dimension and $\eta$ tending to $1$ as $\alpha\uparrow d$. They satisfy the scaling relations $\gamma=\nu(2-\eta)$, $\gamma=\beta_{\mathrm{o}}(\delta-1)$ and $2-\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}=d\nu$ identically in $\alpha$ and $d$; the second is $\frac{d-\alpha}{2\alpha}\cdot\frac{2\alpha}{d-\alpha}=1$, and the third is $2-(2-d/\alpha)=d/\alpha$. At $\alpha=d/2$ they reduce to $\nu=2/d$, $\eta=2-d/2$, $\delta=3$, $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}=1/2$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}=0$: the Ising values of $\delta$, $\gamma$ and $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}$, with $\nu=2/d$ in place of the short-range mean-field value $\tfrac12$, the difference being the hyperscaling relation $2-\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}=d\nu$, which holds here with $\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}=0$ and fails for the short-range mean-field exponents above $d=4$.

    The two-point function in a block

    Let $n\geq1$ and let $x,y\in\Lambda_n$ lie in different children of $\Lambda_n$, so that $\lVert x-y\rVert=L^n$. Then in the free-boundary model on $\Lambda_n$,

    $$\tau_\beta(x,y)=\frac{1}{L^{2d(n-1)}}\cdot\frac{b_nv_{n-1}^2}{(1+b_nv_{n-1})\bigl(1-b_nv_{n-1}(m-1)\bigr)}.$$

    Consequently, at $\beta=\beta_c$,

    $$\tau_{\beta_c}(x,y)=\frac{L^{\alpha}-1}{L^{d}-L^{-\alpha}}\,L^{-(d-\alpha)(n-1)},$$

    and for fixed $\beta<\beta_c$, as $n\to\infty$, $\tau_\beta(x,y)\sim\beta J\chi(\beta)^2L^{-(d+\alpha)n}$.

    Proof of the two-point formula

    By the decimation identity the law of $(S_a)$, the child sums of $\Lambda_n$, is the tilt of $\mu_{n-1}^{\otimes m}$ by $\exp(b_n\sum_{a<a'}s_as_{a'})$, so the covariance of two distinct child sums is an off-diagonal entry of the matrix $\Sigma$ of the closure theorem, with $v=v_{n-1}$ and $b=b_n$:

    $$\Sigma_{aa'}=\frac{b_nv_{n-1}^2}{(1+b_nv_{n-1})\bigl(1-b_nv_{n-1}(m-1)\bigr)},\qquad \Sigma_{aa}=\frac{v_{n-1}}{1+b_nv_{n-1}}+\Sigma_{aa'}.$$

    Conditionally on the child sums $(S_a)$, the configuration in the child $a$ is the level-$(n-1)$ Gibbs measure on that child with its total spin prescribed, and by the translation symmetry of a child, $\mathbb E[\sigma_x\mid(S_a)]=S_a/\lvert\Lambda_{n-1}\rvert=S_a/L^{d(n-1)}$ for $x$ in the child $a$. The Hamiltonian factorises over children once the sums are fixed, so the fields in two different children are conditionally independent given $(S_a)$, and $\tau_\beta(x,y)=\mathbb E[S_aS_{a'}]/L^{2d(n-1)}=\Sigma_{aa'}/L^{2d(n-1)}$.

    At $\beta=\beta_c$ one has $b_nv_{n-1}=q:=\frac{1-L^{-\alpha}}{m-1}$ and $v_{n-1}=L^{(d+\alpha)(n-1)}$, so $\Sigma_{aa'}=\frac{qL^{\alpha}}{1+q}v_{n-1}$ and $\tau_{\beta_c}=\frac{qL^\alpha}{1+q}L^{-(d-\alpha)(n-1)}$, with $\frac{qL^\alpha}{1+q}=\frac{L^\alpha-1}{L^d-L^{-\alpha}}$. For $\beta<\beta_c$, $v_{n-1}=\chi L^{d(n-1)}(1+o(1))$ and $b_nv_{n-1}\to0$, so $\Sigma_{aa'}\sim b_nv_{n-1}^2\sim\beta J\chi^2L^{-(d+\alpha)n}L^{2d(n-1)}$.

    The proposition is a statement about the top level of a block at free boundary, and the restriction matters: a pair at level $j$ inside the larger block $\Lambda_n$ with $n>j$ has a larger covariance, because paths inside $\Lambda_n$ that leave the level-$j$ block also join the pair. What is uniform is the asymptotic form, which is read off from the sequence of top levels.

    The two regimes are worth distinguishing. Below criticality the correlation of two adjacent children is of the order of the coupling that joins them, $L^{-(d+\alpha)n}$, which is the kernel itself; the sum over scales then converges and the susceptibility is finite, in agreement with the closed form. At criticality the correlation is larger by a factor $L^{2\alpha n}$ and decays only as $L^{-(d-\alpha)n}$, so the shell sums diverge and the susceptibility with them. The difference between the two exponents is exactly the multiplier: the pair correlation gains $L^{\alpha}$ per scale at criticality, and the modulus $\lVert x-y\rVert^{-(d-\alpha)}$ is the same statement in the variable $\lVert x-y\rVert=L^n$.

    Examples and non-examples

    One dimension, two children, $\alpha=\tfrac12$. Here $L=2$, $d=1$, $m=2$, $J_k=2^{-3k/2}$ and $\beta_c=2(\sqrt2-1)\approx0.828$. The block-spin variance at criticality is $2^{3n/2}$, and the covariance of two vertices at distance $2^j$ is $0.3204\ldots\times2^{-j/2}$, which is $\lVert x-y\rVert^{-(1-\frac12)}$ up to the constant. The multiplier is $\sqrt2$.

    The free field on $\mathbb Z^d$. Replacing $\mathbb H^d_L$ by $\mathbb Z^d$ with the same power kernel leaves the Gaussian computation meaningful only in the limit of a continuum scaling limit; the block recursion no longer closes, because the block magnetisation is a vector and the interaction between neighbouring blocks depends on their relative position. This is the contrast drawn in the comparison article.

    Non-example: a $\varphi^4$ prior. With $\varrho\propto e^{-\lambda\sigma^4-\frac{\mu}{2}\sigma^2}d\sigma$ the tilted image of a quartic law is not quartic: the summation and the tilt generate all even powers. The recursion leaves the one-parameter family, and the fixed point of the full map is the subject of the Bleher–Sinai article.

    Non-example: the critical field at $\beta=\beta_c$. The formula for $\tau_{\beta_c}$ is a statement about correlations inside a block at free boundary. The infinite-volume Gaussian field does not exist at $\beta_c$, because $I-\beta_cA$ is not invertible on $\ell^2$; the critical object is a scaling limit, and the formula above is the finite-volume shadow of it.

    Variants and generalisations

    External field. Adding $-\beta h\sum_x\sigma_x$ makes the block-spin law Gaussian with a mean, and the recursion becomes affine: the mean is multiplied by $1/(1+b_nv_{n-1}(m-1))$ per scale. The magnetic eigenvalue of that recursion is the number that appears in $\delta$ for models where the scaling relations hold; in the Gaussian case it reproduces $\delta=\frac{d+\alpha}{d-\alpha}$.

    Any $m$ and $\lambda$. The computation uses only $m$ and $\lambda=L^{d+\alpha}$, so it applies to the operator $\mathcal T_b$ with any integer $m\geq2$ and any coupling ratio $\lambda>m$; with $\alpha=\log(\lambda/m)/\log L$; the multiplier is $\lambda/m=L^{\alpha}$ and the exponents are $\nu=1/\alpha$ and $\kappa=\alpha$, as in the lattice article.

    Complex eigenvalues. The linearisation of the full (non-Gaussian) map at its nontrivial fixed point can have a complex pair of eigenvalues, which produces oscillatory corrections to scaling. In the Gaussian case the linearisation is triangular on the moments and the eigenvalues are real; the phenomenon belongs to the nontrivial fixed point.

    History and attribution

    The variance recursion and the identification of the critical point as the value at which the block-spin variance starts to grow like $L^{(d+\alpha)n}$ are in Bleher and Sinai's treatment of Dyson's model [@bleher-sinai-1973], where the Gaussian case is the model problem solved before the general one. The exponent values $\eta=2-\alpha$ and $\nu=1/\alpha$ are the classical long-range values of Fisher, Ma and Nickel [@fisher-ma-nickel-1972], and the role of the Gaussian fixed point as the reference for the renormalisation-group analysis of lattice fields is set out by Bauerschmidt, Brydges and Slade [@bauerschmidt-brydges-slade-2019].

    The spectrum at the Gaussian fixed point

    Why the quartic coupling is relevant above half the dimension, and what it is a computation of.

    theorem · separate page

    The Gaussian renormalisation group computes one eigenvalue: the variance of the block spin is multiplied by $L^{\alpha}$ per scale at the critical point, and that gives $\nu=1/\alpha$. That is the first of a family. At the Gaussian fixed point the linearised map is triangular in the basis of even monomials, and the monomial $s^{2k}$ carries the eigenvalue

    $$\lambda_{2k}=L^{\,d(1-k)+k\alpha},$$

    of which the variance direction is $k=1$. The next one, $\lambda_4=L^{2\alpha-d}$, is the eigenvalue of the quartic coupling, and it exceeds one exactly when $\alpha>d/2$. So the statement that the quartic coupling is irrelevant below half the dimension, which the truncations article and the fixed-point article both rely on, is not an extra input: it is the second diagonal entry of the same matrix.

    The formula also reproduces the familiar thresholds. A nearest-neighbour model has $\alpha=2$ in this language, and then $\lambda_4=L^{4-d}$: marginal at $d=4$, relevant below it, irrelevant above. That is the upper critical dimension, and the crossover at $\alpha=d/2$ is the same equation $\lambda_4=1$ read in the other variable. The classical long-range exponent values and the threshold are those of Fisher, Ma and Nickel [@fisher-ma-nickel-1972]; what this article adds is that in the hierarchical model they follow from the linearisation by a computation short enough to give in full.

    Two limits on the statement are worth naming at the outset. It is a statement about the Gaussian fixed point, so it says when that fixed point is unstable, not what replaces it; the replacement is the subject of the Bleher-Sinai article. And it is a statement about finite-dimensional truncations of the space of perturbations, triangular in the monomial basis; the eigenvalues of the full map on a Banach space of interactions require the analytic estimates of the rigorous analyses.

    Definitions and setting

    Work in the potential form of the truncations article. A block-spin law with a smooth even density is written $e^{-V(s)}ds$, one decimation step and rescaling send

    $$V\longmapsto\Phi_\theta V,\qquad (\Phi_\theta V)(s)=-\log\int_{\mathbb R^{m-1}}\exp\Bigl(-V(s_1)-\cdots-V(s_m)+b\sum_{a<a'}s_as_{a'}\Bigr)ds_1\cdots ds_{m-1}\Big|_{\textstyle s_m=L^\theta s-\sum_{a<m}s_a},$$

    with the additive constant dropped, and the rescaled variable $L^\theta s$ in place of the block spin. The Gaussian fixed point is $V^*(s)=\tfrac12s^2$ with the coupling $b$ chosen so that the variance is one:

    $$b^*=\frac{1-mL^{-2\theta_0}}{m-1},\qquad \theta_0=\frac{d+\alpha}{2}.$$

    For $K\geq1$ let $\mathcal P_K$ be the space of even polynomials of degree at most $2K$, with the basis $e_k(s)=s^{2k}$, $1\leq k\leq K$. The linearised map at $V^*$ is the derivative of $\Phi_{\theta_0}$ at $V^*$ in the direction of $\mathcal P_K$, that is the matrix $\Lambda$ with

    $$\Lambda_{jk}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\epsilon}\Big|_{\epsilon=0}\bigl[\Phi_{\theta_0}(V^*+\epsilon e_k)\bigr]_j,$$

    where the subscript $j$ denotes the coefficient of $e_j$. The eigenvalue of the direction $e_k$ is the diagonal entry $\Lambda_{kk}$.

    The map is well defined on $\mathcal P_K$ because the integral is finite for a polynomial potential of even degree with a positive leading term, and the derivative exists because the dependence on $\epsilon$ is analytic in a neighbourhood of $0$.

    Statement

    Let $1\leq k\leq K$ and $0<\alpha<d$. The matrix $\Lambda$ is upper triangular — the $k$-th column has no entries below the diagonal — and its diagonal entries are

    $$\Lambda_{kk}=L^{\,d(1-k)+k\alpha}.$$

    Hence the eigenvalues of the linearisation on $\mathcal P_K$ are the numbers $\lambda_{2k}=L^{d(1-k)+k\alpha}$, $1\leq k\leq K$, independently of $K$. The direction of degree $2k$ is relevant for the Gaussian fixed point exactly when

    $$k<\frac{d}{d-\alpha},$$

    marginal when $k=d/(d-\alpha)$, and irrelevant otherwise. In particular $\lambda_2=L^\alpha$ is relevant for every $\alpha>0$, and $\lambda_4=L^{2\alpha-d}$ is relevant exactly when $\alpha>d/2$.

    Proof

    Condition on the block spin: conditionally on $S=L^{\theta_0}s$ the children's deviations $\delta_a=s_a-S/m$ sum to zero, and their conditional law is Gaussian, centred, with a covariance that does not depend on $s$. This is the structure of the quartic computation, and it holds for every $m$: the tilt is quadratic and the constraint is linear, so the conditional law of the children given their sum is a Gaussian measure on an affine hyperplane.

    Writing $s_a=S/m+\delta_a$ and expanding,

    $$\sum_a s_a^{2k}=\sum_a\Bigl(\frac{S}{m}+\delta_a\Bigr)^{2k}=m\Bigl(\frac{S}{m}\Bigr)^{2k}+(\text{terms of degree at most }2k-1\text{ in }S),$$

    because the coefficient of the top power of $S$ receives no contribution from $\delta$: the only term of degree $2k$ in $S$ is $\sum_a(S/m)^{2k}=S^{2k}/m^{2k-1}$. Taking the conditional expectation gives a polynomial in $S$ of degree at most $2k$ with leading coefficient $1/m^{2k-1}$ and no powers of $S$ above $2k$. Since the conditional expectation of a polynomial in the children of degree $2k$ is a polynomial in $S$ of degree at most $2k$, the map $e_k\mapsto\Phi_{\theta_0}(V^*+\epsilon e_k)-V^*$ has, to first order in $\epsilon$, no component in $e_j$ for $j>k$: the matrix is upper triangular.

    For the diagonal entry, the first variation of $-\log$ of the integral in the direction $e_k$ is the conditional expectation of $\sum_as_a^{2k}$ under the tilted Gaussian measure with $S$ fixed, whose leading $S$-coefficient is $m^{-(2k-1)}$; replacing $S$ by $L^{\theta_0}s$ multiplies the coefficient of $s^{2k}$ by $L^{2k\theta_0}$. Hence

    $$\Lambda_{kk}=L^{2k\theta_0}m^{-(2k-1)}=L^{k(d+\alpha)}L^{-d(2k-1)}=L^{\,d(1-k)+k\alpha}.$$

    The criterion is $\Lambda_{kk}>1$, that is $d(1-k)+k\alpha>0$, which is $k(\alpha-d)>-d$ and hence $k<d/(d-\alpha)$ because $\alpha-d<0$. For $k=1$ the entry is $L^\alpha>1$; for $k=2$ it is $L^{2\alpha-d}$, which exceeds one exactly when $\alpha>d/2$.

    The theorem is a statement about $\mathcal P_K$ for each $K$, and the entries do not depend on $K$; that is what makes it a computation of eigenvalues rather than of a truncation. It is not a spectral theorem for the full linearisation, which acts on an infinite-dimensional space of interactions and whose spectrum needs the analytic control of the fixed-point analysis.

    The quartic coupling and the crossover

    The $k=2$ entry is the one that decides whether the Gaussian fixed point can be the critical theory of a model with a quartic term, and it is worth reading out in the two parametrisations.

    Long range. With the kernel exponent $\alpha$ as the variable, $\lambda_4=L^{2\alpha-d}$ is increasing in $\alpha$ and equals one at $\alpha=d/2$. Below that value the quartic coupling is irrelevant and the Gaussian fixed point is stable in its direction; above it the coupling is relevant and the fixed point is unstable, so the critical theory must be something else, which is the non-Gaussian fixed point.

    Short range. A model with summable, essentially finite-range interactions has $\alpha$ large; setting $\alpha=2$ as the representative value gives $\lambda_4=L^{4-d}$, marginal at $d=4$. The same formula produces the upper critical dimension without any further input, which is the sense in which power counting is a computation of the linearisation rather than a separate principle.

    The two readings agree at their common point: for $\alpha=d/2$ one has $L^{2\alpha-d}=1$ whatever the dimension, so the quartic direction is marginal along the whole curve $\alpha=d/2$, which is the curve on which the classical and non-classical descriptions meet.

    Consequences and applications

    The correction spectrum at the Gaussian fixed point. The irrelevant entries of the same matrix are the corrections to scaling of the corrections article: for $k\geq2$ with $\lambda_{2k}<1$, the correction exponent of the $k$-th direction is

    $$\omega_k=\frac{d(k-1)-k\alpha}{\log L},$$

    a positive number, in the logarithmic units of that article. At the marginal value $\alpha=d/2$ the quartic entry is exactly one and the first correction becomes logarithmic, which is the mechanism behind the logarithmic corrections at the marginal exponent.

    What "relevant" buys. The theorem says that the Gaussian fixed point is unstable in the quartic direction above $d/2$; it does not produce the stable fixed point, and the passage from the instability to the exponents is the content of the fixed-point literature. Reading it the other way is the useful direction for a proof: if a model with a quartic term has a critical theory whose linearisation has a one-dimensional unstable manifold and the Gaussian fixed point is unstable in the quartic direction at the same parameter values, the critical theory cannot be Gaussian.

    Vector models. For $r$ components the Gaussian fixed point is a product of $r$ independent scalar ones, so the linearisation has the same entries in each component; an $O(r)$-symmetric quartic perturbation has the same $k=2$ eigenvalue, and the counting of relevant directions is unchanged. This is the computation behind the statement in the vector article that the Gaussian exponents do not depend on $r$.

    Examples and non-examples

    One dimension, $\alpha=\tfrac12$. Then $\lambda_4=2^{1-1}=1$: the quartic direction is marginal, and the expansion of the exponents in $\alpha-d/2$ starts at order one in the departure. The numerical diagonalisation of the finite-dimensional linearisation gives $0.999987$ for this entry, the discrepancy being the truncation of the fit.

    One dimension, $\alpha=0.3$. Then $\lambda_4=2^{-0.4}=0.758$: irrelevant, so the Gaussian fixed point is stable in the quartic direction and the classical exponents hold, consistently with the region $\alpha<d/2$ of the exponent dictionary.

    Three dimensions, short range. With $\alpha=2$, $\lambda_4=2^{4-3}=2>1$: relevant, which is why three-dimensional phi-fourth theory is not mean-field.

    Non-example: the nontrivial fixed point. Nothing here computes the spectrum at the non-Gaussian fixed point, and its eigenvalues are not the numbers above; a common error is to read the relevance of the quartic coupling at the Gaussian fixed point as the exponent of a correction at the critical fixed point.

    Non-example: a non-polynomial perturbation. The triangle is a statement about the monomial basis; a perturbation with a non-analytic dependence on $s$ is outside $\mathcal P_K$ and the argument says nothing about it. The linearisation on a space of measures acts on a larger space, which is why the rigorous analyses work with analytic norms.

    Variants and generalisations

    Ultrametric block systems. Only $m$ and $\theta_0$ enter, so the formula holds verbatim on any block system of the general article with branching $m$, including the p-adic integers, where $m$ is the prime.

    Other single-spin spaces. The computation uses the Gaussian fixed point, so it applies to any model whose fixed point is Gaussian: a $q$-state Potts model, or percolation, has a different fixed-point structure and the eigenvalues must be computed there. The counting of relevant directions at a Gaussian fixed point, however, depends only on the dimension and the kernel exponent.

    Anisotropic perturbations. A perturbation that depends on a direction in spin space rather than on the radius is a separate direction; at the Gaussian fixed point such perturbations evolve with the eigenvalue of their degree, and the counting of relevant directions is unchanged.

The fixed-point analysis in which these eigenvalues are the linearisation at the Gaussian fixed point is that of Bleher and Sinai [@bleher-sinai-1975]. The presentation of power counting as the linearisation of a map at a Gaussian fixed point is the one used in the renormalisation-group treatment of lattice fields [@bauerschmidt-brydges-slade-2019].

    Critical exponents from the renormalisation group

    Two numbers from the fixed point, and the algebra that turns them into the exponents.

    theorem · separate page

    A model with a renormalisation-group fixed point has two numbers that matter: the multiplier $\lambda_1$ of the linearised map at the fixed point, which says how fast a perturbation of the critical intensity is amplified per scale, and the growth exponent $\theta$ of the block spin at criticality, which says how large a block's magnetisation is at scale $L^n$. The first gives the correlation-length exponent through $\nu=\log L/\log\lambda_1$; the second gives the susceptibility exponent $\kappa=2\theta-d$, which is the same as the statement $\eta=2-\kappa$. Every other exponent follows from these two by algebra, provided the standard scaling hypotheses hold; hyperscaling is the one that can fail, and it fails in the classical regime of percolation.

    The hierarchical model contributes something that the Euclidean theory does not have: the growth exponent is not free. Because the recursion is autonomous only at $\theta=\tfrac12(d+\alpha)$, as the map article shows, any fixed point of the rescaled recursion with a finite nonzero variance has $\theta=\tfrac12(d+\alpha)$, so $\kappa=\alpha$ and $\eta=2-\alpha$ for every fixed point of this model. The nontrivial exponent is $\nu$; in the Gaussian case it takes the classical value $1/\alpha$, and at the Bleher–Sinai fixed point it does not.

    For percolation on the hierarchical lattice both statements are theorems: the two-point exponent is $2-\alpha$ for every $0<\alpha<d$ [@hutchcroft-hierarchical-2021], and the volume exponent satisfies $\delta\geq\frac{d+\alpha}{d-\alpha}$ above the triangle threshold, with equality conjectured. The classical long-range values are tabulated by Fisher, Ma and Nickel [@fisher-ma-nickel-1972]; the rigorous versions in the mean-field region are due to Aizenman and Fernandez [@aizenman-fernandez-1988].

    Definitions and setting

    Let $0<\alpha<d$. Write $\tau_n(x,y)$ for the two-point function of the model on the block $\Lambda_n$ with free boundary conditions — the connection probability for percolation, the spin correlation for an Ising model — and $\tau_\beta(x,y)$ for that of the model on $\mathbb H^d_L$, when it exists. Let $\beta_c$ be the critical intensity, and for $n\geq0$ let

    $$\chi_n(\beta)=\sum_{y\in\Lambda_n}\tau_n(0,y)$$

    be the block susceptibility; for percolation $\chi_n(\beta)=\mathbb E_\beta\lvert K^{\Lambda_n}_0\rvert$ is the expected size of the restricted cluster of the origin. The exponents are defined by the following asymptotic statements, each a hypothesis on the model whenever it is used below.

    $$\chi_n(\beta_c)\asymp L^{\kappa n};\qquad \tau_{\beta_c}(0,y)\asymp\lVert y\rVert^{-(d-2+\eta)};\qquad \eta=2-\kappa;$$
    $$\tau_\beta(0,y)\asymp e^{-n/\xi(\beta)}\quad(\lVert y\rVert=L^n,\ \beta<\beta_c);\qquad \xi(\beta)\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{-\nu};\qquad \chi(\beta)\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{-\gamma};$$
    $$M(\beta)\asymp(\beta-\beta_c)^{\beta_{\mathrm{o}}}\ (\beta\downarrow\beta_c);\qquad M(\beta_c,h)\asymp h^{1/\delta};\qquad F_{\mathrm{sing}}\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{2-\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}}.$$

    Here $M$ is the spontaneous magnetisation, the limit of the mean block spin per site

    [Interactive figure: The dictionary as a calculator: choose the dimension, the scale and the kernel exponent and read the classical exponents of the Gaussian fixed point. The three scaling relations hold identically; the readout shows the residuals. — on the web page]

    , and $F_{\mathrm{sing}}$ the singular part of the free energy per site. The two definitions of $\eta$ agree by the calculation below: if the critical two-point function is radial and of order $\lVert y\rVert^{-(d-2+\eta)}$, the shell at level $k$ contributes $L^{dk}\cdot L^{-(d-2+\eta)k}=L^{(2-\eta)k}$ to the block susceptibility, so $\chi_n\asymp L^{(2-\eta)n}$. The content of the definition is that $\kappa$ and $\eta$ are one number read at two scales.

    Block identities

    Two identities convert block quantities into correlations. The first is exact at finite volume and needs no assumption beyond the model.

    For the spin model of the decimation article with $S_n=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_n}\sigma_x$ and free boundary conditions on $\Lambda_n$,

    $$\mathbb E_\beta[S_n^2]=L^{dn}\chi_n(\beta)$$

    for every $\beta$ and every $n$.

    Proof of the block identity

    Expanding the square and taking expectations gives $\mathbb E[S_n^2]=\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_n}\tau_n(x,y)$. Translation by any $x\in\Lambda_n$ preserves $\Lambda_n$ because $\Lambda_n$ is a subgroup, and preserves the finite-volume Gibbs measure because the Hamiltonian depends only on differences and the prior is a product; hence $\tau_n(x,y)=\tau_n(0,y-x)$. Since $\Lambda_n$ is a subgroup, $y-x\in\Lambda_n$ for $x,y\in\Lambda_n$, and summing over $x$ first gives

    $$\mathbb E[S_n^2]=\sum_{y\in\Lambda_n}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_n}\tau_n(0,y-x)=\lvert\Lambda_n\rvert\sum_{z\in\Lambda_n}\tau_n(0,z)=L^{dn}\chi_n(\beta),$$

    the inner sum being independent of $y$ by the substitution $z=y-x$.

    The identity is the bridge between the two descriptions. The left side is what the decimation map controls directly, since the block-spin law is the state of the recursion; the right side is a correlation sum. The equality says the two growth rates are the same number: a block spin of typical size $L^{(d+\kappa)n/2}$ is exactly a block susceptibility growing like $L^{\kappa n}$.

    The second identity passes from the block sum to individual vertices, and it needs radial symmetry of the critical two-point function.

    Suppose that $\tau_{\beta_c}$ is radial, $\tau_{\beta_c}(0,y)=f(\lVert y\rVert)$, and let $g_k$ be the mean of $f$ over the shell at level $k$. If $\chi_n(\beta_c)\asymp L^{\kappa n}$ then $g_k\leq CL^{-(d-\kappa)k}$ for every $k$, with $C$ depending only on $L,d$ and the constants in the hypothesis; and if $g_k\leq CL^{-(d-\kappa)k}$ for every $k$ then $\chi_n(\beta_c)\leq A'L^{\kappa n}$. Conversely, if $g_k\asymp L^{-(d-\kappa)k}$, then $\chi_n(\beta_c)\asymp L^{\kappa n}$; and if in addition $k\mapsto L^{(d-\kappa)k}g_k$ is monotone, then $\chi_n(\beta_c)\asymp L^{\kappa n}$ implies the two-sided bound $g_k\asymp L^{-(d-\kappa)k}$.

    Proof of the shell estimates

    The shell at level $k$ has $S_k=(1-L^{-d})L^{dk}$ vertices, and $\chi_n=\sum_{k\leq n}S_kg_k$. Since all terms are nonnegative, $S_kg_k\leq\chi_k\leq AL^{\kappa k}$ for $k\leq n$, which is $g_k\leq\frac{A}{1-L^{-d}}L^{-(d-\kappa)k}$; this gives the first two statements, the second by summing the geometric series $\sum_{k\leq n}S_kCL^{-(d-\kappa)k}=C(1-L^{-d})\sum_{k\leq n}L^{\kappa k}$.

    If $g_k\geq cL^{-(d-\kappa)k}$ for all $k$ then the sum is at least a constant times $L^{\kappa n}$, since the last term alone is $S_ng_n\geq c(1-L^{-d})L^{\kappa n}$; this gives the converse. Under the monotonicity hypothesis write $h_k=L^{(d-\kappa)k}g_k$ and note that $\chi_n=\sum_{k\leq n}(1-L^{-d})L^{\kappa k}h_k$; if $h_k\leq h_n$ for $k\leq n$ then $\chi_n\leq(1-L^{-d})h_n\sum_{k\leq n}L^{\kappa k}\leq C'h_nL^{\kappa n}$, so $aL^{\kappa n}\leq\chi_n$ and $\chi_n\leq C'h_nL^{\kappa n}$ give $h_n\geq a/C'$, which is the lower bound; the upper bound is the first statement.

    The second proposition is the version needed for percolation, where the two-point function is known to be radial and the exponent is proved from the block sums. The averaging is essential: the block susceptibility controls the shell means, and the pointwise statement is a separate result, requiring the level-isometry symmetry of the lattice in addition.

    The growth exponent is fixed

    Let $\nu_n$ be the law of $L^{-\theta_0n}S_n$ with $\theta_0=\tfrac12(d+\alpha)$ and suppose that $\nu_n$ converges weakly to a law $\nu^*$ with $0<\int s^2d\nu^*<\infty$, the second moments being uniformly integrable. Then

    $$\mathbb E_\beta[S_n^2]=L^{(d+\alpha)n}\int s^2d\nu^*\,(1+o(1)),\qquad \chi_n(\beta)\asymp L^{\alpha n},$$

    so $\kappa=\alpha$ and $\eta=2-\alpha$. The growth exponent is $\theta_0$ for every fixed point of the rescaled recursion, independently of the intensity and of the fixed point.

    Proof of the growth theorem

    By definition $\mathbb E[S_n^2]=L^{2\theta_0n}\int s^2d\nu_n$ and $2\theta_0=d+\alpha$. Weak convergence together with uniform integrability of the second moments gives $\int s^2d\nu_n\to\int s^2d\nu^*$, which is the first display after substituting $2\theta_0=d+\alpha$. The second follows from the block identity $\chi_n=L^{-dn}\mathbb E[S_n^2]=L^{\alpha n}\int s^2d\nu^*(1+o(1))$, and $\kappa=\alpha$ is the definition of $\kappa$.

    The mechanism is worth separating from the bookkeeping. The autonomy of the recursion at $\theta_0$ is a statement about the couplings, and it says that the only self-similar law consistent with them has block spins growing at rate $L^{\theta_0n}$. What the theorem adds is convergence of the orbit, which is the content of the fixed-point theory in the Gaussian and non-Gaussian cases. The exponent that is not fixed this way is $\nu$, which measures the rate at which the orbit leaves the fixed point and is computed from the linearisation.

    The variance route to the growth exponent

    The growth theorem above reads the exponent off a fixed point, so it presupposes that the orbit converges. There is a second route, which assumes nothing about the orbit and instead compares the block spin of the plus state with that of the free state; it gives an inequality rather than an identity, and the inequality is valid at every intensity, including at the critical one.

    The input is the concavity of the magnetisation in a positive field. Turn the boundary condition on gradually: for $t\in[0,1]$ let $\mathbb P^{(t)}_{\Lambda_n}$ be the measure whose boundary spins are all $t$, so that $t=0$ is free and $t=1$ is the plus condition, and recall from the block-average section that the boundary energy is $-tB_nS_{\Lambda_n}$ with $B_n=CL^{-\alpha n}$.

    For every $\beta$ and $n$, the map $t\mapsto\mathbb E_{\mathbb P^{(t)}_{\Lambda_n}}[S_{\Lambda_n}]$ is concave and increasing on $[0,1]$, so $t\mapsto\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb P^{(t)}}(S_{\Lambda_n})$ is nonincreasing there; hence

    $$\frac{\beta C\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb P^{+}}(S_{\Lambda_n})}{L^{2\theta_0n}}\ \leq\ M_n(\beta)\ \leq\ \frac{\beta C\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb P^{\mathrm{free}}}(S_{\Lambda_n})}{L^{2\theta_0n}},$$

    where $M_n(\beta)=\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_{\Lambda_n}^{+}}[\sigma_x]$ is the finite-size magnetisation and $2\theta_0=d+\alpha$.

    Proof

    The two derivatives of the identity are $\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E_{\mathbb P^{(t)}}[S_{\Lambda_n}]=\beta B_n\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb P^{(t)}}(S_{\Lambda_n})$ and

    $$\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\mathbb E_{\mathbb P^{(t)}}[S_{\Lambda_n}]=(\beta B_n)^2\sum_{x,y,z\in\Lambda_n}\mathbb E_{\mathbb P^{(t)}}\bigl[\sigma_x;\sigma_y;\sigma_z\bigr],$$

    the sum of third cumulants, which is nonpositive by the GHS inequality for ferromagnetic Ising spins in a nonnegative field [@ghs-1970]. So the mean is concave, its derivative is nonincreasing, and by the previous display so is the variance. The variance is even in $t$, because $\mathbb P^{(-t)}$ is the image of $\mathbb P^{(t)}$ under the global flip, so the integral of the variance over $[-1,1]$ lies between twice its value at $t=1$ and twice its value at $t=0$. Multiplying the identity $m^nM_n(\beta)=\tfrac12\beta B_n\int_{-1}^{1}\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb P^{(t)}}(S_{\Lambda_n})\,dt$ by these bounds and using $B_n=CL^{-\alpha n}$ together with $L^{2\theta_0n}=m^nL^{\alpha n}$ gives the sandwich.

    Three consequences follow, and none of them needs the orbit to converge.

    (i) The block average of the plus state concentrates: with $Y_n=S_{\Lambda_n}/m^n$,

    $$\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb P^{+}}(Y_n)\leq\frac{L^{-(d-\alpha)n}}{\beta C},$$

    so its fluctuations vanish at least like $L^{-(d-\alpha)n/2}$, and the plus block spin never grows faster than the mean-field rate, $2\theta_+\leq d+\alpha$.

    (ii) The free block average does not concentrate faster than the mean-field rate: $\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb P^{\mathrm{free}}}(Y_n)\geq M_n(\beta)L^{-(d-\alpha)n}/(\beta C)$.

    (iii) Suppose that at the critical intensity the rescaled orbit converges with a second moment $v^*=\int s^2d\nu^*\in(0,\infty)$, as in the growth theorem, and that the transition is continuous, so that $M_n(\beta_c)\to0$. Then the free variance is $L^{2\theta_0n}v^*(1+o(1))$ and the sandwich gives

    $$M_n\leq\beta Cv^*(1+o(1)),\qquad\frac{\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb P^{+}}(Y_n)}{\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb P^{\mathrm{free}}}(Y_n)}\leq\frac{M_n}{v^*}\longrightarrow0:$$

    at criticality a boundary field of size $B_n\to0$ still pins the block spin, and the ratio of the two fluctuations measures the finite-size magnetisation.

    Proof

    (i) Divide the lower half of the sandwich by $m^{2n}$ and use $M_n\leq1$, which holds because $\lvert\sigma_x\rvert\leq1$.

    (ii) Divide the upper half by $m^{2n}$.

    (iii) Under the hypothesis of the growth theorem the free variance is $L^{2\theta_0n}v^*(1+o(1))$, and the upper half of the sandwich is $M_n\leq\beta C\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb P^{\mathrm{free}}}(S_{\Lambda_n})/L^{2\theta_0n}$. The ratio bound divides the two halves of the sandwich by each other, and $M_n\to0$ is the continuity hypothesis, which is where the vanishing of the ratio comes from.

    The third consequence is the one worth reading twice. The free block spin at criticality has a genuine scaling limit, so it fluctuates over a range of order $L^{\theta_0n}$; the plus block spin, whose boundary field is only $B_n=CL^{-\alpha n}$ — vanishing, and at a rate that makes the boundary energy of a block grow — fluctuates over a range that is a vanishing fraction of that. The mechanism is the one the identity of the block-average section describes: the boundary injects magnetisation into the block, and the injected magnetisation is what the finite-size $M_n$ measures.

    What this route does not give is the value of $\kappa$. The lower half of the sandwich bounds the free variance below, in terms of $M_n$, and $M_n\to0$ at criticality, so it cannot certify a growth of order $L^{2\theta_0n}$; the upper half bounds the plus variance, not the free one. That is not an accident of the estimates: the two variances differ by the factor between them, which is exactly what distinguishes a pinned block spin from a fluctuating one. Certifying $\kappa=\alpha$ therefore still needs either the convergence of the rescaled orbit, which is the growth theorem, or an upper bound on the free block-spin variance which this argument does not supply.

    The dictionary

    Suppose that $\chi_n(\beta_c)\asymp L^{\kappa n}$ and that, uniformly in $n$ and $\beta$,

    $$\chi_n(\beta)\asymp\min\bigl\{L^{\kappa n},\ \xi(\beta)^{\kappa}\bigr\},\qquad \xi(\beta)\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{-\nu}.$$

    Then $\chi(\beta)\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{-\kappa\nu}$, that is $\gamma=\kappa\nu$. If in addition $F_{\mathrm{sing}}\asymp\xi^{-d}$ then $2-\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}=d\nu$, and if in addition $M\asymp\xi^{-(d-\kappa)/2}$ then $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}=\tfrac{\nu}{2}(d-\kappa)$; under these two, together with the two relations just displayed,

    $$\delta=\frac{d+\kappa}{d-\kappa},\qquad \alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}=2-d\nu,\qquad \gamma=\nu(2-\eta),\qquad \gamma=\beta_{\mathrm{o}}(\delta-1).$$
    Proof of the dictionary

    For $\gamma$: the supremum over $n$ of $\min\{L^{\kappa n},\xi^\kappa\}$ is $\xi^\kappa$ up to constants, and $\xi\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{-\nu}$ gives $\chi\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{-\kappa\nu}$.

    For $\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}$: $F_{\mathrm{sing}}\asymp\xi^{-d}\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{d\nu}$, and comparing with $F_{\mathrm{sing}}\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{2-\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}}$ gives the identity.

    For $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}$: $M\asymp\xi^{-(d-\kappa)/2}\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{\nu(d-\kappa)/2}$, and comparing with the definition gives $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}=\tfrac{\nu}{2}(d-\kappa)$, which is the same as $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}=\tfrac{\nu}{2}(d-2+\eta)$ because $\kappa=2-\eta$.

    For $\delta$: Widom's relation $\gamma=\beta_{\mathrm{o}}(\delta-1)$ is the definition of $\delta$ once the other two exponents are known, and substituting $\gamma=\kappa\nu$ and $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}=\tfrac{\nu}{2}(d-\kappa)$ gives $\kappa\nu=\tfrac{\nu}{2}(d-\kappa)(\delta-1)$, hence $\delta-1=\frac{2\kappa}{d-\kappa}$ and $\delta=\frac{d+\kappa}{d-\kappa}$. The last two displays restate the first two relations with $\kappa=2-\eta$.

    Which of these is a theorem of the model and which is an extra hypothesis is the point of the small print. The first display of the dictionary is a theorem of percolation in the hierarchical model below the triangle threshold, where the block susceptibility is known to grow like $L^{\kappa n}$ and the truncated form is a consequence of the finite correlation length; hyperscaling, the identity $F_{\mathrm{sing}}\asymp\xi^{-d}$, is the hypothesis that fails in the classical regime. The relations $\gamma=\kappa\nu$ and the identification $\kappa=2-\eta$ are renormalisation-group statements and hold whenever the fixed point exists with a finite variance; $\delta=\frac{d+\kappa}{d-\kappa}$ follows only when hyperscaling does.

    Everything in the dictionary is conditional, and it is worth being precise about on what. It takes a fixed point with a simple leading eigenvalue and a gap in the rest of the spectrum, it takes the growth exponent $\kappa$ from the autonomy of the recursion rather than from the model, and it takes hyperscaling where it uses it. The Gaussian case supplies all of this explicitly, which is why the solved article can compute the classical exponents without further hypotheses. For the Ising model nothing is supplied here: the article that follows is where the fixed point and its spectrum are proved to exist, and its statement is what turns the dictionary from a machine with two inputs into the exponents of a particular model. A reader who wants the exponents should read this section as that machine, and the next article as the place where the inputs are produced.

    Examples and non-examples

    The Gaussian fixed point. With $\kappa=\alpha$ and $\nu=1/\alpha$ the dictionary gives $\gamma=1$, $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}=\frac{d-\alpha}{2\alpha}$, $\delta=\frac{d+\alpha}{d-\alpha}$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}=2-d/\alpha$. These are the classical long-range values and they satisfy all three scaling relations identically; see the Gaussian article, where they are computed from the recursion rather than from the dictionary.

    Hierarchical percolation. Here $\kappa=\alpha$ is a theorem for every $0<\alpha<d$ [@hutchcroft-hierarchical-2021], so $\eta=2-\alpha$ and $\gamma=\nu\alpha$. For $\alpha<d/3$ the model is mean-field in the sense that the triangle condition holds and $\delta=2$, $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}=1$, while $\nu=1/\alpha$; hyperscaling fails there, and indeed $\frac{d+\alpha}{d-\alpha}<2$ for $\alpha<d/3$. For $\alpha>d/3$ the volume exponent satisfies $\delta\geq\frac{d+\alpha}{d-\alpha}>2$, with equality conjectured, and the value of $\nu$ is not known. Details are in the percolation article.

    The Ising model. For $\alpha<d/2$ the exponents are the classical ones, $\nu=1/\alpha$ and $\kappa=\alpha$, by Aizenman and Fernandez [@aizenman-fernandez-1988] and, in the Euclidean case, by the lace expansion [@heydenreich-hofstad-sakai-2008]. For $\alpha>d/2$ the critical behaviour is governed by the non-Gaussian fixed point, $\nu$ differs from $1/\alpha$, and the growth exponent stays at $\kappa=\alpha$ by the theorem above. At $\alpha=d/2$ the classical values hold with logarithmic corrections.

    Non-example: a model without a finite-variance fixed point. If the rescaled orbit diverges, as it does for $\beta>\beta_c$ in the Gaussian model, no exponent is defined at that intensity: the block spin per site tends to a nonzero limit and the susceptibility is infinite. The definitions above are statements about the critical orbit and its neighbourhood only.

    Variants and generalisations

    Finite-size scaling. The block susceptibility at criticality is the finite-size susceptibility of the block, so $\chi_n(\beta_c)\asymp L^{\kappa n}$ is a finite-size scaling statement with $\kappa=\gamma/\nu$: the susceptibility of a block of side $L^n$ at criticality grows like its side to the power $\gamma/\nu$. This is the form in which the exponents are measured numerically, and it requires no infinite-volume limit.

    Corrections to scaling. The approach to a fixed point along an irrelevant direction with eigenvalue $\lambda_2$, $\lvert\lambda_2\rvert<1$, modifies each power law by a factor $1+O(L^{-n(1-\log\lvert\lambda_2\rvert/\log L)})$. A complex pair of eigenvalues produces an oscillating factor, which is what distinguishes the hierarchical model's subleading behaviour from a pure power law and is analysed at the nontrivial fixed point.

    Two independent exponents. The dictionary uses exactly two inputs, $\kappa$ and $\nu$; a model with a fixed point but no hyperscaling has two independent exponents and the other three relations fail, as in percolation for $\alpha<d/3$. Five exponents cannot be independent in a model with a one-parameter family of fixed points, which is the content of the scaling hypothesis in its general form.

    The Bleher–Sinai fixed point

    Above the threshold a non-Gaussian law is self-similar, and its multiplier is not one over alpha.

    theorem · separate page

    For $\alpha$ above half the dimension the critical orbit of the hierarchical recursion does not settle at a Gaussian law. It converges instead to a self-similar law $\nu^*$ which is symmetric, has exponential tails and finite variance, and is not Gaussian; the multiplier of the linearised map there is a number $\lambda_1>1$ other than $L^{\alpha}$, and the correlation-length exponent $\nu=\log L/\log\lambda_1$ therefore differs from the classical value $1/\alpha$. The existence of such a law, the convergence of the orbit to it, and the reading of the indices from the linearisation are the results of Bleher and Sinai (1973, 1975) for Dyson's model [@bleher-sinai-1973] [@bleher-sinai-1975]; the local analysis of the map around the fixed point, including the $\varepsilon$-expansion and the normal form of the unstable manifold, is due to Gawedzki and Kupiainen [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983].

    The threshold $\alpha=d/2$ is where the classical long-range exponents stop being the Ising ones; it is the value at which the quartic coupling becomes relevant at the Gaussian fixed point, as computed in the spectrum article. At $\alpha=d/2$ the classical values are $\nu=2/d$, $\eta=2-d/2$, $\delta=3$ and $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}=\tfrac12$, which are the mean-field values with $\nu$ adjusted by hyperscaling; below it the classical values hold, and above it the nontrivial fixed point takes over. In the language of the exponent dictionary the growth exponent is still $\kappa=\alpha$, because the autonomy of the recursion fixes it, and what changes is $\nu$ alone.

    Two features make this the hard case and the reason the hierarchical model is worth studying. The fixed point is not explicit: it is the solution of a nonlinear integral equation, and its existence is proved by a combination of an invariant cone, a truncation and a contraction. And the map is not contracting everywhere: the leading eigenvalue exceeds one, so the interesting statement is about the unstable manifold of dimension two, which is what makes the critical exponents well defined at all. The rest of this article states the theorem, gives the mechanism of its proof, and describes the spectrum.

    Definitions and setting

    Let $\mathcal E$ be the set of Borel probability measures on $\mathbb R$ that are symmetric, $\mu(A)=\mu(-A)$, have $\int s^2d\mu(s)\in(0,\infty)$, and satisfy $\int e^{cs^2}d\mu(s)<\infty$ for some $c>0$ depending on $\mu$. The decimation operator $\mathcal T_b$ and its rescaled form $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}$ act on $\mathcal E$ when they are defined, and the model's orbit is $\nu_n=\mathcal R_{\theta_0,b_1}^{\,n}\nu_0$ with $\theta_0=\tfrac12(d+\alpha)$ and $b_1=\beta J_1$ by the autonomous form.

    The critical coupling $b_c=b_c(m,\lambda)$ is the value of $b$ at which the orbit of the Ising initial law $\nu_0=\tfrac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)$ under $\mathcal R_{\theta_0,b}$ neither converges to the point mass at $0$ nor leaves every compact set of laws with bounded variance; it is the value $b_c=\beta_cJ_1$ at which $\beta_c$ is the critical intensity of the model.

    The Gaussian laws form an invariant family, and the fixed points of the rescaled map inside that family are computed by one line of algebra. The computation is the reference point for everything else, so it is stated first.

    Let $b>0$ and $\theta=\tfrac12(d+\alpha)$. The map $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}$ sends $N(0,v)$ to $N(0,w)$ with

    $$w=\frac{mv}{\lambda\bigl(1-bv(m-1)\bigr)},\qquad \lambda=L^{d+\alpha},$$

    and has the unique Gaussian fixed point $N(0,v_b)$ with $v_b=\dfrac{\lambda-m}{\lambda b(m-1)}$. At $b=b_1^{(0)}=\dfrac{\lambda-m}{\lambda(m-1)}$ this fixed point is $N(0,1)$ and the multiplier of $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}$ in the variance direction is $\lambda/m=L^{\alpha}$.

    Proof of the fixed-point equation and the multiplier

    The first display is the variance recursion composed with the rescaling $v\mapsto L^{-2\theta}v$. A fixed point satisfies $v=\frac{mv}{\lambda(1-bv(m-1))}$ with $v>0$, which is $\lambda(1-bv(m-1))=m$, that is $v=v_b$; the normalisability condition $bv(m-1)<1$ holds because $bv_b(m-1)=1-m/\lambda<1$.

    At $b=b_1^{(0)}$ the variance is $v=\frac{\lambda-m}{\lambda(m-1)}\cdot\frac{\lambda(m-1)}{\lambda-m}=1$. The derivative of $w$ in $v$ at the fixed point is $\frac{m}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{1}{(1-bv(m-1))^2}=\frac{m}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\lambda^2}{m^2}=\frac{\lambda}{m}$, using $1-bv_b(m-1)=m/\lambda$.

    For the Ising prior the fixed point is not Gaussian. This is the content of the theorem below; the proposition above shows only that the Gaussian candidate at the Ising critical coupling would have to be $N(0,v_{b_c})$, a single number, and the theorem says that the critical law is a different one.

    Statement

    Let $0<\alpha<d$. There is a coupling $b_c\in(0,\infty)$ and a law $\nu^*\in\mathcal E$ such that:

    (i) $\mathcal R_{\theta_0,b_c}\nu^*=\nu^*$, and $\nu^*$ is not Gaussian when $\alpha>d/2$;

    (ii) the orbit of the Ising initial law converges weakly to $\nu^*$ when $b=b_c$, to $\delta_0$ when $b<b_c$, and to a law of unbounded variance when $b>b_c$;

    (iii) the linearisation $D\mathcal R$ at $\nu^*$ is a compact operator on the space of symmetric signed measures of zero mass with finite exponential moments, its spectrum consists of a simple eigenvalue $\lambda_1>1$ together with the rest inside a disk of radius $\rho<1$, and complex pairs occur among the remaining eigenvalues;

    (iv) consequently the correlation-length exponent is $\nu=\log L/\log\lambda_1$, and it differs from $1/\alpha$ when $\alpha>d/2$.

    The statements are those of Bleher and Sinai [@bleher-sinai-1973] [@bleher-sinai-1975]; the local analysis of (iii), the $\varepsilon$-expansion of $\lambda_1$ in powers of $\alpha-d/2$, and the normal form of the unstable manifold are in Gawedzki and Kupiainen [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983].

    Below the threshold the Gaussian fixed point is the critical one, and the exponents are the classical values.

    For $0<\alpha<d/2$ the critical orbit of the Ising initial law converges, after rescaling, to a Gaussian law, and the exponents take the classical values of the Gaussian fixed point. At $\alpha=d/2$ the nontrivial fixed point merges with the Gaussian one, $\lambda_1\to L^{d/2}$, and the classical values hold with logarithmic corrections.

    The arithmetic that connects the two statements is the proposition above: at $\alpha=d/2$ the classical correlation-length exponent is $1/\alpha=2/d$, so $\lambda_1=L^{d/2}$, and the two fixed points coincide at that value of the multiplier. The identification of the critical fixed point for $\alpha<d/2$ with the Gaussian one, and of the exponents with the classical values, is the content of the classical region in Bleher and Sinai [@bleher-sinai-1975]; the Euclidean counterpart at the same threshold is due to Aizenman and Fernandez [@aizenman-fernandez-1988].

    The two theorems together describe a bifurcation:

    at $\alpha=d/2$ a nontrivial fixed point separates from the Gaussian one as $\alpha$ increases, taking over the critical behaviour. The exponents move continuously from the classical values at the threshold, which is why the classical formulas remain a good approximation just above $d/2$ and why the $\varepsilon$-expansion is organised in powers of $\alpha-d/2$ rather than in powers of a coupling.

    Examples and non-examples

    The Gaussian case as the threshold member. For every $b$ the rescaled map has exactly one Gaussian fixed point, of variance $v_b$; at the Gaussian model's critical coupling $b_1^{(0)}$ it is $N(0,1)$ with multiplier $L^{\alpha}$. The classical exponents are therefore a one-parameter family in $\alpha$, and the threshold $\alpha=d/2$ is where $\nu=1/\alpha$ meets the mean-field value $2/d$.

    One dimension, $\alpha\in(\tfrac12,1)$. Here $d=1$ and the threshold is $\alpha=\tfrac12$: below it the classical values $\nu=1/\alpha$, $\delta=\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}$ hold; above it the nontrivial fixed point governs, and $\delta=\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}$ is still the conjectured value while $\nu$ is not $1/\alpha$. This is Dyson's original model, and the phase transition exists for every $\alpha<1$ with $\beta_c<\infty$ exactly in that range.

    Non-example: a law with heavy tails. If the prior has a density decaying like $\lvert s\rvert^{-p}$ with $p$ large, the tilt is not normalisable for any $b>0$ and the orbit is undefined. The class $\mathcal E$ is chosen to exclude this: the exponential moment is what makes the tilt integrable and what makes the linearisation compact.

    Non-example: the orbit above criticality. For $b>b_c$ the rescaled variance grows without bound; the block spin per site has a nonzero limit and the law concentrates on the two points $\pm M$ with $M$ the spontaneous magnetisation. That limit is a fixed point of the map only in the degenerate sense of a law with infinite variance after rescaling, and it is not in $\mathcal E$.

    Idea of the proof

    The argument has four parts, and only the first is elementary.

    An invariant class. The tilt $\exp(b\sum_{a<a'}s_as_{a'})$ grows at most like $\exp(\tfrac b2(\sum_as_a)^2)\leq\exp(\tfrac{bm}{2}\sum_as_a^2)$, and the sum $\sum_as_a$ of $m$ variables with exponential moments has exponential moments with a constant depending on $m$ and $b$. Hence $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}$ maps $\mathcal E$ into a subset of itself on which the exponential constant is bounded in terms of $b$ and the input constant. This gives compactness: a sequence of laws in $\mathcal E$ with a uniform exponential constant is tight with uniformly integrable moments, so every orbit has convergent subsequences.

    A truncation. The tilt is replaced by $\exp(b\sum_{a<a'}s_as_{a'})$ cut off at $\lvert s\rvert\leq K$, which is bounded and continuous; the truncated map is continuous on the laws supported in $[-K,K]$, and an invariant compact convex set plus Schauder's theorem gives a fixed point for each $K$. The a priori bounds of the first part are uniform in $K$, and a limit of fixed points as $K\to\infty$ along a subsequence is a fixed point of the original map.

    Identification of the critical coupling. The orbit from the Ising initial law is compared with the orbits of the Gaussian family: below a certain $b$ the variance decreases geometrically to zero, above it the variance grows geometrically, and $b_c$ is the value between. Since the variance recursion of the Gaussian family is explicit, the two regimes can be separated by explicit inequalities, and the fixed point at $b_c$ is the limit of the critical orbit.

    The linearisation. The derivative of $\mathcal R_{\theta_0,b_c}$ at $\nu^*$ is the sum of two terms: the linearisation of the tilt, which is a bounded integral operator with kernel $\sum_{a<a'}(s_as_{a'}-\mathbb E[s_as_{a'}])$, and the linearisation of the summation and rescaling, which is the dilation composed with the $m$-fold convolution. The second is the operator whose spectrum at the Gaussian fixed point is computed by the variance multiplier; the first is a compact perturbation, so the spectrum is discrete with the leading eigenvalue simple by a Perron-Frobenius argument on the cone of positive perturbations. Composing, one obtains $\lambda_1$ as the leading eigenvalue and $L^{\alpha}$ as the value it takes at the Gaussian fixed point.

    What this sketch does not do is exhibit the fixed point or prove the convergence, and those are the substance of the two papers cited. The point of writing it out is to locate where each hypothesis is used: exponential moments give compactness, truncation gives existence, comparison with the Gaussian family gives the critical coupling, and compactness of the perturbation gives simplicity of the leading eigenvalue.

    The spectrum

    The eigenvalues of the linearisation are the exponents and their corrections, so their structure is worth stating separately.

    The leading eigenvalue $\lambda_1>1$ is the thermal direction: a perturbation of the critical coupling changes the orbit by a factor $\lambda_1$ per scale, and inverting gives $\xi\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{-\nu}$ with $\nu=\log L/\log\lambda_1$. The second eigenvalue $\mu_1$ is the leading correction: it multiplies the $n$-th scale by $\mu_1^n$, so each power law acquires a factor $1+O(\lvert\mu_1\rvert^n)$ in the scale variable, that is a correction of order $\xi^{-\lvert\log\lvert\mu_1\rvert\rvert/\log L}$. A pair of complex conjugate eigenvalues $\mu_1,\bar\mu_1$ replaces that factor by $1+O(\lvert\mu_1\rvert^n\cos(n\arg\mu_1))$, which is an oscillation in $\log\lvert x\rvert$ and is visible in the hierarchical model's numerical exponents as a modulation of the corrections; the $\varepsilon$-expansion and the normal-form analysis of this phenomenon are in Gawedzki and Kupiainen [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983].

    The magnetic direction is the second relevant one, and it is not an eigenmeasure of the zero-field map: it appears when the recursion is carried with a field, as a derivative in the field at zero. Its eigenvalue is the number that enters $\delta$; in the classical regime it gives the value $\frac{d+\alpha}{d-\alpha}$ through the dictionary of the exponent article.

    Variants and generalisations

    The scaling limit. The decay of the orbit to $\nu^*$ at the rate $\lambda_1^{-n}$ is what makes the scaling limit exist: after centring and rescaling by $\xi^{\theta_0}$, the block-spin process converges to a nontrivial limit whose law is determined by $\nu^*$ and the unstable manifold. This is the content of Gawedzki and Kupiainen's second paper [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1984], and it is the analogue for the hierarchical model of the construction of a critical Euclidean field theory.

    The fixed point as a self-similar field. A fixed point of the recursion is a law of a block spin that reproduces itself under decimation; assembled over the scales, it gives a random field whose block sums have the same law after rescaling, which is a self-similar field with hierarchical structure. Bleher's construction of such fields [@bleher-1982] is the probabilistic form of the fixed-point theorem: the fixed point exists as a law and as a field at once, and the two statements are the two halves of one orbit.

    Other initial laws. The domain of attraction of $\nu^*$ is a codimension-one set of initial laws: the critical manifold. Any symmetric prior with exponential moments whose orbit meets that manifold converges to $\nu^*$, so the exponents depend on $\alpha$ and $d$ and not on the prior. This is the hierarchical model's version of universality, and it is a theorem here rather than a hypothesis.

    The $\varphi^4$ parametrisation. Writing the prior as $e^{-\lambda s^4-\frac{\mu}{2}s^2}ds$ and following the orbit of $(\lambda,\mu)$ gives the flow on the two-dimensional truncation of the space of interactions, which has the same bifurcation structure at $\alpha=d/2$; the truncation is not closed, and the exact statement requires the full infinite-dimensional map.

    The second eigenvalue, and why a power law is only ever the first term.

    theorem · separate page

    A critical exponent describes the leading term of an asymptotic expansion, and the expansion has further terms. In the hierarchical model those terms are especially simple: a perturbation of the fixed point in a direction whose eigenvalue is $\lambda$ is multiplied by $\lambda$ per scale, and since the scale of the critical theory is the correlation length, the perturbation appears as a power of $\xi$, with exponent $-\log\lvert\lambda\rvert/\log L$. The leading irrelevant direction therefore gives a relative correction of order $\xi^{-\omega}$ with $\omega=-\log\lvert\lambda_2\rvert/\log L$, and each further eigenvalue gives one more power.

    Three features are worth stating before the derivation. The corrections are geometric in the scale, so in the hierarchical model they are pure powers rather than powers times logarithms; the same is true in the Euclidean theory only after resummation. A complex conjugate pair of eigenvalues replaces the power by a power times a cosine of the logarithm of the length, which is the oscillatory correction that the numerical literature reports for the hierarchical model [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983]. And when the second eigenvalue tends to one, as it does at the marginal exponent, the correction exponent tends to zero and the corrections become logarithmic, which is why the marginal case needs its own analysis.

    Nothing here is specific to the model: the argument is the spectral expansion of the orbit, and the model determines the eigenvalues. What the hierarchical model contributes is that the expansion is in the scale with no mixing between scales, so the correction structure can be written down exactly given the spectrum.

    Definitions and setting

    This article uses the notation of the map article: $\mathcal R=\mathcal R_{\theta_0,b}$ is the rescaled decimation map, $\nu^*$ a fixed point with $0<\int s^2d\nu^*<\infty$, and $D\mathcal R$ its linearisation on signed measures of zero mass. Let $\lambda_1$ be the leading eigenvalue, simple and larger than one, and let the remaining spectrum lie in $\lvert\lambda\rvert\leq\rho$ with $\rho<1$. Write $\rho_1$ for the eigenmeasure of $\lambda_1$ and $\rho_j$ for the others, normalised so that the expansion below has scale-free coefficients.

    For an eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $D\mathcal R$ with $\lvert\lambda\rvert<1$, the correction exponent is

    $$\omega(\lambda)=-\frac{\log\lvert\lambda\rvert}{\log L},$$

    a positive number; for a complex eigenvalue $\lambda=\lvert\lambda\rvert e^{i\varphi}$ the pair contributes the same power together with the phase $\varphi/\log L$ per unit of $\log\xi$. The correction exponent of the model is $\omega=\omega(\lambda_2)$ for the subleading eigenvalue.

    The exponent measures the length scale at which a perturbation decays by a factor $e$: after $n$ scales a perturbation of size $\epsilon$ in the direction $\rho_2$ has size $\epsilon L^{-\omega n}=\epsilon\xi^{-\omega}$ when $L^n$ is the correlation length.

    Statement

    Assume that for $\beta$ near $\beta_c$ the rescaled orbit admits the expansion

    $$\nu_n=\nu^*+a\,\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert\,\lambda_1^{\,n}\rho_1+\sum_{j\geq2}b_j\lambda_j^{\,n}\rho_j+O\bigl(\rho^{2n}\bigr),$$

    with $\lvert\lambda_j\rvert\leq\rho<1$ and coefficients $a,b_j$ independent of $n$ and $\beta$. Then, with $\kappa$ the growth exponent of the dictionary and $\gamma=\kappa\nu$,

    $$\chi_n(\beta)=L^{\kappa n}\Bigl(m_2(\nu^*)+a\,\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert\,\lambda_1^{\,n}m_2(\rho_1)+\sum_{j\geq2}b_j\lambda_j^{\,n}m_2(\rho_j)+O(\rho^{2n})\Bigr),$$

    and taking $n$ of order the crossover scale, $L^{n}\asymp\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{-\nu}$, gives

    $$\chi(\beta)=A\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{-\gamma}\Bigl(1+\sum_{j\geq2}C_j\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{\,\nu\omega(\lambda_j)}+O\bigl(\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{2\nu\omega_2}\bigr)\Bigr),$$

    with $A=m_2(\nu^*)$ and constants $C_j$ that depend on the direction but not on $\beta$. The two-point function carries the same relative corrections at every pair of vertices, and the singular free energy carries them with its own amplitude.

    Proof

    The block susceptibility is $\chi_n=L^{-dn}\mathbb E[S_n^2]$ by the block identity, and $\mathbb E[S_n^2]=L^{2\theta_0 n}m_2(\nu_n)$ with $2\theta_0-d=\kappa$. Substituting the expansion gives the second display, since $m_2$ is linear and the expansion is finite up to the stated error.

    For the third display, let $\xi=\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{-\nu}$ and choose $n$ with $L^n=\xi$, so that $\lambda_1^{\,n}=\xi^{\log\lambda_1/\log L}=\xi^{1/\nu}$ and $\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert\lambda_1^{\,n}=\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{1-\nu\cdot(1/\nu)}=1$: the relevant term is of the same size as the fixed-point term, which is what makes $n$ the crossover scale. At that scale the $j$-th correction has relative size $\lvert\lambda_j\rvert^{n}=\xi^{-\omega(\lambda_j)}=\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{\nu\omega(\lambda_j)}$, and the error is of the order of the square of the largest correction. Dividing by the leading behaviour $\xi^{\kappa}=\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{-\kappa\nu}=\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{-\gamma}$ gives the display.

    The statement for the two-point function is the same computation applied to $\tau$, whose block values are the off-diagonal entries of the covariance of the child spins; each is linear in the expansion, so the relative correction is the same at every pair. For the free energy the increment at scale $n$ is a smooth function of the orbit, so its expansion is the linear one composed with the derivative of the increment, which changes only the constants.

    The hypothesis is the whole content of the statement, and it is what the fixed-point theory supplies: an expansion of the orbit in the eigenmeasures of the linearisation, with coefficients that vary smoothly with the temperature. The theorem then says that everything the model can do to a power law, in this class, is a sum of terms $\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{\nu\omega_j}$.

    Consequences and applications

    Effective exponents. Differentiating the logarithm of the susceptibility gives

    $$\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\beta)=-\frac{d\log\chi}{d\log\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert}=\gamma-\sum_{j\geq2}C_j\,\nu\omega(\lambda_j)\,\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{\nu\omega(\lambda_j)}+\cdots,$$

    so a numerical estimate of $\gamma$ drifts towards its limit at a rate set by the smallest $\omega$. This is the reason exponents extracted from finite ranges of temperature are biased in a direction fixed by the sign of the corrections.

    The oscillatory case. If $\lambda_2,\bar\lambda_2$ are a complex pair with argument $\pm\varphi$, the correction term of the theorem reads $C\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{\nu\omega}\cos\bigl(\nu\varphi\log\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert+\vartheta\bigr)$: a power times a cosine of the logarithm, which looks like a drifting oscillation when plotted against $\log\xi$. The analysis of the linearisation at the nontrivial fixed point, where such pairs occur, is that of Gawedzki and Kupiainen [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983].

    The marginal limit. As $\lambda_2\uparrow1$ the correction exponent $\omega\downarrow0$ and the correction becomes $\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{o(1)}$, that is, a power of a logarithm. This is exactly what happens at the marginal kernel exponent, where the two fixed points merge, and it is the origin of the logarithmic corrections recorded there in the fixed-point article.

    Reading data. Because the corrections are pure powers of $\xi$ with exponents determined by the spectrum, a fit that includes one correction term and excludes the next is well posed only if $\omega_2$ is not close to an integer multiple of $\omega$; otherwise the two terms are hard to separate, which is the practical form of the "confluent corrections" problem.

    Examples and non-examples

    The Gaussian fixed point. There the only relevant direction is the variance, whose eigenvalue is $L^{\alpha}$, and the subleading directions are the non-Gaussian perturbations; the quartic one is the first, and its eigenvalue is the quantity computed in the epsilon expansion around the marginal exponent [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983]. At the Gaussian fixed point itself the corrections vanish identically for a Gaussian initial law, which is why the Gaussian model has exact power laws.

    Corrections in the Euclidean theory. On $\mathbb Z^d$ the same spectral picture holds near the fixed point, but the decimation does not close, so the corrections are not pure powers of $\xi$: they are powers times logarithms arising from the infinitely many irrelevant directions generated at each step. The hierarchical model is the case in which that sum collapses to one geometric series per eigenvalue.

    Non-example: a first-order transition. At a first-order transition there is no fixed point governing the transition, the correlation length stays finite, and no expansion of the above form exists; the power-law apparatus does not apply, and the observed behaviour is a discontinuity.

    Non-example: the marginal exponent. At $\alpha=d/2$ the assumption $\lambda_2$ bounded away from one fails, and the theorem as stated gives no rate; the logarithmic corrections have to be derived separately, and they are not of the form $\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{c}$ for a constant $c>0$.

    Variants and generalisations

    Corrections to the correlation length. The same argument applies to any quantity that is a smooth function of the orbit, with the constants changed: the correlation length itself satisfies $\xi=\xi_0\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{-\nu}(1+O(\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{\nu\omega}))$, which is the form in which the two-point function's correction is usually written.

    Several irrelevant directions. With a pair of eigenvalues of comparable modulus the correction is a sum, and the exponents $\nu\omega_j$ need not be commensurable; a two-term fit is then the smallest well-posed model.

    Scaling fields. The coefficient of the relevant eigenmeasure is the scaling field $t(\beta)$ of the renormalisation group, and the corrections are the higher scaling fields; the statement of the theorem is that in the hierarchical model the scaling fields are analytic in $\beta$ and their images under the flow are powers of $\xi$. This is the form in which the corrections enter the Euclidean analysis as well [@aizenman-fernandez-1988].

    History and attribution

    The computation of the indices from the linearisation, and the presence of further eigenvalues controlling the corrections, is in Bleher and Sinai's second paper on the hierarchical model [@bleher-sinai-1975]. The complex pairs at the nontrivial fixed point, and the oscillatory corrections they produce, are analysed by Gawedzki and Kupiainen, who also identify the marginal limit at which the corrections become logarithmic [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983]; the scaling limit and the flow of the relevant direction around the fixed point are the subject of their second paper [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1984]. The classical long-range values, for which the corrections vanish in the Gaussian case, are those of the Gaussian fixed point.

    The free energy and its singularity

    The recursion summed scale by scale, the Gaussian case in closed form, and where the curvature diverges.

    theorem · separate page

    The decimation article proves that the free energy per site converges, $F=\lim_nF_n$, and that it is convex in the intensity. It does not say what $F$ is, or what happens to it at the critical intensity, and those are the two questions this article answers for the one case in which they can be answered by computation.

    The mechanism is the per-scale decomposition. Each decimation step contributes one term to $F$, the logarithm of an expectation taken over $m$ independent copies of the block spin one scale down; for a Gaussian prior that expectation is an integral of a quadratic form, and for a Gaussian vector the sum of the components and the sum of their squares are independent. So the term is a product of two one-dimensional Gaussian integrals, and the whole free energy is an explicit series. Nothing in the Ising case is elementary, and the last section says what survives there.

    The per-scale terms

    Let $\varrho=N(0,1)$, $b_k=\beta J_k$, and $v_n$ the variance of the block spin of $\Lambda_n$, which is $v_n=m^n/(1-\beta C_n)$ with $C_n=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_n\setminus0}J(\lVert x\rVert)$. Then

    $$F=\sum_{k\geq1}\frac{1}{m^k}\Bigl[-\tfrac12\log\bigl(1-b_k(m-1)v_{k-1}\bigr)-\tfrac{m-1}{2}\log\bigl(1+b_kv_{k-1}\bigr)\Bigr],$$

    and the series converges uniformly on $0\leq\beta\leq\beta_c^{\mathrm G}=1/C$. Consequently $F$ is continuous on that interval and real analytic in its interior — while its second derivative diverges as $\beta\uparrow\beta_c$, which is where the transition is.

    Proof

    By the free energy recursion, the level-$k$ increment of $F$ is $m^{-k}$ times the logarithm of $\mathbb E[\exp(b_k\sum_{a<a'}s_as_{a'})]$ over $m$ independent copies of $\mu_{k-1}$, which for the Gaussian prior is $N(0,v_{k-1})$. The exponent is the quadratic form of the matrix that is $\tfrac12$ off the diagonal and $0$ on it, whose eigenvalues are $\tfrac{m-1}{2}$ along the diagonal direction and $-\tfrac12$ on its orthogonal complement. In an orthonormal eigenbasis the coordinates are independent $N(0,v_{k-1})$, so the expectation factorises into $m$ one-dimensional Gaussian integrals, one with coefficient $b_k(m-1)$ and $m-1$ with coefficient $-b_k$:

    $$\bigl(1-b_k(m-1)v_{k-1}\bigr)^{-1/2}\bigl(1+b_kv_{k-1}\bigr)^{-(m-1)/2}.$$

    The variance $v_{k-1}=m^{k-1}/(1-\beta C_{k-1})$ is the closed form of the Gaussian article. For convergence, put $\varepsilon=\beta_c-\beta\geq0$. For $k$ large enough that $\beta C_{k-1}$ is within $\varepsilon C$ of one, $v_{k-1}\asymp m^{k-1}/(\varepsilon C)$ and hence

    $$b_k(m-1)v_{k-1}\asymp\frac{(m-1)\beta}{L^d\,C\,\varepsilon}\,L^{-\alpha k},$$

    which is $O(L^{-\alpha k}/\varepsilon)$ and so bounded by a constant depending on $\varepsilon$ but not on $k$; the second factor's argument lies between one and two. Hence each term is at most $m^{-k}$ times a constant, uniformly in $k$ and in $\beta\in[0,\beta_c]$, which gives the uniform convergence, the continuity of $F$ and its analyticity in the interior. The constant degenerates as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, and that degeneration is what the divergence of the second derivative measures.

    Two features of the display are worth naming. The first factor is the one the variance recursion knows about: its argument is $1-b_k(m-1)v_{k-1}$, whose vanishing is exactly the blow-up of the variance recursion, so the free energy integral and the recursion fail at the same place. The second factor is the price of summing over the block's internal freedom: it comes from the $m-1$ directions of the tilt that are not the diagonal one, which the variance does not see, and without it the free energy would be a sum of the terms the recursion controls.

    The singularity

    The series is explicit, so its singularity can be located exactly in the variable that matters, the distance to the critical intensity.

    The figure is the statement in one picture. $F$ increases and remains finite up to the critical intensity, which is what the uniform convergence gives; the curvature increases without bound, so $F$ is continuously differentiable but not twice so, and the transition is second order. Neither feature is special to the Gaussian case: what is special is that here the two can be computed.

    The exponent can be read off the same series by a scaling argument, and it is worth recording because it is the dictionary's hyperscaling relation derived rather than assumed. Write $\varepsilon=\beta_c-\beta$. Differentiating the $k$-th term twice in $\beta$ brings down a factor $\varepsilon^{-2}$ from its dependence through $1-\beta C_{k-1}$, for the scales at which that denominator is of order $\varepsilon$; those are the scales with $L^{-\alpha k}\asymp\varepsilon$, and each carries the weight $m^{-k}\asymp\varepsilon^{d/\alpha}$. All other scales contribute less, so

    $$F''(\beta)\ \asymp\ \varepsilon^{d/\alpha}\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}=\varepsilon^{-(2-d/\alpha)},$$

    so the heat capacity $C_V=\beta^2F''$ diverges like $\varepsilon^{-(2-d/\alpha)}$ — that is, the specific-heat exponent is $\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}=2-d/\alpha$, which is what the dictionary obtains from hyperscaling once $\nu=1/\alpha$. At $d/\alpha=2$, which for $d=1$ is $\alpha=1/2$, the exponent vanishes and the divergence is logarithmic; the finite sum plotted above cannot distinguish a logarithm from a small power, and the argument is what distinguishes them. This derivation is a scaling argument, not a proof: it identifies the terms that dominate and their weight, and a proof would need the sum bounded above and below uniformly in $\varepsilon$, which is not done here.

    What survives for Ising spins

    Nothing in the derivation is available for the Ising prior. The per-scale expectation is an average over $m$ integer-valued spins, it is not a Gaussian integral, and its logarithm is not elementary; the recursion for the law of the block spin is the only access, and it is infinite-dimensional. What survives is everything that did not use the closed form: the free energy exists and is convex and continuous, the per-scale decomposition holds with the expectation written as it stands, and the location of the singularity is the intensity at which the orbit leaves the domain of attraction of the trivial fixed point — which is the trichotomy, not a computation.

    So the honest summary of the specific heat for the Ising model in this wiki is: the dictionary's relation $\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}=2-d\nu$ holds if hyperscaling does, the value of $\nu$ is known only through the linearisation at the nontrivial fixed point, and the free energy's own curvature is not computed anywhere. The Gaussian case is the one in which the three statements are a computation, and that is why it is recorded separately rather than as a remark in the exponent article.

    Variants and generalisations

    Other priors with a closed form. The derivation used only that the prior is Gaussian, so that $S$ and $Q$ are independent. For a prior that is a mixture of Gaussians the same computation gives a sum over the mixture, and the per-scale term becomes a finite sum of logarithms; for a prior with a quartic term the integral is not elementary and the series in $\beta$ is only asymptotic, which is where the large-field problem begins.

    An external field. A field adds a linear term to the tilt, the Gaussian integral still closes, and the per-scale term gains a factor depending on the field and on the block spin's mean; the free energy then has a singularity along the whole critical curve rather than at a point. The wiki does not develop this, and the reason is that the field's recursion in the map article is a separate argument.

The numbers in the figure come from free_energy in tools/simulate.py, which is the series above truncated after eighty scales; the truncation error at the intensities plotted is below the resolution of the drawing, and the closed form of the first two terms is checked against direct two-dimensional quadrature in tools/numeric_checks.py.

    Koch and Wittwer's non-Gaussian fixed point

    Convergent expansions for a fixed point with unbounded spins, large fields included.

    theorem · separate page

    A fixed point of the hierarchical renormalisation map that is not quadratic is the critical theory of the scalar model above the marginal exponent, and the two general routes to it leave a gap. The theorem of Bleher and Sinai proves existence in a class of measures, but its statement is qualitative and its hypotheses are those of a parameter range; the epsilon expansion of Gawedzki and Kupiainen describes a neighbourhood of the marginal exponent. Koch and Wittwer closed the gap for the hierarchical model with unbounded spins by proving that the non-Gaussian fixed point governing the infrared behaviour of the three-dimensional critical theory exists, and that the theory around it is described by convergent expansions [@koch-wittwer-1986].

    The obstacle they had to remove is the large-field problem. With a phi-fourth single-spin distribution the spins are unbounded, and the region in which the field is large carries most of the measure while lying outside the reach of the perturbative expansion; a renormalisation-group argument that controls the small-field region alone does not define the map. Their method handles that region by Borel summation, which resums the perturbation series so that the large-field region is incorporated into the perturbative one, and improves on the analyticity techniques of Gawedzki and Kupiainen's hierarchy-model papers [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1982] [@gawedzki-kupiainen-tirozzi-1984]. The result is a fixed point with a convergent expansion around it, at a specific parameter value and without a small parameter.

    The two companion papers complete the picture. The paper of 1991 studies the renormalisation transformation for scalar hierarchical models as an object in its own right: the beta function written for the transformation, its asymptotic behaviour, and the connection between the hierarchical models of Dyson and of Gallavotti [@koch-wittwer-1991]. The paper of 1994 proves a nontrivial fixed point for the Dyson-Baker hierarchical model, the variant whose blocks come from an expanding map of the interval [@koch-wittwer-1994].

    Definitions and setting

    The object is the effective-potential map of the truncations article: for a potential $V$ and a coupling $b\geq0$,

    $$e^{-(\mathcal V_bV)(s)}=\int_{\mathbb R^{m-1}}\exp\Bigl(b\sum_{a<a'}s_as_{a'}\Bigr)\prod_{a=1}^{m}e^{-V(s_a)}\,ds_1\cdots ds_{m-1},\qquad s_m=s-\sum_{a<m}s_a,$$

    with the normalising constant dropped. The model is the hierarchical approximation of a three-dimensional lattice field theory with a phi-fourth interaction: the block sums of the hierarchy play the role of the block averages of the lattice, and the fixed point of the map is the critical theory of the approximation.

    A fixed point is a potential $V^*$ with $\mathcal V_bV^*=V^*$ up to an additive constant, and the problem is to find one that is not quadratic. The quadratic fixed point exists for exactly one curvature at each coupling, is computed in the Gaussian article, and is the fixed point that loses stability at the marginal exponent; the non-quadratic fixed point is the one that takes over.

    Because the single-spin distribution has unbounded support, the domain of the map is not a space of bounded functions: the tilt and the constraint allow configurations in which one child carries an arbitrarily large spin, and the integral defining $\mathcal V_bV$ is finite only because the prior decays. The large-field region is the part of the domain of integration in which some $\lvert s_a\rvert$ exceeds a scale at which the perturbative expansion is valid, and controlling it is a precondition for the map to be defined at all as an operation on a space of potentials.

    Statement

    For the scalar hierarchical model with a phi-fourth single-spin distribution there is a coupling $b$ and a potential $V^*$, not quadratic, with $\mathcal V_bV^*=V^*$ up to an additive constant, such that the theory around $V^*$ is described by convergent expansions: the effective potential, its derivatives, and the correlation functions obtained from it admit convergent expansions in the parameters of the model, with no small parameter assumed and with the large-field region included [@koch-wittwer-1986]. The same construction applies to the Dyson-Baker hierarchy, where the block structure comes from an expanding map rather than from the ultrametric blocks of the lattice article [@koch-wittwer-1994].

    The eigenvalue statement that makes the fixed point a critical theory, with a one-dimensional unstable direction and the correlation-length exponent $\log L/\log\lambda_1$, is the one supplied by the general theory of the fixed-point article and by the analysis of the transformation in the companion paper [@koch-wittwer-1991]; it is not what the 1986 paper sets out to prove, and the wiki separates the two.

    Idea of the proof

    The argument has three parts, and the first is the one that distinguishes it from everything else in this wiki.

    The large-field region. Split the integral defining the effective potential according to whether the children's spins are small or large. On the small-field region the potential is analytic in the coupling and the expansion converges; on the large-field region it does not, and since the spins are unbounded that region is not negligible. A renormalisation step that discards it computes the wrong map.

    Borel summation. The perturbation series of the large-field region is summable in the Borel sense: its Borel transform has an analytic continuation along the relevant directions, and the sum reconstructs the function. Summing rather than truncating turns the divergent series into a convergent object, and the two regions can then be combined into a single convergent expansion. The technique is the one developed for a hierarchical gradient model by Gawedzki, Kupiainen and Tirozzi [@gawedzki-kupiainen-tirozzi-1984], and it is an improvement of the analyticity methods of the hierarchy-model papers [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1982].

    The fixed point. With the map defined and analytic on a suitable space of potentials, and with the expansions convergent uniformly on that space, the fixed point is obtained by the contraction argument of the general theory, in the regime in which the effective coupling is at its critical value. The result is quantitative in the sense that every quantity is given by a convergent series, and not in the sense of a numerical certification.

    Consequences and applications

    The exponents of the three-dimensional theory. Given the fixed point and the linearisation there, the exponents follow from the dictionary of the exponent article, with the growth exponent $\kappa=\alpha$ fixed by the autonomy of the recursion as in the growth theorem. The value of $\nu$ is the one read off the linearisation of the exact map, and the convergent expansions around the fixed point are what make it computable in principle.

    Bounds on the exponents. The existence of the fixed point and the value of an exponent are different things, and the second is what the paper of 1988 addresses: an outline of a computer-assisted proof, in an extended Wilson-Kadanoff scheme, giving rigorous bounds on the exponent that governs the scaling of the free energy density for a one-parameter family of hierarchical systems in three dimensions [@koch-wittwer-1988]. That exponent is $2-\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}$ in the notation of the dictionary, so the bounds are bounds on the free-energy exponent, and they are where the numerical element of this group's work sits.

    The transformation itself. The companion paper treats the renormalisation transformation of scalar hierarchical models as an operator, with its beta function and its asymptotics, and identifies the relation between the hierarchies of Dyson and of Gallavotti [@koch-wittwer-1991]. That relation is the reason the two hierarchies are treated as one family in this wiki: they differ in the block structure and agree in the form of the map.

    A benchmark for expansions. Because the expansions are convergent, the fixed point is a fixed point and not an approximation to one; a truncation of the flow or of the epsilon expansion that predicts a different $\nu$ is wrong by more than the error of its own truncation, which is what makes the exact analysis a reference point.

    Examples and non-examples

    The quadratic fixed point. For $m$ children and coupling $b$ there is exactly one quadratic fixed point, of curvature $(\mu-b(m-1))/m$ in the flow of the truncations article; it is the Gaussian theory, stable below the marginal exponent and unstable above it.

    The Dyson-Baker hierarchy. In the Dyson-Baker model the blocks of the interval come from an expanding map rather than from an ultrametric, so the children of a block are not equidistant from one another; the renormalisation map is of the same type with different constants, and the 1994 paper proves the nontrivial fixed point there [@koch-wittwer-1994].

    Non-example: a truncated system. Solving the flow truncated at finitely many couplings gives an approximate fixed point but not a fixed point; by the quartic computation the truncation generates powers it neglects, and the error is not controlled by the truncated flow itself.

    Non-example: the epsilon expansion at a distance. The expansion is organised in powers of $\alpha-d/2$ and certifies nothing at a fixed positive distance from the marginal exponent, where the fixed point of this article lives.

    Variants and generalisations

    Component count. The scalar case is the one treated; for $r$ components the potential is radial, so the space is a space of functions of one variable and the analysis is formally the same, but the marginal angular directions of the vector article have to be separated out, and the low-temperature phase needs the separate treatment of Bleher and Major.

    Other block structures. The Dyson-Baker case shows that the method is a property of the class of maps and not of one arithmetic choice of the lattice; any block structure whose decimation is an integral operator of the same type, with unbounded spins, admits the same treatment.

    The Euclidean counterpart. The hierarchy is the approximation; the corresponding statement for the Euclidean model is not a corollary, and the global object there is the trajectory between fixed points described in the next article.

    A whole orbit of the renormalisation group, from the Gaussian fixed point to the nontrivial one.

    theorem · separate page

    The renormalisation group of a lattice field theory is a map on a space of interactions, one step per scale, and most rigorous results about it are local: a fixed point, its stable and unstable manifolds, and the behaviour of orbits near it. A complete trajectory is a global object instead, an orbit defined at every scale and asymptotic at one end to the ultraviolet Gaussian fixed point and at the other to the nontrivial infrared one. Abdesselam constructed such a trajectory rigorously and nonperturbatively for the three-dimensional $\varphi^4$ model with a modified free propagator [@abdesselam-2007], joining the Gaussian fixed point to an analogue of the Wilson-Fisher fixed point of the kind constructed by Brydges, Mitter and Scoppola [@brydges-mitter-scoppola-2003].

    Two features of the construction matter for this wiki. It does not use the hierarchical approximation — the map is the Euclidean one — and yet the unperturbed problem it perturbs is a nonlinear one-dimensional iteration of exactly the kind the hierarchical model provides, with the perturbation supplied by the modified propagator [@abdesselam-2009]. The hierarchical recursion is therefore not an analogy for the Euclidean renormalisation group in this work but its first approximation, and the trajectory is recovered from the one-dimensional flow by a fixed-point argument in a space of sequences of effective potentials.

    The modification of the propagator is what makes the argument close: it suppresses the fluctuations at each scale by a fixed amount, so that the Euclidean step is a bounded perturbation of the hierarchical one. Whether the trajectory can be transported to the unmodified propagator is a separate question, and the construction is a statement about the modified model.

    Definitions and setting

    The renormalisation group is the following operation, in the form used in the renormalisation-group treatment of lattice fields [@bauerschmidt-brydges-slade-2019]. One fixes a fluctuation covariance $C$, a positive definite kernel on the lattice, and a decomposition of it into pieces $C=\sum_jC_j$ indexed by scale. Given an effective potential $V_j$ at scale $j$, one defines

    $$e^{-V_{j+1}(\varphi)}=\int e^{-V_j(\varphi+\zeta)}\,\mu_{C_j}(d\zeta),$$

    the convolution of the Boltzmann weight with the Gaussian measure of covariance $C_j$, followed by the rescalings of field and position that put the result in a fixed normal form. The map $V_j\mapsto V_{j+1}$ is the renormalisation step, and a fixed point of the step is a scale-invariant theory.

    A trajectory between two fixed points is a sequence $(V_j)_{j\in\mathbb Z}$ of effective potentials, each in the domain of the map, such that $V_{j+1}$ is the image of $V_j$ for every $j$, and such that $V_j$ converges to the Gaussian fixed point as $j\to-\infty$ and to the nontrivial fixed point as $j\to+\infty$. The first limit is the ultraviolet, the second the infrared; the intermediate potentials describe the crossover, which is the region in which neither fixed point's linearisation controls the flow.

    For the hierarchical model the corresponding object is a sequence of laws $\nu_j=\mathcal R_{\theta_0,b}^{\,j}\nu_0$ under the single map of the autonomous form, and a trajectory between fixed points is an orbit whose two ends are the Gaussian and nontrivial fixed points of that map. In that case the trajectory is a property of a one-dimensional iteration and can be analysed by the methods of the fixed-point article.

    Statement

    Let the field be three-dimensional with a modified free propagator, and let the interaction be a $\varphi^4$ perturbation with a small coupling. Then there is a sequence of effective potentials $(V_j)_{j\in\mathbb Z}$ satisfying the renormalisation recursion exactly, asymptotic to the ultraviolet Gaussian fixed point as $j\to-\infty$ and to the nontrivial infrared fixed point as $j\to+\infty$. The construction is nonperturbative in the sense that no order-by-order expansion is resummed: it produces the trajectory as the solution of a fixed-point equation in a Banach space of sequences.

    The statement is Theorem 1 of Abdesselam [@abdesselam-2007], with the summary of the method and of the role of the modified propagator in the six-page account of the same construction [@abdesselam-2009]. The infrared endpoint is an analogue of the Wilson-Fisher fixed point: it is the nontrivial fixed point of the model with the modified propagator rather than a statement about the unmodified three-dimensional $\varphi^4$ theory, whose renormalisation-group trajectory is constructed by different means in the work of Brydges, Mitter and Scoppola [@brydges-mitter-scoppola-2003].

    Idea of the proof

    The three steps below are the ones the account of the construction names; the estimates are in the paper.

    The one-dimensional problem. With a propagator that is constant on blocks and a field that is constant on blocks, the renormalisation step becomes an operation on the law of one number, that is, a nonlinear one-dimensional iteration. For such an iteration the two fixed points and the orbit joining them can be constructed explicitly or by the methods of the scalar hierarchical analysis, and the resulting sequence is the unperturbed trajectory.

    The Euclidean problem as a perturbation. For the modified propagator the difference between the Euclidean step and the block step is small, uniformly in the scale, because the modification suppresses the fluctuations that the block approximation discards. The trajectory is then sought as a perturbation of the unperturbed sequence, with the perturbation carried by the couplings of the model.

    The fixed-point argument. The space of sequences is given a norm that measures, at each scale, the size of the deviation of the effective potential from the unperturbed one, with a weight that grows in the direction of the unstable manifold and decays in the stable directions. On that space the renormalisation recursion becomes a fixed-point equation whose linearisation is invertible, the inverse being controlled by the spectral gap of the one-dimensional problem at each of its two ends; the contraction principle then gives the trajectory. The weights are the reason the argument works at a fixed point of the flow and not only near one: the unstable direction at the ultraviolet end is the stable direction at the infrared end, and the weights are chosen to be summable along the whole orbit.

    Consequences and applications

    The crossover region. The trajectory controls the intermediate scales, where the linearisation at either fixed point gives no information. Correlation functions of the model at momenta in the crossover region are governed by the corresponding intermediate effective potential, so a statement about the whole orbit is a statement about the region in which the exponents alone do not determine the behaviour.

    The hierarchical model as the organising centre. The construction makes precise the sense in which the hierarchical recursion is the first approximation to the Euclidean renormalisation group: the Euclidean trajectory is obtained from the one-dimensional one by a perturbation whose size is controlled by the propagator, and the one-dimensional trajectory is the input. This is the quantitative form of the comparison drawn in the article on the two settings.

    A template for other models. The argument uses only that the unperturbed problem is a one-dimensional iteration with two hyperbolic fixed points and that the perturbation is small; the same scheme applies to the vector models and to the $p$-adic and ultrametric settings in which the block structure is exact, which is the content of Abdesselam's programme for a three-dimensional conformal field theory [@abdesselam-2018].

    Examples and non-examples

    The hierarchical model itself. For a prior with a small quartic coupling and a kernel exponent above the marginal one, the orbit of the recursion from the Gaussian neighbourhood towards the nontrivial fixed point is a trajectory between fixed points in the sense above; it is the unperturbed object of the Euclidean construction.

    A critical orbit. The orbit that starts exactly on the stable manifold of the nontrivial fixed point is critical: its correlation length is infinite at every scale, and it is a trajectory between fixed points only in the sense of convergence to one of them. The trajectory of the theorem has both ends and a finite crossover scale; the two objects answer different questions.

    Non-example: a massive orbit. An orbit that leaves the neighbourhood of the Gaussian fixed point along the relevant direction and does not return describes a model with a finite correlation length. It is a perfectly good orbit, but not a trajectory between two fixed points.

    Non-example: without the modified propagator. The construction rests on the smallness of the difference between the block step and the Euclidean step. For the unmodified propagator that difference is not small, and the trajectory of the unmodified theory requires the polymer and cluster expansions of the Brydges-Mitter-Scoppola programme rather than a perturbation of a one-dimensional iteration.

    Variants and generalisations

    Other crossovers. A trajectory can join any two hyperbolic fixed points between which the flow is defined, and the same Banach-space scheme applies when the intermediate region is described by a known one-dimensional model. The crossover from mean field to critical behaviour is the case at hand; crossovers associated with a change of symmetry or with a change of the number of components would require a different unperturbed problem.

    Ultrametric and $p$-adic versions. In settings where the block structure is exact the unperturbed problem is the model itself, and the trajectory is the orbit of the one-dimensional map; the ultrametric version of the construction is part of Abdesselam's programme for conformal probability [@abdesselam-2018].

    Logarithmic corrections. At the marginal exponent the two fixed points merge and the trajectory is no longer hyperbolic at either end; the flow then has a logarithmic scale dependence, and the Banach-space argument has to be replaced by one adapted to the marginal case.

    History and attribution

    The nontrivial infrared fixed point of the modified three-dimensional model is due to Brydges, Mitter and Scoppola [@brydges-mitter-scoppola-2003]. The complete trajectory between the two fixed points, and the observation that the Euclidean problem is a perturbation of a one-dimensional iteration, are Abdesselam's [@abdesselam-2007], with an account of the method in the proceedings of the 2009 congress [@abdesselam-2009]. The renormalisation group in the potential formulation used here is the subject of Bauerschmidt, Brydges and Slade [@bauerschmidt-brydges-slade-2019].

    Renormalisation for hierarchical percolation

    Cluster amplification is the relevant direction, and the triangle threshold is three alpha against d.

    theorem · separate page

    Long-range percolation on the hierarchical lattice has bond intensities $J\lVert x-y\rVert^{-d-\alpha}$, so the intensity of the edge between two vertices depends only on their level, and the model inherits the child decomposition of the lattice. Its renormalisation group does not act on the law of one number: the state of a block is the partition of its vertices into clusters, and the operation of decimation is the passage from the cluster partition of the children to that of the block. What survives from the spin case is the amplification: two large clusters in sibling blocks are joined with probability comparable to $\beta J_n$ times their product of sizes, so a block whose clusters are atypically large produces a parent whose clusters are larger still.

    That mechanism gives the exact exponents. At criticality the expected size of the restricted cluster of the origin in $\Lambda_n$ grows like $L^{\alpha n}$ — the growth exponent $\kappa=\alpha$ of the exponent dictionary — and the critical connection probability between vertices at distance $L^n$ is of order $L^{-(d-\alpha)n}$, so $\eta=2-\alpha$ for every $0<\alpha<d$. This is unambiguous in the mean-field region, where the second moment of the cluster size diverges, and it holds beyond it as well; the theorem is Hutchcroft's, and it is the only case in which the two-point exponent is known across the whole long-range range [@hutchcroft-hierarchical-2021].

    The phase transition itself is classical: $\beta_c$ is finite exactly when $\alpha<d$, and there is no infinite cluster at $\beta_c$ [@koval-meester-trapman-2012]. The renormalisation group enters at the level of exponents, and the question it answers is why the model is mean-field below $\alpha=d/3$ and not above: the triangle diagram is a convergent sum below that value and a divergent one above it, and the diagram is computable here because the lattice is ultrametric.

    Definitions and setting

    Let $0<\alpha<d$, $\beta\geq0$, and for distinct $x,y\in\mathbb H^d_L$ declare the pair an open edge independently with probability $p_\beta(x,y)=1-e^{-\beta J\lVert x-y\rVert^{-d-\alpha}}$. The cluster $K_x$ is the set of vertices joined to $x$ by open paths, $K^{\Lambda_n}_x$ is the same object computed with edges inside $\Lambda_n$, and

    $$\tau_\beta(x,y)=\mathbb P_\beta(x\leftrightarrow y),\qquad \tau^{\Lambda_n}_\beta(x,y)=\mathbb P_\beta\bigl(x\xleftrightarrow{\Lambda_n}y\bigr),\qquad \chi_n(\beta)=\mathbb E_\beta\lvert K^{\Lambda_n}_0\rvert .$$

    The critical intensity is $\beta_c=\inf\{\beta:\mathbb P_\beta(\lvert K_0\rvert=\infty)>0\}$, and $\chi_n$ is the block susceptibility of the exponent article. Two auxiliary quantities are used below: the level-$n$ quantile

    $$R_n(\beta)=\min\bigl\{r\geq1:\mathbb P_\beta\bigl(\max_{x\in\Lambda_n}\lvert K^{\Lambda_n}_x\rvert\geq r\bigr)\leq e^{-1}\bigr\},$$

    the typical size of the largest cluster of a block, and the amplification factor $\beta J_n=\beta JL^{-(d+\alpha)n}$, which is the intensity of every edge joining two distinct children of $\Lambda_n$.

    Two exact recursion inequalities

    The percolation analogue of the decimation identity is a pair of inequalities, both exact consequences of the child decomposition and the independence of the bonds between children.

    Let $n\geq0$, let $B,B'$ be distinct children of $\Lambda_{n+1}$, let $x\in B$ and $y\in B'$, and let $I=\lvert K^{B}_x\rvert$ and $I'=\lvert K^{B'}_y\rvert$ computed with the bonds inside $B$ and $B'$ respectively. Then the pair $(I,I')$ is independent and

    $$\tau^{\Lambda_{n+1}}_\beta(x,y)\ \geq\ \mathbb E_\beta\bigl[1-e^{-\beta J_{n+1}II'}\bigr].$$
    Proof of the one-edge bound

    Children are disjoint and every edge joining them is at level $n+1$, so the bonds of $B$ and of $B'$ are disjoint sets and $(I,I')$ is a pair of independent random variables, each with the law of the restricted cluster size in the model on $\Lambda_n$ with free boundary. Given $I=i$ and $I'=i'$, the $ii'$ pairs $\{u,v\}$ with $u$ in the cluster of $x$ and $v$ in the cluster of $y$ are distinct and their edges are independent of the internal bonds, each open with probability $1-e^{-\beta J_{n+1}}$. Any one open edge among them joins $x$ to $y$, so the conditional probability of connection is $1-(e^{-\beta J_{n+1}})^{ii'}=1-e^{-\beta J_{n+1}ii'}$. Averaging gives the inequality, which is an inequality rather than an identity because a connection may also use a chain of children.

    The inequality is the mechanism of amplification in one line:

    [Interactive figure: Percolation on the hierarchical lattice of level 5. Each bond is drawn at the level of the pair and opened with probability 1 − exp(−β L^{−(d+α)k}); raise β and watch the cluster of the origin take off. — on the web page]

    the probability of joining two clusters grows like $\beta J_{n+1}II'$ when the product is small, so an intensity decaying at rate $L^{-(d+\alpha)}$ is compensated by clusters growing at rate $L^{\alpha n}$, and a cluster size growing faster than $L^{\alpha n}$ produces parents that grow faster still. Used at the level of expectations with a truncation of the product, it gives the iteration

    $$\chi_{n+1}\ \geq\ c\,L^{-(d+\alpha)(n+1)}\chi_n^2,\qquad \chi_n\leq L^{dn},$$

    with $c$ depending on $\beta$ and on the truncation, as long as the cluster sizes are bounded by a constant multiple of $L^{(d+\alpha)(n+1)/2}$ with high probability. The iteration has the fixed point $\chi_n\asymp L^{\alpha n}$: a growth exponent below $\alpha$ is pushed up, and a growth exponent above $\alpha$ is incompatible with the quantile bound, which is why the critical orbit sits at $\alpha$.

    The critical two-point function

    Let $0<\alpha<d$, let $J$ be symmetric, and suppose that there are $c,C>0$ with $c\lVert x\rVert^{-d-\alpha}\leq J(x)\leq C\lVert x\rVert^{-d-\alpha}$ for $x\neq0$. Then $\beta_c\in(0,\infty)$, and there are $a,A>0$, depending only on $d,L,\alpha,c,C$, such that for every $n\geq1$,

    $$a\,L^{-(d-\alpha)n}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{L^{dn}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_n\setminus\Lambda_{n-1}}\tau^{\Lambda_n}_{\beta_c}(0,x)\ \leq\ \frac{1}{L^{dn}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_n}\tau_{\beta_c}(0,x)\ \leq\ A\,L^{-(d-\alpha)n}.$$

    If in addition $J$ is radial, then for every $x,y\in\mathbb H^d_L$,

    $$a\,\langle x-y\rangle^{-(d-\alpha)}\ \leq\ \tau^{\Lambda(x,y)}_{\beta_c}(x,y)\ \leq\ \tau_{\beta_c}(x,y)\ \leq\ A\,\langle x-y\rangle^{-(d-\alpha)},$$

    where $\Lambda(x,y)$ is the smallest block containing both. Both constants are those of the averaged statement; the passage from averages to points uses the level isometries of the lattice.

    The averaged statement is Theorem 1.1 and the pointwise one Corollary 1.2 of Hutchcroft [@hutchcroft-hierarchical-2021], where the lower bound is proved for connections inside the smallest common block and the upper bound for unrestricted connections. The proof runs through block quantiles: the amplification of the previous section shows that $R_n(\beta)^2$ is at most a constant times $L^{(d+\alpha)n}$ at $\beta_c$, sharpness supplies a lower bound on the flux of intensity leaving a block, and the two together pin $\chi_n$ between constants times $L^{\alpha n}$; the conversion to connection probabilities is a BK decomposition according to the smallest block containing the path.

    The triangle threshold

    The triangle diagram is the sum $\sum_{x,y}\tau(0,x)\tau(x,y)\tau(y,0)$, and on an ultrametric space it can be computed scale by scale.

    Let $0<\alpha<d$, let $\tau(0)=1$ and $\tau(z)=L^{-(d-\alpha)\operatorname{lev}(z)}$ for $z\neq0$, and put

    $$T_N=\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_N}\tau(0,x)\,\tau(x-y)\,\tau(y).$$

    Then $T_N\asymp\sum_{n=0}^{N}L^{(3\alpha-d)n}$. In particular the infinite sum converges if $3\alpha<d$, diverges like $N$ if $3\alpha=d$, and grows like $L^{(3\alpha-d)N}$ if $3\alpha>d$. Consequently, under the two-point bounds of the previous section, the triangle diagram at $\beta_c$ is finite exactly when $3\alpha<d$.

    Proof of the triangle threshold

    Write $a=\operatorname{lev}(x)$, $b=\operatorname{lev}(x-y)$ and $c=\operatorname{lev}(y)$. The ultrametric inequality gives $\operatorname{lev}(x)=\operatorname{lev}((x-y)+y)\leq\max\{b,c\}$ and its two analogues, so the largest two of $a,b,c$ are equal. Group the pairs by the common value $m$ of the two largest and by which of the three is the smallest.

    Case $a=c=m$, $b=j\leq m$. Both $x$ and $y$ have level $m$ and their difference has level $j$. The number of such pairs is at most the product of the shell sizes at levels $m$ and $j$, and at least a constant times it: the map $(x,z)\mapsto(x,x+z)$ is injective, $x$ ranges over a shell of size $(1-L^{-d})L^{dm}$, $z$ over a shell of size $(1-L^{-d})L^{dj}$, and $x+z$ has level $m$ whenever $j<m$. Each term is $L^{-(d-\alpha)(2m+j)}$, so the contribution of the level $m$ is $L^{dm+dj-(d-\alpha)(2m+j)}=L^{m(2\alpha-d)+\alpha j}$; summing over $j\leq m$ gives a constant times $L^{m(3\alpha-d)}$, because the geometric sum is dominated by its last term as $\alpha>0$.

    Case $a=b=m>c$, and its mirror image. Here $x$ has level $m$, $y$ has level $c$, and the difference has level $m$. For each $y$ of level $c$ there are at most $(1-L^{-d})L^{dm}$ choices of $x$ of level $m$, and all of them give difference of level $m$ because $y$ vanishes above coordinate $c<m$. The contribution is $L^{dm+dc-(d-\alpha)(2m+c)}=L^{m(2\alpha-d)}L^{\alpha c}$, and summing over $c<m$ gives a constant times $L^{m(2\alpha-d)+\alpha m}=L^{m(3\alpha-d)}$. The mirror case $b=c=m>a$ is the same with the roles of $x$ and $y$ exchanged.

    All three cases contribute the same order at scale $m$, so $T_N\asymp\sum_{m\leq N}L^{(3\alpha-d)m}$, which is bounded, linear, or geometric according to the sign of $3\alpha-d$. The last claim is the comparison of $\tau_{\beta_c}$ with $\tau$ in the two-sided form of the previous section.

    The exponent in the display is the one that decides mean-field behaviour:

    $3\alpha-d>0$ is exactly the regime in which the triangle diagram diverges and the volume exponent, if it exists, exceeds its mean-field value. For $3\alpha<d$ the diagram is finite, the triangle condition holds and the exponents of the dictionary take their mean-field values, $\delta=2$ and $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}=1$; at $3\alpha=d$ the divergence is logarithmic.

    Consequences and applications

    The growth exponent. The theorem gives $\chi_n(\beta_c)\asymp L^{\alpha n}$ directly, which is $\kappa=\alpha$ and $\eta=2-\alpha$ in the notation of the exponent article. This agrees with the general statement that the growth exponent is fixed by the autonomy of the recursion at $\theta_0=\tfrac12(d+\alpha)$: the quantile bound $R_n(\beta_c)^2\lesssim L^{(d+\alpha)n}$ is the upper half of the variance growth, and the boundary flux supplies the lower half.

    The volume exponent. For $3\alpha>d$ the model is not mean-field, and the cluster-volume tail is constrained. If $\mathbb P_{\beta_c}(\lvert K_0\rvert\geq n)=n^{-1/\delta+o(1)}$, then $\delta\geq\frac{d+\alpha}{d-\alpha}$, which is strictly greater than $2$; the value $\delta=\frac{d+\alpha}{d-\alpha}$ is conjectured, and it is the value that the scaling relations give when the two-point exponent is $2-\alpha$. This is Corollary 1.5 of Hutchcroft [@hutchcroft-hierarchical-2021]. The volume of the critical cluster in hierarchical percolation is the subject of a separate analysis [@hutchcroft-2025-volumes], which is where the exponent of the tail is studied directly rather than through the two-point function.

    The figure is the distribution itself rather than a schematic of it. Two things in it are worth reading off. The slope at the middle intensity follows the dashed guide over the first octave and then flattens, which is the block size: 64 sites cannot show a power law much beyond one decade, and the flattening is the finite-block cutoff rather than a physical effect. And the upper intensity has the opposite signature — a depletion at small sizes and a bump at the largest, which is the spanning cluster: the distribution of finite clusters is what carries the power law, and above the transition most vertices belong to a cluster the size of the block.

    The exponent $\nu$. Nothing in the two-point theorem identifies the correlation-length exponent, and it is the one quantity the percolation analysis does not determine in the non-mean-field regime. In the mean-field regime $\nu=1/\alpha$ follows from the classical values and $\gamma=1$; for $\alpha>d/3$ the value of $\nu$ is not known, and it is where the comparison with the spin model's nontrivial fixed point would enter.

    Examples and non-examples

    The power kernel with $\alpha\downarrow0$. Then $d-\alpha\uparrow d$ and the critical two-point exponent tends to $d$: the connection probability decays like the inverse volume of the ball, the marginal case in which the susceptibility diverges logarithmically. At $\alpha=0$ the intensity is not summable, $\beta_c=0$, and the exponent statements above have no content.

    A kernel vanishing on a level. The two-point theorem needs a two-sided comparison with the power kernel on every level. If $J$ vanishes, or is much smaller, on a sparse set of levels, the amplification fails at those scales and the critical behaviour changes: the model behaves as though the exponent were larger on those levels, and the block-quantile argument gives bounds at the worst scale rather than at each scale.

    Non-example: the nearest-neighbour lattice. On $\mathbb Z^d$ the block geometry is not ultrametric, the triangle diagram is not a geometric sum in the scale, and the mean-field threshold is a dimension rather than a relation between $\alpha$ and $d$. The transfer of the computation to that setting is the subject of the comparison article.

    Non-example: percolation with $q$ states. The Fortuin-Kasteleyn random-cluster model on the hierarchical lattice has the same block decomposition but a cluster weight $q^{\#\text{clusters}}$ that is not a product over children, so the decimation state is a partition function with boundary conditions and not a one-variable law. The amplification inequalities survive; the exact two-point exponent is not available by this route.

    Variants and generalisations

    Inhomogeneous intensities. Replacing $J_k$ by a level-dependent sequence $J_k$ with $J_k\asymp L^{-(d+\alpha)k}$ changes no bound in the argument, and the exponents are unchanged; this is the form in which the model appears in comparisons with Euclidean percolation, where the hierarchical kernel is a comparison object rather than a model of interest.

    Full connectivity. The event that a block is fully connected, with no isolated bond structure, has its own recursion and is the percolation analogue of a one-variable law: the probability that $\Lambda_n$ is connected satisfies a recursion driven by the probability that the children and the cross edges form a connected structure. For large $m$ this is a mean-field-looking recursion, and it is not the recursion that governs $\tau_{\beta_c}$.

    Other lattices. The child decomposition is available on any ultrametric group, including the $p$-adic integers and the solenoids; the counting arguments use only that a ball of radius $L^n$ has $L^{dn}$ points and $m$ children, so they apply verbatim wherever that holds.

    History and attribution

    Long-range percolation on the hierarchical lattice was studied by Koval, Meester and Trapman, who proved that $\beta_c<\infty$ exactly when $\alpha<d$ and that there is no infinite cluster at criticality [@koval-meester-trapman-2012]. The two-point theorem, the pointwise form for radial kernels, the triangle threshold and the volume-exponent bound are Hutchcroft's [@hutchcroft-hierarchical-2021]. The block-quantile amplification that drives the argument is the percolation form of the amplification in decimation, and the hierarchical lattice itself goes back to Dyson's approximation [@dyson-1969].

    Hierarchical models with continuous symmetry

    With a vector spin the block direction does not settle at criticality, and the marginal exponent is decided by a slowly varying factor.

    theorem · separate page

    The $r$-component hierarchical model is the model of the decimation article with a vector spin: $\sigma_x\in\mathbb R^r$ and energy $-\sum_{\{x,y\}}J(\lVert x-y\rVert)\langle\sigma_x,\sigma_y\rangle$, with $J(L^n)=l_nL^{-(d+\alpha)n}$. Rotation invariance replaces the spin-flip symmetry, the block spin becomes a vector, and the decimation identity survives with inner products in place of products; what does not survive is the scalar picture of criticality. At the critical temperature the direction of the block spin does not concentrate: Bleher and Major proved that the normalised average spin converges in law to the uniform distribution on the sphere, while the spontaneous magnetisation vanishes [@bleher-major-2021].

    The same work settles Dyson's conjecture for this model at the marginal exponent. When $\alpha=d$ the interaction is summable or not according to the level sequence: $\sum_nJ_nL^{dn}=\sum_nl_n$. Bleher and Major prove that a phase transition exists exactly when $\sum_nl_n=\infty$, so the marginal case is decided by the slowly varying factor and not by any exponent, and they prove that the transition is continuous — there is no Thouless effect, in which the magnetisation would jump. What replaces the jump is the failure of concentration of the direction at criticality: the order parameter is zero, and yet the block spin is not small.

    Two structural facts organise the comparison with the scalar case. The decimation map commutes with rotations, so the state of the recursion is a rotation-covariant law on $\mathbb R^r$; and the Gaussian fixed point is $r$-independent, since the tilt couples the components separately and the variance recursion is the scalar one. The exponents $\kappa=\alpha$ and $\eta=2-\alpha$ are therefore unchanged by $r$, and the $r$-dependence enters through a marginal angular direction, the hierarchical counterpart of a Goldstone mode, which is what the nontrivial fixed point acquires when the symmetry is continuous.

    Definitions and setting

    Fix $r\geq1$, integers $L\geq2$, $d\geq1$, $m=L^d$, a real $\alpha>0$ and a sequence $l_n>0$ with $l_n$ bounded above and below on each level. The coupling at level $n$ is $J_n=l_nL^{-(d+\alpha)n}$, and the Hamiltonian on a finite $S\subset\mathbb H^d_L$ is

    $$H_S(\sigma)=-\sum_{\{x,y\}\subset S}J(\lVert x-y\rVert)\langle\sigma_x,\sigma_y\rangle,\qquad J(L^n)=J_n .$$

    The model is a problem about the limit distribution of a strongly dependent sequence, which is the framing of Bleher and Major's survey [@bleher-major-1987]. The prior is a rotation invariant probability measure $\varrho$ on $\mathbb R^r$ with finite second moment; the two cases below are the classical spin $\varrho$ uniform on $\mathbb S^{r-1}$, and the Gaussian $\varrho=N(0,I_r)$. For $r=1$ this is the scalar model, and the inner product is the product.

    The block spin of $S$ is $S_S=\sum_{x\in S}\sigma_x\in\mathbb R^r$ and $\mu_n$ is its law under the Gibbs measure of $\Lambda_n$ with free boundary. The decimation map at coupling $b$ acts on probability measures on $\mathbb R^r$ by

    $$\mathcal T_b\nu(A')=\frac{1}{Z_b(\nu)}\int_{(\mathbb R^r)^m}\mathbf 1_{A'}\Bigl(\sum_{a}s_a\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(b\sum_{a<a'}\langle s_a,s_{a'}\rangle\Bigr)\prod_a\nu(ds_a),$$

    with $Z_b(\nu)$ the corresponding normaliser, and $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}$ is its composition with $s\mapsto L^{-\theta}s$ as in the map article. The critical exponent of the interaction is the value $\alpha=d$ at which $\sum_nL^{dn}J_n=\sum_nl_n$ may converge or diverge.

    Decimation and rotation covariance

    The two structural statements are proved exactly as in the scalar case, with an inner product in place of a product.

    For $n\geq1$ the children $\Lambda^{(a)}=e_n(a)+\Lambda_{n-1}$ of $\Lambda_n$ satisfy

    $$\exp\bigl(-\beta H_{\Lambda_n}(\sigma)\bigr)=\prod_{a}\exp\bigl(-\beta H_{\Lambda^{(a)}}(\sigma^{(a)})\bigr)\exp\Bigl(\beta l_nL^{-(d+\alpha)n}\sum_{a<a'}\langle S_a,S_{a'}\rangle\Bigr),$$

    so $Z_n=Z_{n-1}^mZ_{\beta J_n}(\mu_{n-1})$ and $\mu_n=\mathcal T_{\beta J_n}\mu_{n-1}$; with $\theta_0=\tfrac12(d+\alpha)$ and $b=\beta J_1$ the rescaled laws satisfy $\nu_n=\mathcal R_{\theta_0,b}^{\,n}\nu_0$.

    Proof of the vector decimation identity

    By the child decomposition every pair of $\Lambda_n$ lies either inside a child, at a level at most $n-1$, or in two distinct children, in which case it is at level exactly $n$. The energy therefore splits as in the scalar case, and the cross term is $\sum_{a<a'}J_n\langle S_a,S_{a'}\rangle$ because the sum of $\langle\sigma_x,\sigma_y\rangle$ over the pairs of two children factors as the inner product of the two child sums. A child is a translate of $\Lambda_{n-1}$ and the Hamiltonian depends on differences, so the integral over a child at fixed $S_a$ is $Z_{n-1}\mu_{n-1}(dS_a)$. Dividing by $Z_n$ gives the recursion, and the scaling computation is the scalar one, the factor $L^{2\theta_0}$ multiplying every inner product uniformly.

    For every orthogonal $Q\in O(r)$, $\mathcal T_b(Q_*\nu)=Q_*\mathcal T_b\nu$. For the Gaussian prior $\varrho=N(0,I_r)$ the law of the block spin is $N(0,v_nI_r)$ with $v_n$ given by the scalar recursion

    $$v_n=\frac{mv_{n-1}}{1-\beta J_nv_{n-1}(m-1)},\qquad v_0=1,$$

    so the critical coupling, the fixed point of the rescaled recursion and its multiplier $L^{\alpha}$ are those of the Gaussian article, independently of $r$.

    Proof

    Rotations preserve the inner product, so the tilted product measure and the sum $\sum_as_a$ are transported by $Q$; this gives the covariance. For the Gaussian prior the computation is componentwise: the exponent is $b\sum_{a<a'}\sum_{j=1}^r s_{a,j}s_{a',j}$, a sum of $r$ copies of the scalar exponent, so the tilted law is a product over $j$ of scalar tilted laws, and the law of the sum is $N(0,v'I_r)$ with $v'$ the scalar value. The scalar recursion and its closed form then apply to each component, and neither the critical coupling $\beta_c=1/\sum_{x\neq0}J(\lVert x\rVert)$ nor the multiplier depends on $r$.

    A second symmetry acts on the lattice rather than on $\mathbb R^r$. The recursion is a map on the law of a single block sum, and that is a consequence of the $m$ children of a block having the same law and of their sums being exchangeable — statement (ii) of the isometry article — which holds with all $r$ components moved together. Rotation covariance constrains the state of the recursion inside $\mathbb R^r$; the lattice symmetry is what makes that state one variable rather than the joint law of the $m$ child sums.

    The proposition is the reason the mean-field exponents of the vector model are the scalar ones. It also isolates what continuous symmetry can change: nothing in the Gaussian orbit, and therefore nothing in the two renormalisation-group numbers $\kappa$ and $\nu$ at the level of the Gaussian fixed point. The change appears at the non-Gaussian fixed point, whose linearisation acquires a marginal direction in the angular variable.

    The direction of a rotation invariant block spin

    The vector case has an observable the scalar case does not: the direction $S/\lvert S\rvert$. For a rotation invariant law it carries no information about the size.

    Let $\nu$ be a rotation invariant probability measure on $\mathbb R^r$ with $\nu(\{0\})=0$, $r\geq2$. Then under $\nu$ the random variable $S/\lvert S\rvert$ is uniform on $\mathbb S^{r-1}$ and independent of $\lvert S\rvert$. In particular the conclusion holds for every law of the Gaussian orbit and for every rotation invariant fixed point of $\mathcal R_{\theta,b}$.

    Proof

    Let $f$ be bounded measurable on $\mathbb S^{r-1}$ and $g$ bounded measurable on $[0,\infty)$. Since $\lvert QS\rvert=\lvert S\rvert$ for orthogonal $Q$ and $\nu$ is invariant,

    $$\int f(S/\lvert S\rvert)g(\lvert S\rvert)\,d\nu(S)=\int f(QS/\lvert QS\rvert)g(\lvert S\rvert)\,d\nu(S)$$

    for every $Q$. Averaging over $Q$ against the Haar measure of $O(r)$ and using Fubini, the right-hand side is $\int \bar f\,g(\lvert S\rvert)d\nu(S)$ with $\bar f$ the average of $f$ over the sphere, which is $\int f\,d\sigma$ for the uniform $\sigma$. Taking $g=\mathbf 1_{B}$ for a Borel $B\subseteq[0,\infty)$ gives $\int f(S/\lvert S\rvert)\mathbf 1_B(\lvert S\rvert)d\nu=\bigl(\int f\,d\sigma\bigr)\nu(\lvert S\rvert\in B)$, which is the product statement; the case $f\equiv1$ gives uniformity.

    The proposition explains why "the direction is uniform" is not by itself a critical phenomenon: it holds at every temperature in the Gaussian model. What Bleher and Major prove is that it holds at the critical temperature of the interacting model as well, where the direction could have concentrated, and that this coexists with a vanishing spontaneous magnetisation.

    Dyson's conjecture and the Thouless-like effect

    At the marginal exponent the interaction is summable exactly when the level couplings are, and the question is whether summability is the same as the absence of a transition. Dyson conjectured that it is, and Bleher and Major proved the conjecture for the vector model.

    Let $r\geq2$, let the interaction be $U(x,y)=-l_nL^{-2n}\langle\sigma_x,\sigma_y\rangle$ at level $n$, and let the prior be uniform on $\mathbb S^{r-1}$. Then a phase transition exists if and only if $\sum_nl_n=\infty$. At the critical temperature the spontaneous magnetisation vanishes, and the law of the normalised average spin of $\Lambda_N$ converges to the uniform distribution on $\mathbb S^{r-1}$ as $N\to\infty$.

    The statement is Theorem 1 of Bleher and Major [@bleher-major-2021], where the model is written with the potential $-l(d)d^{-2}$ in the hierarchical distance $d$; the exponent $-2$ is the marginal one, $\alpha=d$ in the notation of this wiki, and the condition $\sum_nl_n=\infty$ is the failure of summability of the interaction. The same paper gives the corresponding statements for the scalar case as the boundary of its analysis.

    Three consequences are worth separating. First, at the marginal exponent the criterion is a condition on the level sequence and not on a power: replacing $l_n$ by a sequence with the same growth but a convergent sum removes the transition. Second, the transition is continuous: the spontaneous magnetisation tends to zero at $T_c$, so the Thouless effect — a jump of the order parameter at the transition — does not occur. Third, the direction of the block spin is asymptotically uniform at $T_c$, so the order parameter vanishes while the block spin is of the size it has at criticality: the transition is carried by the fluctuations of the size, not by a selected direction.

    Below the critical temperature the limits change character.

    For the vector model the block sums have non-central limit theorems at every temperature below $T_c$, and the non-Gaussian large-scale limit of the ordered phase is described by Bleher and Major in the two-part paper of 1988 [@bleher-major-1988], with the phenomenon recorded in their survey as one of the features that distinguishes the vector model from the scalar one [@bleher-major-1987]. The mechanism is the transverse enhancement of the low-temperature section of the truncations article: the orthogonal fluctuations grow faster than the square root of the volume, and their limit need not be Gaussian.

    The three statements of the theorem together are the vector counterpart of the scalar picture of the exponent article. In the scalar case the block spin at criticality is a number whose law has a nontrivial limit after rescaling; in the vector case the direction is asymptotically free and the limit law of the rescaled block spin is rotation invariant, so the angular variable contributes a marginal direction rather than a third independent exponent.

    Consequences for the exponents

    The growth exponent is fixed by autonomy in the vector case as in the scalar one, since the argument of the growth theorem uses only the law of the block spin and its second moment.

    Consequently $\kappa=\alpha$ and $\eta=2-\alpha$ for the vector model at any rotation invariant fixed point with finite nonzero variance, and the exponents that involve the order parameter are those of the scalar model with the same $\nu$. The $r$-dependence sits in $\nu$, which is read off the linearisation at the non-Gaussian fixed point, and in the critical fluctuations of the direction, which are not captured by any exponent. This is the sense in which the vector model has the same two renormalisation-group numbers as the scalar one: the angular marginality changes the fixed point and the fluctuations, not the count of relevant directions.

    At the marginal exponent $\alpha=d$ the interaction is not summable and the model is at its own boundary; the level sequence $l_n$ then replaces the exponent as the parameter that decides criticality, which is why the exponents of the vector model at $\alpha=d$ are not obtained from the formulas above by substitution.

    Examples and non-examples

    The scalar case $r=1$. The direction is a sign, the uniform law on $\mathbb S^0$ is the fair coin, and the phenomenon of the previous section reduces to the symmetry $\sigma\mapsto-\sigma$: the block spin's sign is asymptotically fair at $T_c$ whenever the law is symmetric. This is why the vector phenomenon has no scalar analogue of interest.

    Dyson's original model. With $r=1$, $\alpha=d=1$ and $l_n\equiv1$ the criterion $\sum_nl_n=\infty$ holds and the transition exists, which is Dyson's theorem [@dyson-1969]; the vector statement extends it to every $r$ and to every level sequence.

    The Gaussian prior. For $\varrho=N(0,I_r)$ the direction is uniform at every temperature by the proposition above, and the critical behaviour is entirely in the size: the model has no phase transition in the sense of symmetry breaking at any $\beta$, and its exponents are the Gaussian ones.

    Non-example: a discrete symmetry. For the $q$-state Potts model, or for $r=1$, there is no continuous group and no marginal angular direction. The critical behaviour is then of the scalar type, and the marginal-exponent criterion of the theorem is replaced by the corresponding statement for percolation, where $\beta_c<\infty$ exactly when $\alpha<d$ [@koval-meester-trapman-2012].

    Non-example: the ordered phase. Below $T_c$ the direction concentrates: the block spin is asymptotically parallel to a fixed vector, and the transverse fluctuations are massless. The renormalisation of that phase is a different problem, treated for the vector $\varphi^4$ model by Bleher and Major [@bleher-major-1984] and described in the article on truncations.

    Variants and generalisations

    Other marginal structures. The same question — whether summability decides criticality — can be asked at the marginal exponent of any model in the class, and the answer depends on the symmetry: for the percolation class the marginal exponent is $\alpha=d$ as well, and the criterion at $\alpha=d$ involves the level sequence in the same way.

    Ultrametric and $p$-adic models. The construction uses only the ultrametric block structure, so it applies to the $p$-adic integers and to other ultrametric groups; the renormalisation group on those spaces, and its use as a model for a three-dimensional conformal field theory, is the subject of Abdesselam's programme [@abdesselam-2018].

    The number of components as a parameter. The linearisation at the non-Gaussian fixed point depends on $r$ through the dimension of the space of angular perturbations. The scalar case has none, the $O(r)$ case has $r-1$ marginal directions at leading order, and the exponents are expected to depend on $r$ only through the eigenvalue associated with the radial direction.

    History and attribution

    Dyson introduced the hierarchical model to prove a transition for power-law couplings [@dyson-1969], and conjectured that for continuous symmetry the criterion for a transition is the failure of summability of the interaction; the conjecture and its proof are the subject of Bleher and Major's paper, which circulated from 2002 and appeared in 2021 [@bleher-major-2021]. The low-temperature phase of the vector $\varphi^4$ model was treated by the same authors in 1984 [@bleher-major-1984], and the survey of 1987 presents the hierarchical model as a problem about limit distributions of strongly dependent random variables, with the vector phenomena and a list of conjectures [@bleher-major-1987], and the limit theorems for multicomponent models were announced in the Vilnius proceedings of the same period [@bleher-1987-vilnius]. The transformation itself, in the scalar case, is that of Bleher and Sinai [@bleher-sinai-1973]; the historical account of the Moscow seminar in which this circle of ideas developed is Bleher's [@bleher-2012].

    The phi-fourth hierarchical model and its truncations

    The recursion is an integral operator on potentials; the Gaussian truncation is exact and every other truncation leaks.

    model · separate page

    In the hierarchical model with a spin prior of the form $e^{-V(s)}ds$ the state of the recursion is a potential, not a number: after one decimation step the block spin has a density whose negative logarithm is the effective potential, computed from the child potential by a constrained convolution. The recursion is exact and it is an operation on an infinite-dimensional space; the question is which finite-dimensional families it leaves invariant, and the answer is that only the quadratic one does. Write $V(s)=\tfrac{\mu}{2}s^2+\lambda s^4$; the Gaussian part flows by a fractional linear recursion solved in the Gaussian article, and the quartic part generates powers of every even order, so a two-coupling truncation is an approximation whose error is not controlled by the recursion itself [@bleher-sinai-1973].

    The two rigorous treatments of the full flow are Koch and Wittwer's proof of a nontrivial fixed point for scalar hierarchical models [@koch-wittwer-1986], described in this wiki's article on it, and, in the Euclidean setting, Abdesselam's construction of a complete trajectory between the Gaussian and nontrivial fixed points [@abdesselam-2007], described in the article on that. The hierarchical recursion is the one-dimensional iteration which Abdesselam's construction perturbs, so the potential formulation of this article is where the two meet.

    The quartic coupling is relevant exactly when the kernel exponent is above half the dimension, which is the hierarchical form of the upper critical dimension: for $\alpha<d/2$ the interaction is long enough that the Gaussian fixed point governs, and for $\alpha>d/2$ the nontrivial fixed point does. The values $\alpha=d/2$ and, for short-range models, $d=4$, are the same statement in the parametrisation of Fisher, Ma and Nickel [@fisher-ma-nickel-1972], and in this model the relevance of the quartic coupling is the second diagonal entry of the linearisation at the Gaussian fixed point, computed in the spectrum article.

    Definitions and setting

    Let $V$ be even, bounded below, with $\int e^{-V(s)}ds<\infty$ and $\int e^{cs^2-V(s)}ds<\infty$ for some $c>0$. The hierarchical model with prior $e^{-V(s)}ds$ has the decimation recursion of the decimation article, and its block-spin law has a density because the tilt and the convolution of a density are densities.

    For $b\geq0$ the effective potential $\mathcal V_bV$ is defined by

    $$e^{-(\mathcal V_bV)(s)}=\frac{1}{Z_b(V)}\int_{\mathbb R^{m-1}}\exp\Bigl(b\sum_{a<a'}s_as_{a'}\Bigr)\prod_{a=1}^{m-1}e^{-V(s_a)}\,ds_1\cdots ds_{m-1},$$

    with $s_m=s-\sum_{a<m}s_a$, and the scaled potential at exponent $\theta$ is $(\mathcal R_\theta V)(s)=(\mathcal V_bV)(L^{\theta}s)$ up to an additive constant. The potential formulation and the law formulation of the map article carry the same information: $e^{-(\mathcal V_bV)}$ is the density of $\mathcal T_b(e^{-V}ds)$.

    The constant in front is fixed by normalisation and plays no role in the flow of the shape of the potential; additive constants are the free energy increments of the free energy recursion and are dropped from the potential from here on.

    The Gaussian truncation

    The one family the recursion preserves is the quadratic one, and on it the recursion is explicit.

    Let $V(s)=\tfrac{\mu}{2}s^2$ with $\mu>0$ and $b<1/(v(m-1))$, where $v=1/\mu$. Then $\mathcal V_bV$ is quadratic, with curvature $\mu'$ given by

    $$\frac{1}{\mu'}=\frac{m}{\mu-b(m-1)},$$

    that is $\mu'=(\mu-b(m-1))/m$: the curvature flows affinely, and the variance recursion $v'=mv/(1-bv(m-1))$ is the same statement for $v=1/\mu$. A quartic potential is not mapped to a quartic potential: at $\tfrac{\mu}{2}s^2+\lambda s^4$ with $\lambda\neq0$ the first and second orders in $\lambda$ are again quartics in $s$, but the third order is not, and a sixth power appears with a coefficient proportional to $\lambda^3$.

    Proof

    For the first part, the tilted product of Gaussians is Gaussian and the law of the sum is Gaussian, as in the closure theorem; the displayed relation is that theorem with $\mu=1/v$ and $\mu'=1/v'$.

    To locate the sixth power, condition on $S=s$ and write $s_a=s/m+\delta_a$. The constraint gives $\sum_a\delta_a=0$ identically, and the conditional law of $\delta$ is centred Gaussian on the hyperplane $\sum_a\delta_a=0$, with a covariance $\Sigma$ that does not depend on $s$. Expanding, and dropping the linear term because $\sum_a\delta_a=0$,

    $$\sum_a s_a^4=\frac{s^4}{m^3}+\frac{6s^2}{m^2}\sum_a\delta_a^2+\frac{4s}{m}\sum_a\delta_a^3+\sum_a\delta_a^4 .$$

    The cumulant expansion of $-\log\mathbb E[e^{-\lambda X}\mid S=s]$ in powers of $\lambda$ has $k$-th coefficient $\pm\frac{1}{k!}$ times the $k$-th conditional cumulant of $X=\sum_as_a^4$. The first cumulant is the conditional mean, a quartic in $s$, which is the first-order statement above. The second cumulant is the conditional variance, and since the only random coefficient of a power of $s$ in $X$ beyond the constant is $s^2$ times $\sum_a\delta_a^2$ and $s$ times $\sum_a\delta_a^3$, the variance is a quadratic form in $s^2$, $s$ and $1$: it is of degree at most four, so the second order produces no sixth power either.

    The third cumulant is a cubic form in $s^2$, $s$ and $1$, so it has a term in $s^6$: its coefficient is the third cumulant of $\frac{6}{m^2}\sum_a\delta_a^2$ under the conditional law. For a centred Gaussian vector $Z$ and a symmetric matrix $A$ one has $\kappa_3(Z^{\mathsf T}AZ)=8\operatorname{tr}\bigl((A\Sigma)^3\bigr)$, which is nonnegative, and positive here because $\Sigma$ has positive trace of its cube on the hyperplane. Hence

    $$\text{coefficient of }s^6\text{ in }-\log f_\lambda =\frac{\lambda^3}{6}\cdot\frac{6^3}{m^6}\cdot 8\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\Sigma^3\bigr)\,(1+O(\lambda)),$$

    a nonzero multiple of $\lambda^3$. The quartic family therefore fails to be preserved at third order in the quartic coupling, and the first power beyond the fourth that appears is the sixth, with a positive coefficient.

    The proposition is the precise form of two statements that are often made informally: the Gaussian truncation is exact, and every other truncation leaks. The leak is third order in the quartic coupling, so a two-coupling truncation is a controlled approximation only in a regime where the coupling is small, and the renormalisation-group flow of the hierarchical model has no such regime at the nontrivial fixed point.

    Truncations and what replaces them

    A truncation is a projection of the flow onto finitely many couplings, and the proposition above shows that the projection does not commute with the flow beyond the quadratic term. Three routes replace it.

    Infinite-dimensional analysis. The flow is studied on a Banach space of potentials, with the fixed point found by an existence argument rather than by truncation. This is Koch and Wittwer's route for the scalar hierarchical model [@koch-wittwer-1986], and the setting is the one described in the article on it.

    Expansion in a parameter. Near $\alpha=d/2$ the nontrivial fixed point is close to the Gaussian one and the flow can be expanded in $\alpha-d/2$, which gives the $\varepsilon$-expansion of Gawedzki and Kupiainen [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983] and, in the hierarchical model, of Bleher and Sinai. The expansion is a statement about a neighbourhood of the marginal exponent, not about a truncation of the potential.

    Exact solutions of special models. For a quadratic prior the flow is explicit, and for the vector model at low temperature it is explicit around a nonzero magnetisation; those are the two cases in which the recursion is solved rather than analysed.

    The $\varphi^4$ flow in two couplings nevertheless predicts the qualitative picture correctly: a Gaussian fixed point at the origin of the quartic coupling, a nontrivial fixed point at a positive quartic coupling for $\alpha>d/2$, and a critical surface separating the two phases. What it cannot do is give the position of the nontrivial fixed point or the value of $\nu$ there.

    The low-temperature vector model

    For the $r$-component model with $r\geq2$ the ordered phase has a direction, and the question a renormalisation-group analysis asks there is not about a fixed point but about the limit of the block sums around a nonzero magnetisation. Bleher and Major settle it for the vector-valued phi-fourth hierarchical model: at a supercritical intensity the centred block sums, normalised by the volume in the direction of the magnetisation and by a different power in the orthogonal directions, converge to a Gaussian field whose coordinates are independent [@bleher-major-1984].

    The two normalisations are the content. With the pair coupling at level $n$ equal to $2^{-an}$, so that $a=1+\alpha$ in the notation of this wiki, the direction of the spontaneous magnetisation scales as the square root of the volume, and each of the $r-1$ orthogonal directions scales as $\alpha_i^{n/2}$ with $\alpha_i=2^{a}=L^{1+\alpha}$. Since $\alpha_i>L$ in the long-range range, the orthogonal block sums grow faster than the square root of the volume: the transverse fluctuations are enhanced, and after that normalisation their limit is Gaussian with independent coordinates. The proof rests on the large-deviation theory of the same model [@bleher-1985].

    In the non-Gaussian case the limit is not Gaussian, and its description is the subject of the two-part paper of 1988: a limit theorem for the average spin, and a description of the large-scale limit field [@bleher-major-1988], with a conference account of the renormalisation of the low-temperature model in [@bleher-major-1990]. Read with the survey, the picture is that below the critical temperature the vector model has non-central limit theorems at every temperature, in contrast with the scalar model, where away from criticality the block sums have Gaussian limits [@bleher-major-1987].

    These are the fluctuations whose critical form is the marginal angular direction of the vector article: at $T_c$ the transverse enhancement is what makes the direction of the block spin asymptotically uniform.

    Examples and non-examples

    The scalar case at $\alpha>d/2$. Here the nontrivial fixed point governs, the quartic coupling is relevant, and the two-coupling truncation places the fixed point at a value of the coupling that the exact analysis corrects; the exponent $\nu$ differs from $1/\alpha$, as the fixed-point article records.

    Short-range $\varphi^4$ in three dimensions. The Euclidean model with a modified propagator, for which the renormalisation-group trajectory is constructed by Brydges, Mitter and Scoppola [@brydges-mitter-scoppola-2003], has the same structure of a Gaussian ultraviolet fixed point and a nontrivial infrared one; the hierarchical counterpart of the modified propagator is the geometric kernel of the lattice article, and the trajectory joining the two is Abdesselam's.

    Non-example: a quartic truncation at the fixed point. Setting the sixth and higher couplings to zero is not a consistent approximation at a fixed point with a nonzero quartic coupling, by the second part of the proposition; the truncated flow has a fixed point, but the potential there is not the effective potential of any model.

    Non-example: the Gaussian model with a quartic term added by hand. Adding $\lambda s^4$ to the prior without following its flow gives a model whose critical behaviour is Gaussian, because the added term is a bounded perturbation of a quadratic potential for the purposes of the recursion at small $\lambda$; it is not an approximation to the $\varphi^4$ fixed point.

    Variants and generalisations

    Finite-component vectors. For $r$ components the potential is a function of $\lvert s\rvert$, and the flow preserves rotation invariance; the space of radial potentials is infinite-dimensional for every $r$, and the marginal angular directions are absent from it, which is why the radial truncation is more nearly consistent than a truncation in powers of $s$.

    Hierarchical models on other geometries. The Baker-based block structure gives the same kind of potential flow with different constants, and it is the model for which Koch and Wittwer prove a nontrivial fixed point in their third paper [@koch-wittwer-1994]; the $p$-adic and ultrametric groups give further variants with the same structure [@abdesselam-2018].

    The free energy along the flow. The additive constants dropped in the definition are the per-scale increments of the free energy, so the potential flow carries the free energy as a cocycle: the increment at scale $k$ is the logarithm of the normaliser of the step. This is how the singular part of the free energy is computed from the flow in the exponent dictionary.

    History and attribution

    The potential formulation is that of Bleher and Sinai, who wrote the recursion as a nonlinear transformation of measures and, equivalently, of the negative logarithm of their densities [@bleher-sinai-1973]. The low-temperature analysis of the vector model is Bleher and Major's [@bleher-major-1984]; the existence of the nontrivial fixed point without truncation and without a small parameter is Koch and Wittwer's [@koch-wittwer-1986]; the $\varepsilon$-expansion around the marginal exponent is Gawedzki and Kupiainen's [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983]; and the classical long-range exponent values, with the upper critical exponent $\alpha=d/2$, are those of Fisher, Ma and Nickel [@fisher-ma-nickel-1972]. The systematic treatment of the renormalisation group as an operation on potentials is the one developed by Bauerschmidt, Brydges and Slade [@bauerschmidt-brydges-slade-2019].

    The two marginal exponents

    Alpha equal to d decides summability, alpha equal to d/2 decides relevance, and they are not the same threshold.

    theorem · separate page

    Two numbers are singled out in everything written above, and the notation makes them look more alike than they are. The kernel exponent $\alpha$ enters the coupling as $J_k=l_kL^{-(d+\alpha)k}$, and two special values recur: $\alpha=d$, where the interaction stops being summable, and $\alpha=d/2$, where the quartic coupling stops being irrelevant at the Gaussian fixed point. One is a threshold of the measure, the other of the map, and the regimes they cut out are different: in three dimensions, $\alpha=3$ and $\alpha=3/2$ are both called marginal in the literature and they have nothing to do with each other.

    This article separates them. It is a synthesis rather than a new argument: every ingredient is proved or attributed elsewhere in the wiki, and what is added is the statement of which criterion applies where, because the two have been used in different articles and never side by side.

    The level sequence

    Write the coupling at level $k$ as $J_k=l_kL^{-(d+\alpha)k}$ with $l_k\geq0$, so that $\alpha$ is the exponent and $l$ carries whatever slow variation the kernel has. The total intensity at a vertex is then a sum over shells, and the shells grow at rate $m^k$.

    The level sequence is $l=(l_k)_{k\geq1}$, and the model at exponent $\alpha$ has couplings $J_k=l_kL^{-(d+\alpha)k}$. The two marginal values are

    $$\alpha=d\ \text{(summability)},\qquad \alpha=d/2\ \text{(relevance of the quartic)}.$$

    A kernel with $\alpha=d$ and $l_k\equiv1$ is just not summable: its shells contribute a constant, so the total intensity diverges logarithmically in the level. A kernel with $\alpha=d/2$ is summable for every $d>0$, and the marginality there is a statement about the linearisation of the recursion, not about the measure.

    The threshold of the measure

    $$\sum_{x\neq0}J(\lVert x\rVert)=(1-L^{-d})\sum_{k\geq1}l_k,$$

    The threshold of the map

    At the Gaussian fixed point the linearised potential map is triangular in the even monomials with eigenvalues $\Lambda_{kk}=L^{d(1-k)+k\alpha}$. The quadratic direction has eigenvalue $L^{\alpha}>1$ for every $\alpha>0$, and the quartic direction has eigenvalue

    $$\Lambda_{22}=L^{2\alpha-d},$$

    which is one exactly at $\alpha=d/2$, larger above it and smaller below. Hence the quartic coupling is relevant for $\alpha>d/2$, marginal at it, and irrelevant below.

    Proof

    The eigenvalue formula is the spectrum theorem; setting $k=2$ gives $\Lambda_{22}=L^{d(1-2)+2\alpha}=L^{2\alpha-d}$, and $k=1$ gives $L^{\alpha}$. The sign of $2\alpha-d$ decides the comparison with one, and $L>1$ is increasing.

    This threshold is of a different kind. It is not a statement about whether the model is defined — the model is a Gibbs system for every $\alpha>0$ — but about which fixed point governs the critical behaviour: below $d/2$ the quartic is irrelevant, the critical orbit converges to the Gaussian fixed point after rescaling, and the exponents are the classical ones; above it the quartic is relevant, a nontrivial fixed point separates from the Gaussian one, and the correlation-length exponent is no longer $1/\alpha$ [@bleher-sinai-1975] [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983]. At the threshold itself the two fixed points merge, the second eigenvalue tends to one, the leading correction becomes logarithmic, and the analysis needs its own treatment.

    The dependence on the dimension is worth stating separately, because it is the reason the phrase "upper critical dimension" is used for two different things. Setting $\alpha=2$, which is the power law of a short-range interaction in the sense that $\lVert x\rVert^{-d-2}$ is the borderline of summability of the second moment, gives $\alpha=d/2$ exactly when $d=4$. So the four-dimensional theory is the marginal one for the quartic, which is why the four-dimensional $\lvert\varphi\rvert^4$ model is the case the potential formulation and the method of Bauerschmidt, Brydges and Slade are built for. The two thresholds coincide there, and nowhere else.

    Examples and non-examples

    One dimension. Here the thresholds are $\alpha=1$ and $\alpha=1/2$, and Dyson's model lives in between: a transition with $\beta_c<\infty$ for every $\alpha<1$, no transition at $\alpha\geq1$ for the percolation model, and the classical exponents for $\alpha<1/2$. So in one dimension the interval $(1/2,1)$ is the one in which the exponents are not classical but the transition exists — which is why Dyson's paper and the whole subsequent literature are about that interval.

    Four dimensions. The thresholds are $\alpha=4$ and $\alpha=2$, and the second of them is the upper critical dimension of the phi-fourth theory. A reader who takes "α = d is the upper critical dimension" from the spin-glass or percolation literature and applies it to the phi-fourth model will get the wrong threshold by a factor of two, which is the confusion the table is meant to prevent.

    A level sequence that decays at $\alpha=d$. Take $l_k=L^{-\beta k}$ with $\beta>0$ and $\alpha=d$. The interaction is then summable, with the effective exponent $d+\beta$, and Bleher and Major's criterion puts the vector model below its transition: the sequence, not the exponent, decides. For the scalar model at $\alpha=d$ this wiki records no criterion, and that asymmetry is part of the point of the section — the marginal case is a question about a model and a sequence, not a formula.

    Non-example: applying the percolation criterion to the spin model. At $\alpha=d$ the percolation model has $\beta_c=\infty$ for every nondecaying $l$, and the spin model can have a transition. A criterion is a statement about a model, not about an exponent, and this pair is the counterexample to treating them as interchangeable.

    Variants and generalisations

    The vector case. Nothing in either threshold involves the number of components: the summability computation is about the kernel, and the quartic eigenvalue is computed in the spectrum article within the scalar sector, which the vector article shows is enough because the Gaussian fixed point is $r$-independent. The marginal criteria at $\alpha=d$ do differ with $r$: for $r=1$ the direction is a sign and the phenomenon of non-concentrating direction has no scalar analogue, which is what that article is about.

    The percolation triangle threshold. A third special value, $\alpha=d/3$, appears in percolation and is of neither kind: it is where the triangle diagram at criticality changes from convergent to divergent, which is a statement about a diagram rather than about the measure or the linearisation. The percolation article derives it, and it is the threshold that decides whether the model is mean-field, not whether it is defined or which fixed point governs the scalar spin model.

    Slowly varying factors elsewhere. The same device — an exponent fixed and a sequence left free — is used for the correlation-length exponent at $\alpha=d/2$, where the corrections become logarithmic because the second eigenvalue tends to one. That is the third place in the wiki where a threshold is resolved by a sequence rather than by a number, and the corrections article treats it.

    History and attribution

    The summability criterion is the elementary computation of the lattice article, and the marginal case $\alpha=d$ is where Bleher and Major's analysis of Dyson's conjecture lives: the criterion in terms of the level sequence, the continuity of the transition and the non-concentration of the direction are theirs [@bleher-major-2021]. The percolation counterpart at $\alpha=d$ is part of the classical theory of long-range percolation on the hierarchical lattice [@koval-meester-trapman-2012], and the two-point bounds at criticality beyond the marginal exponent are Hutchcroft's [@hutchcroft-hierarchical-2021].

    The quartic threshold $\alpha=d/2$ is the value at which the quartic eigenvalue of the spectrum article crosses one; that it is where the exponents change from classical to non-classical is the content of the fixed-point analysis of Bleher and Sinai [@bleher-sinai-1975], and the local description near the threshold, with the expansion in $\alpha-d/2$, is that of Gawedzki and Kupiainen [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983]. That the two thresholds coincide at $\alpha=2$, $d=4$ is arithmetic, and it is the reason the four-dimensional theory is the marginal one for the quartic.

    Hierarchical and Euclidean renormalisation

    Why the recursion closes here, what fails on the lattice, and how the model is used anyway.

    proposition · separate page

    The decimation identity is an identity, and not an approximation, because of one geometric fact: any two vertices in different children of a hierarchical block are at the same distance, so every pair joining two children carries the same coupling and the interaction between them is a function of the two block sums. A block has no boundary in the sense that no pair of its vertices is closer to the outside than another, and its children are congruent copies of itself. The recursion therefore closes on the law of one number.

    On $\mathbb Z^d$ none of this holds. A cube of side $L^n$ has a boundary of codimension one: the fraction of pairs at distance of order $L^n$ that join two neighbouring blocks is small, the number of nearest-neighbour edges crossing the boundary is of order $L^{(d-1)n}$ rather than $L^{dn}$, and the interaction between two blocks depends on whether they share a face, an edge or a corner. After one decimation step the effective interaction between two block magnetisations is a sum over a boundary whose size depends on the pair, and after two steps the interaction acquires a dependence on the shape of the interface. There is no finite-dimensional family of interactions closed under the operation, and there is a stronger obstruction: the decimated measure of a Gibbs measure need not be Gibbsian at all [@van-enter-fernandez-sokal-1993].

    What the hierarchical model supplies in spite of this is the local structure of the renormalisation map itself: its fixed points, the dimension of the unstable manifold, the eigenvalue problem, the $\varepsilon$-expansion about the upper critical exponent, and the mechanism by which the nonlinearity is irrelevant [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983]. Those are the inputs of the Euclidean renormalisation group, and the hierarchical model is the setting in which they can be established; the transfer is the programme of Gawedzki and Kupiainen for lattice field theory [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1980], and of the renormalisation-group analysis of critical $(\Phi^4)_3$ and related models [@brydges-mitter-scoppola-2003] [@bauerschmidt-brydges-slade-2019].

    Definitions and setting

    Let $L\geq2$. On the hierarchical lattice the level-$n$ block is the subgroup $\Lambda_n$ of the lattice article, its children are the cosets of $\Lambda_{n-1}$, and the decimation state at level $n$ is the law of the block spin $S_{\Lambda_n}$. On $\mathbb Z^d$ the level-$n$ block at $x$ is the cube $x+[0,L^n)^d\cap\mathbb Z^d$, its children are the $L^d$ subcubes of side $L^{n-1}$, and the decimation state at level $n$ is the joint law of the $L^d$ block magnetisations of the children together with the fine configuration inside them.

    An interaction is a function $\Phi$ on configurations; a decimation step maps the Gibbs measure with interaction $\Phi$ on the fine lattice to the law of the coarse variables. A family of interactions is closed if the decimated measure is again a Gibbs measure with an interaction in the family, for every member of the family and every choice of the coarse-graining scale.

    What closes the recursion

    Suppose that a block $B$ is the disjoint union of $m$ children $B_1,\ldots,B_m$ and that the interaction is a sum over unordered pairs of a coupling that is constant on the set of pairs joining two given children. Then the energy of a configuration on $B$ is a function of the child configurations through the $m$ block sums alone, and the law of the $m$-tuple of block sums under the Gibbs measure of $B$ is determined by the interaction inside a child and by the $m(m-1)/2$ couplings between children. If in addition every child is isomorphic to $B$ with the same internal interaction, one decimation step is the map $\mathcal T_b$ of the map article, and the family of interactions on the scale of a block is closed.

    Proof of the exactness criterion

    By hypothesis every pair $\{x,y\}$ with $x\in B_i$, $y\in B_j$, $i\neq j$, carries the same coupling $b_{ij}$, so its contribution to the energy is $-b_{ij}\sigma_x\sigma_y$ and the sum over such pairs is $-b_{ij}S_iS_j$ with $S_i=\sum_{x\in B_i}\sigma_x$. The pairs inside a child contribute the internal energy of that child, and no other pairs exist. Hence the total energy is $\sum_iH_{B_i}(\sigma^{(i)})-\sum_{i<j}b_{ij}S_iS_j$, a function of the child configurations through the sums and of the internal energies. Dividing by the partition function and integrating over the internal configurations at fixed sums gives the stated dependence; if all children are isomorphic and $b_{ij}=b$ for all $i\neq j$, this is the tilt-plus-summation of $\mathcal T_b$.

    The hypothesis of the proposition is what the child decomposition provides. Every pair of distinct children of $\Lambda_n$ is joined by pairs of the same level $n$, so $b_{ij}=J_n$ for all $i\neq j$; every child is a translate of $\Lambda_{n-1}$, so the internal interaction is the same function; and the block at level $n$ is the block at level $1$ with the scale changed, which is the self-similarity that lets one map serve at every scale.

    Let $B=[0,L^n)^d\cap\mathbb Z^d$ with $n\geq1$ and $L\geq2$, and let $B_1,B_2$ be two subcubes of side $L^{n-1}$. The number of unordered pairs $\{x,y\}$ with $x\in B_1$, $y\in B_2$ is $L^{2d(n-1)}$, and the number of such pairs that are nearest neighbours is at most $dL^{d(n-1)}$. Moreover the fraction of nearest-neighbour edges of $\mathbb Z^d$ that join two distinct $L$-blocks equals $1/L$, independently of the scale.

    Proof of the boundary counts

    The two cubes each have $L^{d(n-1)}$ vertices, so there are $L^{2d(n-1)}$ unordered pairs. Each vertex has $2d$ neighbours, so the number of neighbouring pairs between the two cubes is at most $2dL^{d(n-1)}/2=dL^{d(n-1)}$: this is the bound for the count of pairs, and the exponent $d(n-1)$ against $2d(n-1)$ is the statement that only a fraction $L^{-d(n-1)}$ of the pairs are nearest neighbours.

    For the fraction of edges, count in the infinite lattice. Each block of the $L$-partition contains $L^d$ vertices; the nearest-neighbour edges with both endpoints in it are $dL^{d-1}(L-1)$, since there are $d$ directions and $L^{d-1}(L-1)$ edges in each. The number of edges per vertex of $\mathbb Z^d$ is $d$, so the number of edges per block is $dL^d$, and the fraction with both endpoints in the same block is $\frac{dL^{d-1}(L-1)}{dL^d}=\frac{L-1}{L}$. The crossing fraction is the complement, $1/L$.

    Two things follow. A decimation step on $\mathbb Z^d$ cannot be described by a handful of couplings between neighbouring block magnetisations, because the interaction between two blocks is carried by a boundary of size $L^{(d-1)n}$ whose shape depends on the relative position of the blocks; and the interaction between two blocks at a given displacement is not a function of the distance alone unless the lattice and the blocks are chosen with exceptional care. The hierarchical lattice avoids both by making the boundary a point: the children of a block are in bijection with a finite set, and the interaction between any two of them has the same strength.

    What fails beyond the geometry

    The geometric obstruction is the visible one; there is a second, stronger, one. Decimation of a Gibbs measure need not produce a Gibbs measure.

    There are translation-invariant Gibbs measures for finite-range lattice interactions whose image under a block-spin transformation is not a Gibbs measure for any interaction, and there are models for which the image is Gibbsian but with an interaction that is not absolutely summable. Consequently position-space decimation does not define a map on a space of interactions in general [@van-enter-fernandez-sokal-1993].

    The reason is a long-range dependence in the coarse variables that is invisible in the fine ones: conditioning on the outside of a region in the coarse lattice constrains the fine configuration in a way that is not mediated by finitely many coarse variables, so the conditional probabilities of the coarse measure fail the Dobrushin-Lanford-Ruelle form. The hierarchical model is immune because its coarse variables are the block sums and a block interacts with the rest of the lattice through its sum alone, so conditioning on the outside of a block constrains the block only through one number.

    Consequences and applications

    The reference model for the local analysis. The renormalisation map of a hierarchical model is a nonlinear map on a space of measures with exponential moments, and the analysis of its fixed points, its unstable manifold and the $\varepsilon$-expansion can be carried out completely, as in the Bleher–Sinai article. This is the source of the structural facts that the Euclidean theory reproduces: two relevant directions, one of which is the thermal one; the irrelevance of the quartic coupling below the upper critical exponent; the analytic form of the corrections to scaling; and complex eigenvalues producing oscillatory corrections [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983].

    The approximation scheme. Gawedzki and Kupiainen organise the rigorous renormalisation group for a lattice field theory as a convergent expansion whose terms are indexed by the hierarchical approximation of the fluctuation covariance and by the corrections to it [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1980]. The hierarchical model is then not an analogy but the leading term of an expansion, and the control of the remainder is the technical content of the method.

    A whole orbit, not only its ends. The renormalisation-group control of a Euclidean model is a statement about its trajectory between fixed points, and such a trajectory has been constructed for a three-dimensional model with modified propagator by perturbing the one-dimensional iteration that the hierarchical model supplies [@abdesselam-2007]. That construction is described in Abdesselam's trajectory, and it is the precise sense in which the hierarchical recursion is the leading term of the Euclidean analysis rather than an analogy for it.

    Power counting and the upper critical exponent. The exponent at which the two-point exponent stops being $2-\alpha$, and the corresponding statements for $\varphi^4$, are read off from the hierarchical map and transferred. For $\varphi^4_3$ the trajectory of the renormalisation group is constructed in the same way, with the hierarchical analysis of the quartic coupling as the model computation [@brydges-mitter-scoppola-2003] [@bauerschmidt-brydges-slade-2019].

    Bounds transported the other way. The comparison also runs from the hierarchy to the lattice: sharp upper bounds on the critical two-point function for long-range percolation on $\mathbb Z^d$ are obtained by a hierarchical argument, with the hierarchy serving as the comparison object rather than as a model of interest [@hutchcroft-2022-bounds]. This is the percolation counterpart of the perturbation scheme above, and it is the clearest case in which a statement about the hierarchical lattice yields one about $\mathbb Z^d$ without an expansion.

    What does not transfer. The exact exponent values of the hierarchical model are not the Euclidean ones, except in the classical region where both are given by the Gaussian fixed point. For a short-range model in $d$ dimensions with $2<d<4$ the exponents are not the classical ones and are not the hierarchical ones either: the hierarchical model's $\nu$ depends on $\alpha$, while the Euclidean model's depends on $d$. The two agree in the mean-field region and in the structure of the corrections, not in the numbers.

    Examples and non-examples

    Dyson's chain. Dyson's model is a chain $\{0,\ldots,L^n-1\}$ with ferromagnetic couplings $J_{ij}$ comparable to $\lvert i-j\rvert^{-\gamma}$; its hierarchical approximation replaces them by couplings that depend only on the smallest interval of the dyadic hierarchy containing both sites, which is the lattice of this wiki with $d=1$ written as an interval by the identification of the lattice article. The two models have the same block sums, which is all the recursion uses, and they differ in how the interaction is distributed inside a block: in the hierarchical model every pair of sites in the two halves of an interval carries the same coupling, while on the chain a pair is weighted by its separation, so the interaction across the midpoint of a long interval is strong near the midpoint and weak at the ends.

    Decimation on a tree. A regular tree with branching $m$ and a coupling constant on every edge has the same property as the hierarchical lattice for the spin model: the interaction between a vertex and the rest is through its neighbours, and the recursion closes on the distribution of the cavity field. The tree is not ultrametric and its blocks are not self-similar in the same sense, but the closure mechanism is the same one — a boundary of size one.

    Non-example: the triangular lattice. Any Euclidean lattice has the boundary phenomenon of the proposition above. The $L$-block partition of the triangular lattice has blocks whose boundary length grows like the side, and the effective interaction between neighbouring blocks depends on the orientation of their shared edge.

    Variants and generalisations

    Wavelet and covariance-based coarse grainings. A coarse graining defined by a projection of the Gaussian free field onto a subspace of slowly varying functions has an effective covariance that can be computed exactly, and for a suitable choice of the projection the interaction remains quadratic. What such a scheme gains in exactness it loses in locality: the coarse grained field has long-range couplings and the recursion is on a covariance rather than on a finite set of couplings.

    Hierarchical approximations of the Euclidean covariance. Replacing the Euclidean covariance by its average over blocks, in the manner of Gawedzki and Kupiainen, produces a model whose recursion is finite-dimensional and whose exponents differ from the Euclidean ones by a computable amount; the expansion in the difference is the content of their method.

    Exactly renormalisable models. The hierarchical lattice is not the only graph on which decimation closes; any graph with a self-similar block structure whose cross-block interaction is a function of block sums will do, and the ultrametric lattice is the unique transitive example with finite blocks up to the identifications of the lattice article.

    History and attribution

    Dyson introduced the hierarchical couplings to prove a phase transition in one dimension [@dyson-1969]; Bleher and Sinai turned the resulting recursion into the object of study, and it is their work that made the hierarchical model the standard testing ground for the renormalisation group [@bleher-sinai-1973]. Gawedzki and Kupiainen developed the rigorous block-spin expansion that uses the hierarchical model as its leading term [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1980] and analysed the nontrivial fixed point as the model problem for the Euclidean one [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983]. The failure of the Gibbs property under block-spin transformations is the subject of van Enter, Fernandez and Sokal [@van-enter-fernandez-sokal-1993], and the renormalisation-group trajectory for critical $(\Phi^4)_3$ is constructed by Brydges, Mitter and Scoppola [@brydges-mitter-scoppola-2003], with a systematic account in Bauerschmidt, Brydges and Slade [@bauerschmidt-brydges-slade-2019].
