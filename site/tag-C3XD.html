    Power laws with corrections from the subleading spectrum

    theorem · C3XD · in Corrections to scaling

    Assume that for $\beta$ near $\beta_c$ the rescaled orbit admits the expansion

    $$\nu_n=\nu^*+a\,\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert\,\lambda_1^{\,n}\rho_1+\sum_{j\geq2}b_j\lambda_j^{\,n}\rho_j+O\bigl(\rho^{2n}\bigr),$$

    with $\lvert\lambda_j\rvert\leq\rho<1$ and coefficients $a,b_j$ independent of $n$ and $\beta$. Then, with $\kappa$ the growth exponent of the dictionary and $\gamma=\kappa\nu$,

    $$\chi_n(\beta)=L^{\kappa n}\Bigl(m_2(\nu^*)+a\,\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert\,\lambda_1^{\,n}m_2(\rho_1)+\sum_{j\geq2}b_j\lambda_j^{\,n}m_2(\rho_j)+O(\rho^{2n})\Bigr),$$

    and taking $n$ of order the crossover scale, $L^{n}\asymp\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{-\nu}$, gives

    $$\chi(\beta)=A\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{-\gamma}\Bigl(1+\sum_{j\geq2}C_j\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{\,\nu\omega(\lambda_j)}+O\bigl(\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{2\nu\omega_2}\bigr)\Bigr),$$

    with $A=m_2(\nu^*)$ and constants $C_j$ that depend on the direction but not on $\beta$. The two-point function carries the same relative corrections at every pair of vertices, and the singular free energy carries them with its own amplitude.

    Proof

    The block susceptibility is $\chi_n=L^{-dn}\mathbb E[S_n^2]$ by the block identity, and $\mathbb E[S_n^2]=L^{2\theta_0 n}m_2(\nu_n)$ with $2\theta_0-d=\kappa$. Substituting the expansion gives the second display, since $m_2$ is linear and the expansion is finite up to the stated error.

    For the third display, let $\xi=\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{-\nu}$ and choose $n$ with $L^n=\xi$, so that $\lambda_1^{\,n}=\xi^{\log\lambda_1/\log L}=\xi^{1/\nu}$ and $\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert\lambda_1^{\,n}=\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{1-\nu\cdot(1/\nu)}=1$: the relevant term is of the same size as the fixed-point term, which is what makes $n$ the crossover scale. At that scale the $j$-th correction has relative size $\lvert\lambda_j\rvert^{n}=\xi^{-\omega(\lambda_j)}=\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{\nu\omega(\lambda_j)}$, and the error is of the order of the square of the largest correction. Dividing by the leading behaviour $\xi^{\kappa}=\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{-\kappa\nu}=\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{-\gamma}$ gives the display.

    The statement for the two-point function is the same computation applied to $\tau$, whose block values are the off-diagonal entries of the covariance of the child spins; each is linear in the expansion, so the relative correction is the same at every pair. For the free energy the increment at scale $n$ is a smooth function of the orbit, so its expansion is the linear one composed with the derivative of the increment, which changes only the constants.

    The hypothesis is the whole content of the statement, and it is what the fixed-point theory supplies: an expansion of the orbit in the eigenmeasures of the linearisation, with coefficients that vary smoothly with the temperature. The theorem then says that everything the model can do to a power law, in this class, is a sum of terms $\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{\nu\omega_j}$.

    ## Consequences and applications {#section-consequences}

    Effective exponents. Differentiating the logarithm of the susceptibility gives

    $$\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\beta)=-\frac{d\log\chi}{d\log\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert}=\gamma-\sum_{j\geq2}C_j\,\nu\omega(\lambda_j)\,\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{\nu\omega(\lambda_j)}+\cdots,$$

    so a numerical estimate of $\gamma$ drifts towards its limit at a rate set by the smallest $\omega$. This is the reason exponents extracted from finite ranges of temperature are biased in a direction fixed by the sign of the corrections.

    The oscillatory case. If $\lambda_2,\bar\lambda_2$ are a complex pair with argument $\pm\varphi$, the correction term of the theorem reads $C\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{\nu\omega}\cos\bigl(\nu\varphi\log\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert+\vartheta\bigr)$: a power times a cosine of the logarithm, which looks like a drifting oscillation when plotted against $\log\xi$. The analysis of the linearisation at the nontrivial fixed point, where such pairs occur, is that of Gawedzki and Kupiainen [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983].

    The marginal limit. As $\lambda_2\uparrow1$ the correction exponent $\omega\downarrow0$ and the correction becomes $\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{o(1)}$, that is, a power of a logarithm. This is exactly what happens at the marginal kernel exponent, where the two fixed points merge, and it is the origin of the logarithmic corrections recorded there in the fixed-point article.

    Reading data. Because the corrections are pure powers of $\xi$ with exponents determined by the spectrum, a fit that includes one correction term and excludes the next is well posed only if $\omega_2$ is not close to an integer multiple of $\omega$; otherwise the two terms are hard to separate, which is the practical form of the "confluent corrections" problem.

    ## Examples and non-examples {#section-examples}

    The Gaussian fixed point. There the only relevant direction is the variance, whose eigenvalue is $L^{\alpha}$, and the subleading directions are the non-Gaussian perturbations; the quartic one is the first, and its eigenvalue is the quantity computed in the epsilon expansion around the marginal exponent [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983]. At the Gaussian fixed point itself the corrections vanish identically for a Gaussian initial law, which is why the Gaussian model has exact power laws.

    Corrections in the Euclidean theory. On $\mathbb Z^d$ the same spectral picture holds near the fixed point, but the decimation does not close, so the corrections are not pure powers of $\xi$: they are powers times logarithms arising from the infinitely many irrelevant directions generated at each step. The hierarchical model is the case in which that sum collapses to one geometric series per eigenvalue.

    Non-example: a first-order transition. At a first-order transition there is no fixed point governing the transition, the correlation length stays finite, and no expansion of the above form exists; the power-law apparatus does not apply, and the observed behaviour is a discontinuity.

    Non-example: the marginal exponent. At $\alpha=d/2$ the assumption $\lambda_2$ bounded away from one fails, and the theorem as stated gives no rate; the logarithmic corrections have to be derived separately, and they are not of the form $\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{c}$ for a constant $c>0$.

    ## Variants and generalisations {#section-variants}

    Corrections to the correlation length. The same argument applies to any quantity that is a smooth function of the orbit, with the constants changed: the correlation length itself satisfies $\xi=\xi_0\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{-\nu}(1+O(\lvert\beta-\beta_c\rvert^{\nu\omega}))$, which is the form in which the two-point function's correction is usually written.

    Several irrelevant directions. With a pair of eigenvalues of comparable modulus the correction is a sum, and the exponents $\nu\omega_j$ need not be commensurable; a two-term fit is then the smallest well-posed model.

    Scaling fields. The coefficient of the relevant eigenmeasure is the scaling field $t(\beta)$ of the renormalisation group, and the corrections are the higher scaling fields; the statement of the theorem is that in the hierarchical model the scaling fields are analytic in $\beta$ and their images under the flow are powers of $\xi$. This is the form in which the corrections enter the Euclidean analysis as well [@aizenman-fernandez-1988].

    ## History and attribution {#section-history}

    The computation of the indices from the linearisation, and the presence of further eigenvalues controlling the corrections, is in Bleher and Sinai's second paper on the hierarchical model [@bleher-sinai-1975]. The complex pairs at the nontrivial fixed point, and the oscillatory corrections they produce, are analysed by Gawedzki and Kupiainen, who also identify the marginal limit at which the corrections become logarithmic [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1983]; the scaling limit and the flow of the relevant direction around the fixed point are the subject of their second paper [@gawedzki-kupiainen-1984]. The classical long-range values, for which the corrections vanish in the Gaussian case, are those of the Gaussian fixed point.
