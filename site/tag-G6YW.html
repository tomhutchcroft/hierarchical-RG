    Exponents in terms of the two renormalisation-group numbers

    theorem · G6YW · in Critical exponents from the renormalisation group

    Suppose that $\chi_n(\beta_c)\asymp L^{\kappa n}$ and that, uniformly in $n$ and $\beta$,

    $$\chi_n(\beta)\asymp\min\bigl\{L^{\kappa n},\ \xi(\beta)^{\kappa}\bigr\},\qquad \xi(\beta)\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{-\nu}.$$

    Then $\chi(\beta)\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{-\kappa\nu}$, that is $\gamma=\kappa\nu$. If in addition $F_{\mathrm{sing}}\asymp\xi^{-d}$ then $2-\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}=d\nu$, and if in addition $M\asymp\xi^{-(d-\kappa)/2}$ then $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}=\tfrac{\nu}{2}(d-\kappa)$; under these two, together with the two relations just displayed,

    $$\delta=\frac{d+\kappa}{d-\kappa},\qquad \alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}=2-d\nu,\qquad \gamma=\nu(2-\eta),\qquad \gamma=\beta_{\mathrm{o}}(\delta-1).$$
    Proof of the dictionary

    For $\gamma$: the supremum over $n$ of $\min\{L^{\kappa n},\xi^\kappa\}$ is $\xi^\kappa$ up to constants, and $\xi\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{-\nu}$ gives $\chi\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{-\kappa\nu}$.

    For $\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}$: $F_{\mathrm{sing}}\asymp\xi^{-d}\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{d\nu}$, and comparing with $F_{\mathrm{sing}}\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{2-\alpha_{\mathrm{hy}}}$ gives the identity.

    For $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}$: $M\asymp\xi^{-(d-\kappa)/2}\asymp\lvert\beta_c-\beta\rvert^{\nu(d-\kappa)/2}$, and comparing with the definition gives $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}=\tfrac{\nu}{2}(d-\kappa)$, which is the same as $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}=\tfrac{\nu}{2}(d-2+\eta)$ because $\kappa=2-\eta$.

    For $\delta$: Widom's relation $\gamma=\beta_{\mathrm{o}}(\delta-1)$ is the definition of $\delta$ once the other two exponents are known, and substituting $\gamma=\kappa\nu$ and $\beta_{\mathrm{o}}=\tfrac{\nu}{2}(d-\kappa)$ gives $\kappa\nu=\tfrac{\nu}{2}(d-\kappa)(\delta-1)$, hence $\delta-1=\frac{2\kappa}{d-\kappa}$ and $\delta=\frac{d+\kappa}{d-\kappa}$. The last two displays restate the first two relations with $\kappa=2-\eta$.

    Two things are called mean field in this subject and they are not the same, which is worth fixing before the dictionary is used.

    Mean field as the relevant Green's function. The kernel $J(x)\asymp\lVert x\rVert^{-d-\alpha}$ is the kernel of a fractional Laplacian of order $\alpha$, whose Green's function is $\lVert x\rVert^{-(d-\alpha)}$. A model is mean field in this sense when its critical two-point function is of that order, $\tau_{\beta_c}(0,y)\asymp\lVert y\rVert^{-(d-\alpha)}$: the correlation is the one the kernel dictates, with no anomalous dimension beyond the one built into the kernel. In the notation above that statement is exactly $\eta=2-\alpha$, and it holds here for every $\alpha$, because the growth exponent is $\alpha$ at every fixed point. The free Gaussian field with this kernel already has it — its propagator is the Green's function — so $\eta=2-\alpha\neq0$ is not a failure of mean-field behaviour but its definition in the long-range setting.

    Mean field as the classical exponents. A model is mean field in the second sense when its fixed point is the Gaussian one, so that $\nu=1/\alpha$ and everything the dictionary derives from $\nu$ takes the classical values. That holds only for $\alpha<d/2$, and what changes at $\alpha=d/2$ is $\nu$ and the corrections to scaling, not $\eta$.

    The relation between the two uses is the short-range case. At $\alpha=2$, which is the borderline of summability of the second moment and the case a short-range interaction approximates, $\eta=2-\alpha=0$ and the Green's function is the ordinary $\lVert y\rVert^{-(d-2)}$; so "mean field implies $\eta=0$" is the $\alpha=2$ instance of "mean field means the Green's function", and a reader who has the short-range statement in mind should read $\eta=2-\alpha$ as the same statement rather than as its negation. What is proved pointwise differs by model: for percolation the order $\lVert y\rVert^{-(d-\alpha)}$ is a theorem for every $\alpha$ [@hutchcroft-hierarchical-2021], while for Ising spins the summed statement behind it — $\kappa=\alpha$ with the normalised second moment bounded two-sidedly — is what the amplitude section proves, and the pointwise order is the item it leaves open.

    Which of these is a theorem of the model and which is an extra hypothesis is the point of the small print. The first display of the dictionary is a theorem of percolation in the hierarchical model below the triangle threshold, where the block susceptibility is known to grow like $L^{\kappa n}$ and the truncated form is a consequence of the finite correlation length; hyperscaling, the identity $F_{\mathrm{sing}}\asymp\xi^{-d}$, is the hypothesis that fails in the classical regime. The relations $\gamma=\kappa\nu$ and the identification $\kappa=2-\eta$ are renormalisation-group statements and hold whenever the fixed point exists with a finite variance; $\delta=\frac{d+\kappa}{d-\kappa}$ follows only when hyperscaling does.

    Everything in the dictionary is conditional, and it is worth being precise about on what. It takes a fixed point with a simple leading eigenvalue and a gap in the rest of the spectrum, it takes the growth exponent $\kappa$ from the autonomy of the recursion rather than from the model, and it takes hyperscaling where it uses it. The Gaussian case supplies all of this explicitly, which is why the solved article can compute the classical exponents without further hypotheses. For the Ising model nothing is supplied here: the article that follows is where the fixed point and its spectrum are proved to exist, and its statement is what turns the dictionary from a machine with two inputs into the exponents of a particular model. A reader who wants the exponents should read this section as that machine, and the next article as the place where the inputs are produced.
